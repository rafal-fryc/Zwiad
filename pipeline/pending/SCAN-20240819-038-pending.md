# NYC Deploys Drones for Flash Flood Warnings in Vulnerable Neighborhoods: Emergency Management, Privacy Oversight, and the POST Act

**Jurisdiction:** New York | **Category:** privacy | **Date:** 2024-08-19

## Executive Summary [MEDIUM confidence]

In early August 2024, the New York City Office of Emergency Management (NYCEM) deployed at least five drone teams, in coordination with the NYPD, to flood-prone neighborhoods across Queens, the Bronx, Brooklyn, and Staten Island. The drones — equipped with loudspeakers — broadcast audio warnings in multiple languages urging residents in basement and ground-floor apartments to prepare for flash flooding. The deployment was a direct response to the September 2021 Hurricane Ida disaster, in which eleven New Yorkers drowned in basement apartments. While NYCEM framed the operation as a public-safety communications tool, the deployment occurred within a broader and rapidly escalating pattern of municipal drone use that has drawn substantial criticism from civil liberties groups over inadequate oversight. Two distinct legal regimes apply: the NYC POST Act governs NYPD surveillance technology disclosure; and the NYPD's Impact and Use Policy (IUP) for UAS, published in September 2023 under pressure from that law, sets rules for drone deployment. Both frameworks have been found by independent oversight bodies to be materially deficient.

## Background [HIGH confidence]

### Hurricane Ida and the Basement Apartment Crisis

On September 1–2, 2021, the remnants of Hurricane Ida produced historic rainfall in New York City. Eleven people died, all of them in basement or cellar apartments that flooded rapidly — dwellings that are legal in New York City under specific conditions but that lack above-grade exits and are not captured by standard door-to-door canvassing during weather emergencies ([CNN, August 2024](https://edition.cnn.com/2024/08/07/us/new-york-drones-severe-weather/index.html); [Dronelife, November 2024](https://dronelife.com/2024/11/08/rising-above-the-storm-how-drones-are-revolutionizing-nycs-disaster-response-12-years-after-hurricane-sandy/)). After Ida, the New York City Public Advocate introduced legislation to improve flash-flood emergency response to basement-apartment residents ([NYC Public Advocate press release, 2021](https://advocate.nyc.gov/press/after-hurricane-idas-devastating-flooding-public-advocate-introduces-bill-to-improve-flash-flood-emergency-response/)).

### NYC Drone Policy Chronology

Prior to 2023, flying a drone commercially or governmentally within New York City was effectively prohibited absent FAA waivers. In July 2023, Mayor Eric Adams unveiled new guidelines establishing a permitting process under [38 RCNY § 24](https://rules.cityofnewyork.us/rule/applications-to-launch-or-land-an-unmanned-aircraft-including-a-drone/), administered by NYPD, allowing take-off and landing within city limits with 30-day advance applications ([NYC Mayor's Office, July 2023](https://www.nyc.gov/mayors-office/news/2023/07/mayor-adams-new-guidelines-allow-responsible-drone-usage-new-york-city)). The same year, NYPD published its first formal Impact and Use Policy (IUP) for Unmanned Aircraft Systems under the NYC POST Act.

The POST Act (New York City Local Law 65 of 2020, [Int 0487-2018](https://legistar.council.nyc.gov/LegislationDetail.aspx?ID=3343878&GUID=996ABB2A-9F4C-4A32-B081-D6F24AB954A0)) requires NYPD to publish, maintain, and update impact-and-use policies for each surveillance technology it deploys, covering capabilities, use limitations, data retention, and public access rules. The Act further mandates annual oversight and public comment periods for any new or substantially modified surveillance technology ([NYC NYPD POST Act page](https://www.nyc.gov/site/nypd/about/about-nypd/policy/post-act.page)).

### 2023 Labor Day Controversy

In September 2023, NYPD announced it would deploy drones to monitor large private gatherings — specifically backyard parties — over Labor Day weekend in response to noise complaints. Privacy and civil-liberties advocates immediately raised POST Act compliance concerns: the city had not published a 90-day advance impact-and-use policy as required for new surveillance uses. The Surveillance Technology Oversight Project (STOP) described the deployment as warrantless aerial surveillance of private residences ([CNN, September 2023](https://www.cnn.com/2023/09/02/us/nypd-drone-surveillance-labor-day/index.html); [NBC News, 2023](https://www.nbcnewyork.com/news/local/nypd-using-drones-to-monitor-nyc-backyard-labor-day-parties-spurring-privacy-concerns/4640678/)). The episode accelerated legislative and advocacy attention on drone oversight.

In July 2024, Mayor Adams announced a new joint Drone Operations Committee coordinating NYPD, FDNY, OEM, Parks and Recreation, and the Department of Environmental Protection ([Dronelife, November 2024](https://dronelife.com/2024/11/08/rising-above-the-storm-how-drones-are-revolutionizing-nycs-disaster-response-12-years-after-hurricane-sandy/)).

## Detailed Analysis [MEDIUM confidence]

### The August 2024 OEM Deployment

The August 2024 deployment targeted historically flood-prone neighborhoods identified following Ida: Howard Beach, Rockaways, Ozone Park, Jamaica, and Hollis in Queens; areas near the Bronx River and Throggs Neck in the Bronx; Coney Island in Brooklyn; and New Dorp and Fox Beach on Staten Island ([DroneDJ, August 2024](https://dronedj.com/2024/08/08/nyc-drone-flood-warning/); [Phys.org, August 2024](https://phys.org/news/2024-08-yorkers-skies-impending-danger-storms.html)). NYCEM Commissioner Zachary Iscol stated that the drone messages were issued in multiple languages to reflect neighborhood demographics ([CNN, August 2024](https://edition.cnn.com/2024/08/07/us/new-york-drones-severe-weather/index.html)).

A notable operational failure arose from the multilingual requirement: the Spanish-language audio broadcast was generated by machine translation and was widely mocked by residents as grammatically incoherent, prompting a public apology from city officials ([ABC News, August 2024](https://abcnews.go.com/US/wireStory/drones-warned-new-york-city-residents-storm-flooding-112650186); [Brooklyn Eagle, August 2024](https://brooklyneagle.com/articles/2024/08/07/drones-warned-new-york-city-residents-about-storm-flooding-the-spanish-translation-was-no-bueno/)).

The drones used loudspeakers for outward audio broadcasting and were not described in contemporaneous reporting as carrying video recording equipment during the OEM emergency-warning mission. However, the same drone platform types used by NYPD are capable of high-resolution video streaming and, according to independent oversight reporting, were not fully disclosed as to their technical capabilities in the NYPD IUP ([DOI Report, December 2024](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/doi/reports/pdf/2024/49PostActRelease.Rpt.12.18.2024.pdf)).

### NYPD POST Act Compliance: Documented Deficiencies

In December 2024, the NYC Department of Investigation (DOI/OIG) released a report finding that the NYPD's September 2023 drone IUP materially failed to comply with the POST Act. The report identified that: (1) the IUP did not fully disclose device capabilities including mapping technologies, communication interception features, and glass-breaking attachments; (2) the IUP did not adequately outline health and safety risks or property damage risks; and (3) the NYPD had been flying what it internally labeled the "Drone as First Responder" (DFR) program — which generated 1,715 flights through Q3 2024 — before formally announcing the program or publishing an IUP for it ([DOI Report, December 2024](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/doi/reports/pdf/2024/49PostActRelease.Rpt.12.18.2024.pdf); [STOP press release, December 2024](https://www.stopspying.org/latest-news/2024/12/18/stop-welcomes-oig-report-on-nypd-violations-of-surveillance-transparency-law-in-drone-usage)).

In November 2024, Mayor Adams and Interim Police Commissioner Donlon formally announced the DFR program, rolling it out in four precincts and Central Park ([NYC Mayor's Office, November 2024](https://www.nyc.gov/mayors-office/news/2024/11/mayor-adams-interim-police-commissioner-donlon-drone-first-responder-program-to)).

### Drone Fleet Growth and NYCLU Findings

According to the NYCLU's 2024 statewide drone survey, New York State government entities held 876 active drone registrations across 127 agencies — up 65% from 530 in 2022. Law enforcement agencies accounted for 508 of those registrations. NYPD alone grew from 19 to 99 registrations, a 421% increase ([NYCLU, "Prying Eyes" report, 2024](https://www.nyclu.org/report/prying-eyes-government-drone-data-across-new-york-state)). A separate StateScoop analysis reported NYPD drone flights increased 3,200% since 2022 ([StateScoop, 2025](https://statescoop.com/report-nypd-drone-flights-increase-with-little-public-oversight/)). The NYCLU report specifically flagged that drones can be equipped with biometric surveillance capabilities including facial recognition, gait recognition, and emotion detection — capabilities not disclosed in the NYPD's existing IUP ([NYCLU press release](https://www.nyclu.org/press-release/new-nyclu-data-ny-law-enforcement-drone-arsenal-rapidly-expanding-with-no-oversight)).

### Legal Framework: NYPD Impact and Use Policy

The NYPD's current drone IUP (updated February 4, 2026) prohibits: deployment in areas where there is a reasonable expectation of privacy absent a search warrant; use for "warrantless surveillance"; use for traffic enforcement or vehicle immobilization; and surveillance of First Amendment-protected activity ([NYPD UAS Impact and Use Policy, Feb. 2026](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/nypd/downloads/pdf/public_information/post-final/Unmanned-Aircraft-Systems-NYPD-Impact-and-Use-Policy-2.4.26-FINAL.pdf)). The policy permits emergency deployments authorized by a commanding officer or executive officer, which covers the OEM weather-warning coordination scenario. Critically, however, the OIG's December 2024 report found the IUP does not fully reflect the technological capabilities of the deployed hardware.

### State Legislation: The Protect Our Privacy Act

New York State has not yet enacted comprehensive law-enforcement drone privacy legislation. The "Protect Our Privacy Act" has been introduced in multiple legislative sessions under different bill numbers: S675/A3311 (2021); S4162/A5251 (2023-24); and S1096/A972 (2025-26, currently in committee) ([NY Senate S1096, 2025](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/bills/2025/S1096); [NY Assembly A972, 2025](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/bills/2025/A972)). The bill would: prohibit drone surveillance of protests and First Amendment events without a warrant; ban facial recognition technology on drones; require search warrants for law-enforcement drone investigations; and set data retention and deletion rules. As of April 2025, the 2021-session bill (S675) died in the Senate Codes Committee; the 2025 version remains in the Governmental Operations Committee ([STOP, police drone ban page](https://www.stopspying.org/new-york-state-slate/2023/11/28/police-drone-ban)).

In April 2025, the New York City Council passed an expanded POST Act legislative package that tightened language to close loopholes the NYPD had exploited to avoid transparency disclosures and added additional reporting requirements ([NYC Council press release, April 2025](https://council.nyc.gov/amanda-farias/2025/04/15/city-council-passes-expanded-post-act-legislative-package-to-strengthen-transparency-and-oversight-of-nypd-surveillance-technology/)).

## Impact Assessment [MEDIUM confidence]

### Emergency Management vs. Surveillance: The Blurring Line

The August 2024 OEM deployment illustrates a pattern: the same drone infrastructure developed for public-safety purposes is operationally shared across agencies (NYPD, FDNY, OEM) under the 2024 joint Drone Operations Committee framework. A drone deployed for weather warnings in the morning can, absent policy constraints, be equipped with cameras and re-tasked for patrol in the afternoon. The DOI's finding that NYPD operated an undisclosed DFR program for months before formally announcing it — while the drones counted as part of the growing fleet — demonstrates how quickly operational expansion outpaces compliance frameworks.

### Vulnerable Communities: The Equity Dimension

The neighborhoods targeted by the OEM warning deployment — Howard Beach, Rockaways, Ozone Park, Bronx River corridor, Coney Island — are disproportionately low-income and comprise significant immigrant populations. Residents who are undocumented or who have historically negative experiences with law enforcement may experience audio broadcasts from unmarked drone platforms differently than the emergency-management intent suggests. The Spanish-language translation failure is an operational symptom of a deeper design problem: the program was developed without adequate community input from the populations it targets.

### Compliance Obligations for Operators

Organizations that fly drones in New York City for any purpose — emergency management, inspections, media — must comply with: (1) FAA Part 107 remote pilot certification; (2) the NYPD UAS take-off/landing permit process under 38 RCNY § 24, filed 30 days in advance; and (3) any applicable IUP constraints if the operator is a city agency. Private entities face no current POST Act obligations, but the April 2025 City Council expansion of the POST Act adds scrutiny to city contractors and technology vendors ([NYC NYPD permit page](https://www.nyc.gov/site/nypd/services/law-enforcement/permits-uas-permits.page)).

### FAA Regulatory Baseline

Federal drone law under FAA Part 107 (14 C.F.R. Part 107) governs all civil UAS operations nationally. Local restrictions may supplement but not conflict with FAA airspace management. The NYC permit process was structured to operate alongside — not supersede — FAA authorization requirements. For emergency government operations, FAA Certificate of Waiver or Authorization (COA) or Public Aircraft Operations status typically applies, providing a faster authorization pathway.

## Action Items

- Monitor New York State legislative session for advancement of S1096/A972 (Protect Our Privacy Act) — passage would create the first statutory warrant requirement for law-enforcement drone surveillance in New York.
- Track NYPD publication of a revised UAS IUP that addresses the December 2024 DOI findings; the existing IUP (updated February 4, 2026) does not yet fully disclose hardware capabilities per the DOI report.
- Organizations deploying drones in NYC should verify NYPD take-off/landing permit compliance under 38 RCNY § 24 and maintain FAA Part 107 remote pilot certification records.
- Privacy counsel advising government clients on emergency management programs should review the OEM/NYPD Drone Operations Committee structure and assess whether inter-agency drone sharing triggers additional POST Act disclosure obligations for non-NYPD agency use.
- Track follow-on City Council oversight hearings: the February 19, 2025 Committee on Public Safety hearing on the DOI drone program report signaled continued Council interest in mandatory IUP improvements.
