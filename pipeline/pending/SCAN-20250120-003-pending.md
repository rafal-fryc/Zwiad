# Second Trump Administration: Implications for UK Tech Sector Privacy and Data Flows

**Jurisdiction:** Federal (US), United Kingdom (cross-jurisdictional) | **Category:** Privacy | **Date:** 2025-01-20

## Executive Summary [MEDIUM confidence]

The inauguration of Donald Trump as the 47th US President on January 20, 2025 introduced significant uncertainty for UK technology companies that rely on transatlantic data flows and regulatory interoperability. On his first day in office, Trump revoked Biden's landmark AI executive order (EO 14110), signaling a sharp deregulatory shift in US tech policy. Within days, the administration dismissed the Democratic members of the Privacy and Civil Liberties Oversight Board (PCLOB), the independent body whose continued operation underpinned both the EU-US Data Privacy Framework and the UK-US Data Bridge. These actions raised immediate questions about the legal durability of transatlantic data transfer mechanisms for UK organizations and compelled compliance officers to reassess contingency strategies for data flows to US processors and controllers. By mid-2025 the picture had partially stabilized, but the episode exposed a structural dependency in UK data transfer arrangements that organizations should plan around.

## Background [MEDIUM confidence]

### The UK-US Data Bridge

The [UK-US Data Bridge](https://www.gdpradvisor.co.uk/uk-us-data-bridge) entered into force on October 12, 2023. It is an adequacy mechanism under UK GDPR that allows UK organizations to transfer personal data to certified US businesses without requiring supplementary transfer impact assessments or standard contractual clauses. It mirrors the EU-US Data Privacy Framework (DPF) but operates as a separate UK instrument, grounded in the UK Secretary of State's adequacy assessment.

The UK bridge and the DPF share the same underlying US legal architecture: Executive Order 14086 "Enhancing Safeguards for United States Signals Intelligence Activities," signed by Biden in October 2022, which created a Data Protection Review Court and imposed binding limits on US signals intelligence collection from EEA and UK residents.

The [Privacy and Civil Liberties Oversight Board (PCLOB)](https://lawfaremedia.org/article/trump-s-sacking-of-pclob-members-threatens-data-privacy) is an independent executive branch agency that supervises US compliance with surveillance restrictions. The European Commission's 2023 adequacy decision for the DPF referenced PCLOB 31 times as a critical oversight mechanism. The board's independence was essential to the "essentially equivalent" standard required by EU and UK data protection law. Similarly, the [UK Information Commissioner's Office adequacy assessment](https://ico.org.uk/about-the-ico/what-we-do/information-commissioners-opinions/information-commissioners-opinions-on-adequacy/the-uk-government-s-assessment-of-adequacy-for-the-uk-extension-to-the-eu-us-data-privacy-framework/) for the UK Extension to the DPF treated PCLOB's continued independence as a material element.

### Biden's AI Executive Order

On October 30, 2023, President Biden issued [Executive Order 14110](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Executive_Order_14110) — "Safe, Secure, and Trustworthy Development and Use of Artificial Intelligence" — establishing a comprehensive federal AI governance framework requiring safety testing, risk disclosures, and interagency coordination. UK tech companies operating in or accessing the US market had begun aligning product development with this framework's requirements in anticipation of downstream regulatory interoperability.

## Detailed Analysis [MEDIUM confidence]

### Revocation of Biden's AI Executive Order

On January 20, 2025 — inauguration day — President Trump [revoked EO 14110](https://www.wiley.law/alert-President-Trump-Revokes-Biden-Administrations-AI-EO-What-To-Know) and replaced it on January 23, 2025, with Executive Order 14179: "Removing Barriers to American Leadership in Artificial Intelligence." The new order explicitly prioritized US AI dominance through deregulation and the removal of constraints that the administration characterized as inhibiting innovation. No equivalent federal AI safety testing requirements remain in effect at the federal level in the US.

For UK tech companies, this created a divergence problem. The UK had been tracking loosely with the Biden AI governance approach as part of its "pro-innovation" framing. With the US deregulating sharply, UK policymakers faced pressure — which subsequently materialized — to delay or soften the UK's own planned AI regulation Bill to avoid being seen as imposing burdens that competing US firms do not bear. [Analysis from Queen Mary University's Mile End Institute](https://www.qmul.ac.uk/mei/news-and-opinion/items/the-uk-in-the-ai-regulation-debate-in-hoc-with-trumps-america-or-going-their-own-way.html) documented this dynamic explicitly, noting that the UK government's pivot toward closer AI policy alignment with Washington was driven partly by the need to avoid tariff exposure and maintain favorable trade relations.

### PCLOB Dismissals and Data Transfer Risk

On January 27, 2025, the Trump administration dismissed all three Democratic members of the PCLOB, including the chair, leaving the board without a statutory quorum of three members and effectively paralyzing it. [The Center for Democracy and Technology](https://cdt.org/insights/what-the-pclob-firings-mean-for-the-eu-us-data-privacy-framework/) assessed that the firings created immediate legal vulnerability for the DPF because the European Commission's adequacy decision had expressly relied on PCLOB's independence as a safeguard.

The [risk for the UK-US Data Bridge](https://www.digitalsamba.com/blog/trump-presidency-threatens-the-stability-of-the-eu-us-data-transfer-agreement) was structurally identical: both mechanisms depend on EO 14086 remaining operable and on effective independent oversight of US surveillance practices. Without a functional PCLOB, there is no mechanism for the independent oversight that UK and EU supervisory authorities relied upon.

The [European Parliament](https://www.europarl.europa.eu/doceo/document/E-10-2025-000540_EN.html) responded formally: MEPs filed a parliamentary question in early February 2025 questioning whether the European Commission would suspend the DPF adequacy decision until PCLOB was reconstituted as an independent body. The Commission clarified that EO 14086 itself remained in force and that US courts had reinstated dismissed PCLOB members, temporarily stabilizing the situation.

[CMS Law analysis](https://cms-lawnow.com/en/ealerts/2025/01/is-the-eu-u.s.-data-privacy-framework-in-danger) published in January 2025 outlined four scenarios ranging from PCLOB reinstatement (which occurred in partial form following court action) to a full "Schrems III" CJEU invalidation that would collapse the DPF and, given the bridge's shared legal foundation, likely also undermine the UK-US Data Bridge.

### UK Data (Use and Access) Act: A Partial Buffer

Enacted on June 19, 2025, the [UK Data (Use and Access) Act 2025](https://bills.parliament.uk/bills/3825) (DUAA) introduced a modified adequacy test for international data transfers. The former "essentially equivalent" standard — which mirrored the EU's — was replaced with a "not materially lower" test. This lower threshold was specifically designed to give the UK legislative room to maintain its data bridge with the US even in a scenario where EU courts invalidate the DPF.

[Shoosmiths LLP analysis](https://www.shoosmiths.com/insights/articles/can-the-new-uk-data-laws-flip-the-script-on-the-eu) noted that this provision was "tailor-made for the US" and gives the UK "political cards to play" in the event of a Schrems III ruling. However, the same analysis flagged the reciprocal risk: if the UK strikes too divergent a path from EU data protection standards, the European Commission could revoke or decline to renew the UK's own adequacy status on the grounds that the UK had become a "data laundering hub."

On July 22, 2025, the Commission confirmed it had commenced renewal of UK adequacy decisions, indicating the path to a six-year extension looked clear despite the DUAA's changed standards — a more positive resolution than had appeared likely in January.

### Tariffs, Trade, and the UK-US Tech Relationship

Separate from privacy, the Trump administration's April 2025 tariff regime — imposing a 10% baseline tariff on most UK goods — created immediate exposure for UK tech hardware manufacturers and companies with US supply chain dependencies. A subsequent [UK-US economic deal](https://time.com/7318214/trump-starmer-us-uk-tech-prosperity-deal-signed/) signed on September 18, 2025, and associated with the bilateral Tech Prosperity Deal, softened the impact in specific sectors (auto tariffs reduced, aerospace duties eliminated) and formalized cooperation on quantum computing, 6G, and civil nuclear technology.

The deal also reflected the UK's strategic alignment with US AI deregulation. US companies including Microsoft, Nvidia, and OpenAI committed approximately $42 billion (£31 billion) in capital investment to UK digital infrastructure, which analysts assessed as partly contingent on the UK's regulatory posture remaining favorable to US tech industry interests.

## Impact Assessment [MEDIUM confidence]

### Affected Entities

- **UK organizations transferring personal data to US processors or controllers** under the UK-US Data Bridge — the PCLOB dismissals created immediate legal uncertainty about whether the bridge's legal basis remained sound. Until the partial PCLOB reinstatement in early 2025, organizations relying solely on the bridge bore residual risk.
- **UK AI developers and tech companies with US market exposure** — the revocation of EO 14110 removed a harmonization anchor that had provided some predictability for product compliance across both markets.
- **UK companies subject to UK GDPR and simultaneously operating AI systems trained or deployed in the US** — the regulatory divergence between US deregulation and UK/EU AI governance frameworks increased compliance complexity.
- **UK fintech, healthtech, and SaaS companies** relying on US cloud infrastructure and data processing services — the bridge risk was most acute for sectors with high-volume personal data flows.

### Compliance Considerations

For the period January–mid-2025, organizations with material reliance on the UK-US Data Bridge were advised to:
1. Maintain standard contractual clauses (SCCs) or international data transfer agreements (IDTAs) as backup transfer mechanisms even where the bridge was the primary route, to enable rapid fallback if the bridge's legal validity was challenged.
2. Conduct or refresh transfer impact assessments (TIAs) documenting the legal analysis of EO 14086's continued operation and PCLOB's status.
3. Avoid structural reliance on a single transfer mechanism for high-volume or sensitive category data flows.

For AI governance, UK companies began tracking the divergence between UK and US AI regulatory trajectories, particularly given the UK government's delayed AI regulation Bill and the uncertainty about how the UK would calibrate its "pro-innovation" stance relative to both the EU AI Act (which remained in force for UK firms with EU market exposure) and the deregulated US environment.

### Enforcement Outlook

No enforcement actions specifically targeting the UK-US Data Bridge arose from the PCLOB episode. The partial normalization — EO 14086 remaining in force, US courts reinstating PCLOB members, EU Commission proceeding with adequacy renewal — reduced the acute risk. However, the structural vulnerability remains: the bridge's legal foundation depends on continuing US political commitment to EO 14086 and an operationally independent PCLOB, both of which are subject to executive discretion.

## Action Items

- Maintain backup transfer mechanisms (IDTAs or SCCs) for all personal data flows from UK to US, regardless of current UK-US Data Bridge reliance, as insurance against a future adequacy suspension
- Document the current PCLOB operational status in transfer impact assessments updated at least annually; note the partial reinstatement and monitor for further personnel changes
- Monitor the UK Data (Use and Access) Act 2025 implementation guidance from the ICO regarding the new "not materially lower" adequacy test and what it means for maintaining bridge validity under changed US conditions
- Track the UK-EU adequacy renewal process; Commission renewal confirmed as of July 2025 but is conditional and subject to ongoing review
- For AI governance: assess whether any existing compliance frameworks were built against Biden EO 14110 requirements (safety testing, risk assessments) and determine whether those requirements now arise only under UK or EU law, not US law
- Consider joining sector-level advocacy or monitoring through organizations like the [IAPP](https://iapp.org/news/a/how-could-trump-administration-actions-affect-the-eu-u-s-data-privacy-framework-) for live updates on DPF and data bridge status
