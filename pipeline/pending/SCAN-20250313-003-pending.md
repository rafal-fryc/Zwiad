# California Pharma Pixel Privacy Class Actions: Class Certification Pattern Emerges After Partial Dismissals

## Executive Summary [MEDIUM confidence]

**Note on scope of this report:** This report synthesizes a pattern of California pharmaceutical-sector pixel privacy class actions rather than reporting on a single named ruling. The [Drug & Device Law Blog post titled "Pixels and Class Certification" (March 2025)](https://www.druganddevicelawblog.com/2025/03/pixels-and-class-certification.html) — the primary secondary source for this finding — discusses proceedings in California pharma pixel cases that reached class certification following earlier orders that largely dismissed non-CIPA claims. The specific case name and docket number discussed in that article could not be independently confirmed from public sources at the time of writing. If and when the underlying case is identified, a targeted case-specific report should be filed. All analysis below reflects the pattern of outcomes documented across multiple California pharma pixel class actions.

Across multiple California federal courts, a recurring two-stage pattern has emerged in pharmaceutical-sector pixel privacy class actions. First, courts issue orders substantially narrowing cases by dismissing CMIA, constitutional privacy, and consumer protection claims while preserving California Invasion of Privacy Act (CIPA) wiretap and pen register theories. Second, courts have proceeded to certify classes on the surviving CIPA claims, finding the class ascertainable even where neither party possesses a complete membership list. Reported by [Reed Smith LLP via Lexology](https://www.lexology.com/r/UfIAy5G/2486cfe0e8/XXcN) on March 13, 2025, this pattern signals that pixel-based healthcare class actions can survive class certification in California even after significant claim trimming at the pleading stage.

A near-simultaneous development in Georgia — the [first-ever contested class certification](https://www.bursor.com/class-certification-granted-in-landmark-vppa-lawsuit-against-webmd/) under the federal Video Privacy Protection Act (VPPA) against WebMD — confirms that digital tracking litigation across multiple statutes and courts is reaching a new phase: courts are moving from threshold gatekeeping to class-wide merits.

## Background [HIGH confidence]

### The Tracking Pixel Problem in Healthcare

Tracking pixels are small pieces of code — often invisible image files of one pixel by one pixel — embedded on websites by analytics, advertising, and social media companies. When a user visits a webpage containing a pixel, the pixel fires and transmits data (URL visited, search terms, IP address, device identifiers, cookies, and sometimes Facebook user IDs or other identifiers) to the pixel's operator, typically a third party such as Meta, Google, or TikTok. The healthcare and pharmaceutical sectors began deploying these tools widely for digital marketing, measuring ad effectiveness, and audience analysis.

Beginning around 2022, plaintiffs' firms launched a coordinated wave of class actions alleging that pixel deployments on health-related websites caused the unlawful disclosure of protected health information (PHI) or personal health data to Meta and other platforms. Theories vary but cluster around:

- **Federal Wiretap Act / Electronic Communications Privacy Act (ECPA)**: Claim that pixels intercept "contents" of communications in real time
- **California Invasion of Privacy Act (CIPA)**, Cal. Penal Code § 630 et seq.: State analog to the federal wiretap law; also includes a separate "pen register and trap and trace" provision (§ 638.51) that applies to devices tracking outgoing communications
- **California Confidentiality of Medical Information Act (CMIA)**, Cal. Civ. Code § 56 et seq.: Prohibits providers, health plans, and pharmaceutical companies from disclosing "medical information" without authorization
- **HIPAA** (indirectly): While HIPAA does not support a private right of action, courts have used its framework to analyze the sensitivity of disclosed information

The [In re Meta Pixel Healthcare Litigation](https://dockets.justia.com/docket/california/candce/3:2022cv03580/397058) (No. 3:22-cv-03580-WHO, N.D. Cal., Judge William H. Orrick III) is the most prominent consolidated case. It targets Meta directly for facilitating the collection of patient data through pixels installed by hospital systems, healthcare providers, and pharmaceutical companies on their patient portals and consumer-facing websites. Plaintiffs seek to certify multiple classes, including a Prescription Drug Information Class for users of pharmaceutical company websites that transmitted drug-related queries to Meta through its Pixel.

Beyond In re Meta Pixel Healthcare, dozens of parallel suits name individual healthcare providers, pharmacy benefit managers, and pharmaceutical companies directly under CIPA, CMIA, and related statutes.

## Key Findings: What Happened [MEDIUM confidence]

### Stage 1: Prior Orders Largely Gut Most Claims

In multiple California pharma-related pixel privacy class actions, courts have issued orders on motions to dismiss or for summary judgment that eliminated most claims. The pattern of dismissals is consistent across cases:

- **CMIA claims dismissed**: Courts have repeatedly held that merely visiting a pharmaceutical website — even a page about a specific drug — does not constitute disclosure of "medical information" as defined by CMIA. [Fisher Phillips reported](https://www.fisherphillips.com/en/news-insights/court-tosses-most-claims-in-healthcare-pixel-privacy-suit.html) on a parallel ruling (in the TrueCare context, S.D. Cal.) where the court found that website visit metadata "only shows that she used [the] website, but not how she used it or what her visits revealed about her healthcare information."
- **Constitutional privacy claims dismissed**: California constitutional privacy claims require that the plaintiff demonstrate a "legally protected privacy interest" and a "serious invasion" of it. Courts have found that browsing public pharmaceutical websites does not rise to this standard absent evidence the user's specific condition or treatment was revealed.
- **Consumer protection and negligence claims dismissed**: UCL, CLRA, and negligence per se theories typically failed for want of a cognizable economic injury traceable directly to the pixel disclosure.

What survived: **CIPA wiretapping and/or pen register claims**. The key surviving theory is that a pixel functions as an illegal wiretap (§ 631) because it enables the pixel operator (e.g., Meta) to read the contents of internet communications in transit, or as a pen register (§ 638.51) because it records the origins and destinations of electronic communications. California's wiretap statute is broader than the federal ECPA in important ways: it imposes liability on *aiders and abettors* of wiretapping, which means website operators that install Meta Pixel can be sued even if Meta is the primary collector. (See the aiding-and-abetting theory limitation discussed in the Legal Analysis section below.)

### Stage 2: Class Certification Orders on Remaining Claims

Courts have proceeded to certify classes on the surviving CIPA claims. Several procedurally significant features of these orders, as reported by Reed Smith and consistent with other near-contemporaneous rulings, include:

**Ascertainability despite incomplete records.** Courts have held that a class is ascertainable even though neither the plaintiffs nor the defendant possessed complete information sufficient to identify every class member. This mirrors the February 20, 2025 ruling in [Jancik v. WebMD LLC](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-information-security-law/us-district-court-grants-motion-for-class-certification-in-vppa-case) (No. 1:22-cv-00644, N.D. Ga., Judge Thomas W. Thrash Jr.), where the court found the class ascertainable because the pixel itself — through Meta's data records — could match website visitors to Facebook accounts, making membership objectively determinable even if no single party had the full list. The VPPA class in Jancik covered at least 550,213 unique subscriber emails with video-viewing activity on webmd.com.

**Commonality and predominance met for wiretapping claims.** CIPA wiretapping claims are well-suited for class treatment because the alleged wrongful conduct — installing and activating a pixel on a website — is a single act that affects all visitors uniformly. Unlike CMIA or privacy tort claims, which may require individualized inquiry into what was actually disclosed, the pixel's systematic operation on every visit creates common factual questions: Did the pixel fire? What did it transmit? Was any consent obtained from visitors?

**Class period and definition.** Consistent with other pixel cases, classes have been defined by a time window tied to when the pixel was installed through when it was removed or reconfigured, and limited to California residents who visited specific website pages while logged into a social media account (or otherwise identifiable via cookie matching).

## Legal Analysis and Implications [MEDIUM confidence]

### CIPA's Unusual Breadth Creates Persistent Plaintiff-Friendly Territory

California's Invasion of Privacy Act is one of the oldest state wiretap statutes in the country and one of the broadest, as documented in [WilmerHale's 2024 year-in-review on web tracking litigation](https://www.wilmerhale.com/en/insights/blogs/wilmerhale-privacy-and-cybersecurity-law/20250225-year-in-review-2024-web-tracking-litigation-and-enforcement). Unlike many states' wiretap laws — which require that the interceptor be a third party to the communication — CIPA § 631 reaches parties to a communication who aid a third party in intercepting it. This structural feature is what makes CIPA so powerful against website operators: a pharmaceutical company that installs Meta Pixel "assists" Meta in intercepting communications between the company and its website visitors.

**Limitation on the aiding-and-abetting theory:** The CIPA § 631 aiding-and-abetting theory faces a threshold challenge identified in [Cole v. Quest Diagnostics, No. 25-1449 (3d Cir. Nov. 13, 2025)](https://www.insideclassactions.com/2025/11/25/third-circuit-affirms-dismissal-of-cipa-and-cmia-claims/): where the browser sends a *direct, separate transmission* to the pixel provider (making the pixel provider a party to the communication rather than a third-party interceptor), no "interception" occurs and the aiding-and-abetting theory fails. This is because a party to a communication cannot "intercept" it within the meaning of § 631. Note that Cole v. Quest Diagnostics is a Third Circuit ruling; it is not binding on California federal courts (Ninth Circuit), but it is instructive and has been cited by practitioners analyzing § 631 exposure.

**The pen register provision (§ 638.51)** presents a parallel but distinct theory. Added in 2015 (Stats. 2015, Ch. 204, Sec. 2 (AB 929), effective January 1, 2016), this provision is arguably even more powerful for plaintiffs. Courts have split on whether web tracking pixels constitute pen registers under § 638.51 — many federal district courts in the N.D. Cal. have applied the provision to pixels, while California state courts and some federal courts have taken a narrower view. The [Baker Donelson analysis](https://www.bakerdonelson.com/green-light-for-cipa-new-federal-court-ruling-fuels-digital-tracking-class-actions) of a recent Northern District of California ruling confirms that federal courts are increasingly willing to characterize pixel functionality as a trap-and-trace or pen register operation, but this is not a settled consensus. Notably, California state courts (Feb. 2025) dismissed CIPA § 638.51 claims on the basis that the statute does not extend to internet communications or website tools that collect only device-identifying information, and the [Holland & Knight 2026 analysis](https://www.hklaw.com/en/news/pressreleases/2024/08/holland-knight-wins-dismissal-of-meta-pixel-data-privacy-class-action) specifically describes "uncertainty" on this question rather than a settled broad construction. The pen register theory removes the "contents" requirement that defeats many ECPA and § 631 claims — but its ultimate viability remains contested.

Statutory damages under CIPA are significant: $5,000 per violation or three times actual damages, whichever is greater (Cal. Penal Code § 637.2). In a class of hundreds of thousands of users, even a partial success can create exposure in the billions. This dynamic has fueled the plaintiffs' bar and makes class certification a make-or-break moment for defendants.

### The Ascertainability Question Is Now Resolved (in Plaintiffs' Favor)

One of defendants' primary arguments against class certification in pixel cases has been that no one can identify who the class members are without Meta's records, which defendants do not control. The ascertainability findings in California pharma pixel cases — paralleling Jancik v. WebMD — foreclose this defense in the CIPA context. Courts are holding that where Meta's data can be used to identify matched users, the class is sufficiently ascertainable under Rule 23 even if the defendant itself lacks the records. This shifts the practical battlefield: defendants must either obtain Meta's records in discovery or concede ascertainability.

### CMIA Continues to Narrow as a Viable Theory

The dismissal of CMIA claims in these and parallel cases reflects a growing judicial consensus: the statute protects "medical information," defined as information about a patient's "medical history, mental or physical condition, or treatment." The mere fact that a user visited a pharmaceutical company's website — even a page for a specific drug — does not establish that their medical condition was disclosed. [The Third Circuit's November 2025 ruling affirming dismissal in Cole v. Quest Diagnostics](https://www.insideclassactions.com/2025/11/25/third-circuit-affirms-dismissal-of-cipa-and-cmia-claims/) (Third Circuit; not binding in California federal courts, but provides instructive CMIA analysis) applied the same reasoning, holding that disclosing a URL that a patient used to view test results only revealed that they had been a Quest patient — not their medical condition — and thus fell outside CMIA.

For pharmaceutical companies, this creates a nuanced landscape: CMIA claims are hard to sustain on public-facing marketing websites, but patient portals, condition-specific drug interaction tools, and authenticated prescription management pages present higher risk because the information at issue more plausibly reveals health status.

### VPPA Is a Parallel Threat for Health Information Websites Hosting Video Content

The landmark Jancik v. WebMD certification order (February 20, 2025) covers the VPPA — a 1988 federal statute originally enacted to protect video rental records — which has been expansively applied to cover video viewing on websites. If a health information website (WebMD, Mayo Clinic, pharmaceutical company "disease education" pages, etc.) hosts videos AND uses Meta Pixel or similar technology, plaintiffs can argue the VPPA was violated every time a video view was shared with Meta. VPPA damages are $2,500 per violation, and the Jancik class includes over half a million subscribers. The district court's ascertainability finding in that case (that Facebook's pixel data can reconstruct membership) mirrors the California rulings and will be cited nationally.

## What This Means for Businesses [MEDIUM confidence]

**Pharmaceutical and Healthcare Companies**

- **Patient portals and authenticated pages carry the highest risk.** CIPA wiretapping claims require that the pixel transmit "contents" of communications — and courts are more likely to find "contents" when a user is interacting with a page that reveals their specific health interests or treatment (e.g., a patient portal, a prescription refill page, a drug dosage calculator).
- **Removal is not retroactive relief.** Removing a pixel now does not eliminate liability for past deployments. Class periods typically run from installation through removal, meaning current decisions about pixel deployment affect the scope of future class definitions, not past exposure.
- **Consent defenses are viable but narrow.** Courts have dismissed CIPA claims where a valid cookie consent banner was shown and the user affirmatively opted in to tracking. However, consent must be unambiguous and specific — a generic "by using this site you agree to our terms" does not suffice. [WilmerHale's 2024 year-in-review](https://www.wilmerhale.com/en/insights/blogs/wilmerhale-privacy-and-cybersecurity-law/20250225-year-in-review-2024-web-tracking-litigation-and-enforcement) notes that robust opt-in consent mechanisms have defeated pixel claims in several California federal courts.
- **Class certification is now a real risk, not just a procedural hurdle.** The ascertainability rulings from California pharma pixel cases, combined with Jancik v. WebMD, make it harder to defeat pixel class certification on the ground that the class cannot be identified. Defense strategy must shift toward defeating commonality or predominance by showing individualized consent, individualized injury, or individualized damages.
- **The § 631 aiding-and-abetting theory has a real limitation.** Where the browser sends a direct transmission to the pixel provider, defendants should argue that no interception occurs and the aiding-and-abetting theory fails under the Cole v. Quest reasoning. While Cole is Third Circuit authority (non-binding in the Ninth Circuit), it represents a coherent legal argument that defense counsel should preserve.

**General Website Operators in California**

- **CIPA pen register claims are the most persistent threat — but with uncertainty.** Even where courts dismiss wiretapping (§ 631) and CMIA theories, the pen register provision (§ 638.51) has survived multiple motions to dismiss and demonstrated the capacity for class certification in N.D. Cal. However, the court split on whether pixels qualify as pen registers means this theory is not guaranteed to succeed. Any website using analytics or advertising pixels without explicit written consent from California visitors faces this exposure, but outcomes will turn on the specific court and facts.
- **Audit pixel inventory now.** Organizations should conduct an inventory of all third-party tags firing on their websites, identify what data is transmitted, assess whether the data could constitute "contents" of communications under CIPA, and verify whether any consent mechanisms satisfy California's requirements.
