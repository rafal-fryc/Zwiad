Highlights 

 

  • Washington Governor Signs Two AI Bills Into Law: Washington enacted two AI bills, including a generative AI transparency law (HB 1170) and the first chatbot law of 2026 (HB 2225), which, while framed around “companion chatbots,” may apply more broadly to other AI systems and imposes new safeguards and design restrictions.
  • Seven Bills Pass First Chamber: Idaho’s S 1297 (chatbots); Maryland’s HB 952 (chatbots), HB 883 (health/ chatbots), and SB 0387/ HB 895 (data-driven pricing), and Vermont’s H 814 (study bill) and H 816 (health).
  • White House Issues Legislative Recommendations for a Federal AI Framework: The White House released a roadmap for a national AI framework centered on priorities like child safety and innovation, while advancing a preemptive federal standard to replace the growing patchwork of state AI laws, with legislation expected soon.
  • Democrats Introduce Bill to Repeal AI Executive Order: Rep. Don Beyer (D-VA) introduced the “GUARDRAILS Act” to nullify President Trump’s December 2025 AI executive order and block its implementation using federal funds.

 

Stuff from the States

 

California - 

  • ADMT/Employment Bill Passes Committee - AB 1898 would establish transparency requirements for the use of “workplace AI tools” (ADMTs) that impact employment decisions or enable worker surveillance, requiring employers to provide advance notice and detailed disclosures to workers. Employers would be required to inform workers about how AI is used, what data is collected, and how it may affect roles, performance, or employment conditions, and maintain an updated inventory of such tools. The bill would allow for private rights of action, with penalties, damages, and injunctive relief available for violations. 
  • Transparency - AB 2713: Introduced by Asm. Wicks (D), this bill would increase the civil penalty for violations of the California AI Transparency Act to $5,500 per violation and reinforces enforcement mechanisms for ensuring public access to AI detection tools.

 

Connecticut - Employment - SB 515: Introduced by the Committee on Finance, Revenue, and Bonding, this bill would establish a framework for a surcharge on employers who replace workers with technology and use the proceeds to fund retraining and transition programs.

 

Idaho - Chatbot Bill Passes First Chamber: S 1297 would require operators of “conversational AI services” to disclose to minors that they are interacting with AI, develop protocols to respond to suicidal ideation from users, and most notably, restrict engagement features designed to increase use (e.g. offering rewards at unpredictable intervals), and prohibit sexually explicit or manipulative content for minors. It would also bar systems from fostering emotional or romantic dependence with minors, and requires operators to provide parental controls and privacy tools for minor users. There is no private right of action.

 

Maryland - 

  • Chatbot Bill Passes First Chamber - HB 952: This bill would regulate companion chatbots, combining safety, transparency, and data protection requirements, making it one of the first chatbot bills this year with a strong focus on user data protection to advance through a legislative chamber. Similar to other proposed chatbot bills, it would require self-harm detection and intervention protocols, persistent and recurring disclosures that users are interacting with AI. However, it would also set limits on collecting and using sensitive data, including prohibiting the use of emotional or mental health data to drive engagement. The bill would also require the establishment of a user complaint system for violations. Notably, it would also treat chatbots as products subject to strict liability, imposing an affirmative duty to prevent user harm, establishing strict liability for injuries, and allowing individuals to bring claims for design, manufacturing, or marketing defects.
  • Health / Chatbot Bill Passes First Chamber - HB 883: This bill would prohibit AI systems from presenting themselves as behavioral health providers or being designed to deliver behavioral health care. It also would require disclosures that users are interacting with AI and mandate protocols to detect and respond to self-harm or suicidal ideation. Notably, the bill includes exceptions for general wellness or guidance tools that do not claim to provide behavioral health care, as well as AI used by licensed providers for administrative support.
  • Data Driven Pricing Bills Pass First Chamber - SB 0387 and HB 895 (companion bills) would prohibit food retailers and third-party delivery services from using protected class data to offer or price goods in a way that denies consumers equal access to benefits or services. The bill includes exceptions for standard practices such as promotions, loyalty programs, and pricing differences based on costs, supply, or demand.

 

Minnesota - 

  • Health / Chatbot Bill Passes Committee - HF 3893 an Illinois HB 1806-style bill with additional chatbot-specific provisions, HF 3893 would prohibit unlicensed AI-provided therapy and allow only limited administrative or support uses of AI under human oversight, with licensed professionals retaining full responsibility for interactions, outputs, and data use. However, notably, this bill also introduces new provisions around chatbots/ “ambient listening.” Would require chatbots used in administrative support roles by licensed professionals to disclose they are not human and direct users to seek help when mental health or self-harm issues arise, and imposes consent and transparency requirements for “ambient listening” (devices that capture and transcribe conversations), including disclosures on data use, storage, access, training, and the right to opt out without penalty. 
  • AI Safety/ Frontier Models - HF 4532: Introduced by Rep. Jones (D), this bill is modeled on the New York RAISE Act but takes a broader approach, applying to any person that has trained an AI model (rather than distinguishing frontier developers) and lowering the threshold for critical risk incidents, while still establishing safety, transparency, and disclosure requirements.
  • Bills were also introduced on: ADMT/Employment (SF 4689/HF 4445), Employment (SF 4686/HF 4451 and SF 4573/HF 4537), Data-Driven Pricing (SF 4711/HF 4454), AI Safety (SF 4636/HF 4544), and Chatbots (HF 4452).

 

New Jersey - Data-Driven Pricing Bill Passes Committee - A4085 would prohibit the use of personalized or surveillance-based algorithmic pricing for groceries and food items, making it an unlawful trade practice to vary prices based on personal data. It allows limited exceptions for cost-based pricing and broadly available discounts or loyalty programs, while restricting the use of personal data to those specific purposes. The bill also bans the use of electronic shelf labels connected to systems that enable personalized pricing.

 

South Carolina - Chatbots - SB 1037: Introduced by Sen. Kimbrell (R), this bill would establish strict requirements for chatbot operators to verify user age, restrict minors' access to certain features without parental consent, and implement safeguards against harm.

 

Vermont - 

  • Health Bill Amended as a Study Bill and Passes Chamber - H 814: This bill was amended to become a study bill establishing an Artificial Intelligence Advisory Council to advise the state on AI use across government, including health care, education, and public services. The Council will conduct research, engage in public outreach, and develop recommendations on responsible AI use, including potential statutory changes and emerging issues like neurotechnology. Previously, the bill would have mandated disclaimers for AI-generated patient communications, restricted advertising and data collection for mental health chatbots, and enumerated a list of “neurological rights”. 
  • Health Bill Passes Chamber - H 816: Similar to Illinois HB 1806, this bill would restrict AI use within mental health services to administrative and support functions, while requiring licensed professionals to retain full responsibility for care. It would prohibit AI from independently making diagnoses, treatment decisions, or generating treatment plans, and allow disciplinary action for violations. It would also classify the misuse of AI by mental health professionals as unprofessional conduct.

 

Washington - Two bills signed into law: 

  • GenAI / Transparency Bill Signed- HB 1170: This bill requires covered providers (those with over 1 million monthly users) to include provenance data (e.g. metadata or watermarking) in content that is generated or materially altered by their generative AI systems, with exclusions for video games and systems used solely for upscaling, noise reduction, or compression. “Materially altered” content refers to significant modifications, while minor edits (e.g., cropping or color adjustments) are excluded. The bill also requires government agencies to clearly and conspicuously disclose when consumers are interacting with an AI system and allows disclosures via hyperlink. The law takes effect February 1, 2027.
  • First Chatbot Law of the Year Signed - HB 2225: The first chatbot law enacted in 2026, this bill requires operators of AI companion chatbots to implement safeguards for users. While the law is framed around “companion chatbots,” in practice its reach may extend further as those lines are not always easy to draw and other systems may fall into scope. The law takes effect January 1, 2027.
    • Definitions: The bill defines an AI companion chatbot as providing “adaptive, human-like responses” and sustaining an “ongoing relationship” with users across multiple interactions. Similar to California’s SB 243, the definition excludes bots used solely for business operations or customer service, gaming-related chat features, and voice assistants that do not sustain relationships, and (newly) adds a carveout for narrowly tailored educational tools. However, the law goes further than SB 243 in key areas (particularly in restricting engagement optimization design) and raises open questions for stakeholders around compliance with these provisions and which systems may ultimately fall within scope. 
    • Notable provisions include:
      • Hourly disclosures for minors (more frequent than SB 243 and other prior laws)
      • New limits on engagement design- targeting features that encourage emotional dependence, simulate relationships, discourage breaks, or promote isolation (first chatbot law with these requirements)
      • Required self-harm protocols, including detection, crisis referrals, and public reporting of the number of crisis referral notifications issued to users.
      • A private right of action (without statutory damages)

Federal

 

White House Issues Legislative Recommendations for a Federal AI Framework: The White House released its recommendations for a National AI Legislative Framework, outlining a federal roadmap centered on seven policy priorities, including child safety and a preemptive national standard for AI regulation. The framework builds on President Trump’s December 2025 executive order aimed at limiting state-level AI laws and reflects growing federal focus on replacing the emerging patchwork of state regulations surrounding AI. It also follows recent congressional activity, including Sen. Blackburn’s AI discussion draft. While Sen. Blackburn’s proposal has seen limited traction, the White House-backed framework is expected to drive legislation introduced in coming weeks. The framework calls upon Congress to create a legislative proposal that does the following:

  • Protecting Children and Empowering Parents: Require AI platforms to implement child safety protections, including parental controls, limits on harmful content, and privacy-protective age assurance requirements, while affirming existing child privacy laws and avoiding overly broad liability standards.
  • Safeguarding and Strengthening American Communities: Promote AI infrastructure development and economic growth, such as streamlined permitting for data centers, support for small businesses, and enhanced safeguards against AI-enabled fraud and national security risks.
  • Respecting Intellectual Property Rights and Supporting Creators: Support protections for creators against infringing AI outputs while preserving free expression, deferring key copyright questions to courts, and encouraging licensing frameworks and safeguards against unauthorized digital replicas.
  • Preventing Censorship and Protecting Free Speech:  Encourage Congress to prevent the US government from “coercing technology providers” to “ban, compel, or alter content based on partisan or ideological agendas,” and to provide Americans a way to seek redress for impacts from any such federal efforts to censor or dictate information from AI platforms.
  • Enabling Innovation and Ensuring American AI Dominance: Promote U.S. leadership in AI by reducing regulatory barriers, enabling innovation through tools like regulatory sandboxes and relying on existing sector-specific regulators rather than creating a new federal AI regulatory body.
  • Educating Americans and Developing an AI-Ready Workforce: Expand education, training, and research initiatives to help workers benefit from AI-driven economic growth and job transitions.
  • Establishing a Federal Policy Framework, Preempting Cumbersome State AI Laws: Establish a preemptive federal AI framework to replace fragmented state laws, while preserving state authority over general consumer protection, child safety, fraud, zoning, and government use of AI. The proposal would limit states’ ability to regulate AI development or impose burdens on lawful AI use.

 

Democrats Introduce Bill to Repeal AI Executive Order - HR 8031: Introduced by Rep. Beyer (D-VA), the “GUARDRAILS Act” would repeal President Trump’s executive order “Ensuring A National Policy Framework for AI” introduced this past December. The bill would state that the executive order “shall have no force or effect” and that no federal funds may be used to carry it out. The bill has 33 Democratic co-sponsors and a companion bill is set to be introduced in the Senate. The proposal reflects broader concerns among some lawmakers that the executive order could preempt state action without establishing clear federal protections or governance standards.

 

Chatbot Bill Introduced - HR 7985: Introduced by Rep. Mullin (D-CA), this bill would prohibit AI chatbot providers from falsely representing or implying that their systems are licensed medical, legal, or financial professionals, or that outputs are verified by such professionals. The bill responds to growing concerns around consumer harm and deception, particularly for vulnerable users, by extending existing professional impersonation protections to AI systems. It would also direct the Federal Trade Commission to issue guidance establishing clear compliance standards for industry.

 

Liability Bill Introduced - S 3982: Introduced by Sen. Sheehy (R-MT) and Sen. Rochester (D-DE), this bill would criminalize AI-driven digital impersonation fraud and establish regulatory and technical frameworks for detection and prevention. (House companion bill: HR 7786).
