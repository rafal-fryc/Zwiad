Good morning everyone,  

 

The big news of the week is that the FTC released an enforcement policy statement on COPPA concerning age verification, announcing that they will not bring COPPA enforcement actions against “certain website and online service operators that collect, use, and disclose personal information for the sole purpose of determining a user’s age via age verification technologies.” As always, you can find our legislation and state enforcement trackers in the FPF Portal.

💬 New Resource on Alabama App Store Accountability Act: Last week, Alabama enacted SB 161—the first App Store Accountability Act (ASAA) enacted in 2026. The Act differs from prior ASAA laws by extending age verification and parental consent requirements to existing accounts, requiring parental consent for pre-installed apps, removing application exemptions, and adopting a definition for “in-app purchases.” FPF published a two-page overview here.

 

💬 New Resource on Oklahoma Comprehensive Privacy Bill! Oklahoma is poised to become the first state in nearly two years to enact a comprehensive consumer privacy bill, as SB 546 has been sent to the Senate for a concurrence vote. You can find a two-page overview of the bill—which closely adheres to the Virginia model—on the FPF Portal. 

 

Stuff from the States 

 

Alabama - Comprehensive Privacy Bill Passed House (HB 351): A Connecticut-style comprehensive privacy bill passed the House by a vote of 104-0 earlier this week. Alabama came close to enacting a comprehensive privacy law last year but fell short. 

 

Alaska - Unique Comprehensive Privacy Bill Introduced (HB 367): Rep. Story (D) introduced a novel comprehensive privacy bill this week. Among its requirements are: substantive data minimization for the collection, use, retention, and sharing of PI; distinctions between business purposes, commercial purposes, and operational purposes; opt-in consent for uses of precise geolocation data apart from what is “necessary to provide goods or services that a consumer requests and reasonably expects”; restrictions on disclosing, selling, or sharing the PI of consumers known (or recklessly disregarded) to be aged 16 years or younger; and data broker registration requirements.

 

California - The bill introduction deadline passed last Friday (although new proposals can be introduced later by the “gut-and-amend” process). Here are some bills that were introduced: 

  • Privacy-Protective Default Settings Bill Introduced (AB 2561): Asm. Valencia (D) introduced a CCPA amendment that would require an operating system or an application to configure a user’s default privacy setting to be the most privacy protective setting offered by the operating system or application and would prohibit an operating system or an application from changing a user’s privacy setting without the user’s explicit consent.
  • Mortgage Applications Bill Introduced (AB 2154): Asm. Garcia (D) introduced a bill that would prohibit a person from selling to a third party personal information of a mortgage applicant that was collected by the person during the mortgage application process.

 

Kentucky - Automated Content Recognition Amendment Introduced (HB 692): Rep. Branscum (R) introduced a KCDPA amendment that would add “automatic content recognition data” captured by a smart television or smart monitor as a category of sensitive data. 

 

Minnesota - Consumer Health Bills Active After Hiatus (HF 2700) (SF 2940): Proposed amendments to the MNCDPA that were introduced last year are now active again and received hearings this week. An amendment to HF 2700 pares back some of the proposed changes to the law. Under that most recent text, the bill would add Connecticut SB 3–style protections for consumer health data and add protections against “sharing” sensitive data (requiring opt-in consent).

 

New York

  • Broad Consumer Health Bill (NYHIPA) Reintroduced with Changes (S.9269): The New York Health Information Privacy Act (NYHIPA)—a broad, My Health My Data-style bill—has been reintroduced after being vetoed by Governor Hochul last year. This iteration of the bill includes a changed definition of “regulated health information,” a clarification that consumer rights requests must be verified, expanded permissible processing purposes under the substantive data minimization requirements (capturing product improvement and development), and removing the 24-hour waiting period for obtaining valid authorization for processing purposes that are not strictly necessary under the law. 
  • Networked Camera Device Privacy Bill Introduced (S.9267): Sen. May (D) introduced a bill that would regulate manufacturers and operators of internet-connected camera devices (e.g., video door bells), requiring affirmative opt-in consent, separate from general terms of service, to: enable any “coordinated surveillance feature”; use footage for purposes other than services directly requested by the owner (e.g., algorithm training or product development); retain captured footage that has not been activated with a paid subscription for more than seventy-two hours; or condition basic functionality on owner participation in a “coordinated surveillance feature.” This bill would also impose transparency requirements and limit law enforcement access to footage.

 

South Dakota

  • ISP Privacy Bill Passed Senate (SB 110): The Senate voted 28-6 to pass an ISP privacy bill last Friday. This bill would prohibit a broadband internet access service provider from using, disclosing, selling, or permitting access to customer personal information without prior, opt-in consent, and it would prohibit ISPs from refusing to serve a customer, charging a customer a higher price, or offering a customer a discount or other benefit based on the customer's decision to provide (or not provide) consent. The bill includes several exceptions, including use of personal information as “necessary” for providing the broadband internet access service. 
  • ASAA Bill Passed Senate (HB 1275): This bill passed the Senate as amended in committee on February 20. The bill is now up for consideration in the Senate, where it has already been quickly scheduled for a committee hearing.

 

Texas - AG Targeting Potential Data Sharing in China: Texas AG Paxton has brought a series of lawsuits in the past week all targeting companies (TP Link, Anzu Robots, Lorex, Temu, and Shein) with ties to or a presence in China. These enforcement actions address a perceived risk of the Chinese government accessing Americans' sensitive personal information under Chinese law, which the AG alleges is a material omission in companies’ privacy notices (e.g., “Shein’s failure to disclose the CCP’s access to consumer data has misled Texas consumers into disclosing their personal information to Shein when making purchases, without any understanding of who may ultimately have access to that data”).

 

Vermont - Genetic Privacy Bill Passed House (H.639): The House passed a direct-to-consumer genetic testing privacy bill on February 25. This bill includes consumer data rights, consent requirements, and data security obligations.

 

Virginia 

  • Prohibition on Selling Precise Location Data Passed Committee (SB 338): The House Communications, Technology and Innovation committee reported SB 338 on February 23. This bill—which would amend the VCDPA to prohibit a controller from selling precise geolocation data—previously passed the Senate on February 3. 
  • Reproductive Privacy Amendment Passed Committee (SB 177): The House General Laws committee favorably reported SB 177, a bill that would amend the Virginia Consumer Protection Act to add a GLBA data-level exception to the consent requirement for obtaining, disclosing, selling, or disseminating personally identifiable reproductive or sexual health information. This bill previously passed the Senate on January 27. 

 

Wisconsin - ASAA Bill Passed Assembly (AB 962): This bill passed the Assembly with amendments on February 19 and is now up for consideration in the Senate. Bill amendments adopted prior to passage include: allowing developers to direct an app store to prevent minors from downloading the developer’s apps; providing developers with exceptions from the bill’s requirements if they implement processes to ensure that only adults can create accounts, allow adult users to create accounts on behalf of minors, comply with COPPA, and implement parental controls; removing the private right of action; and creating an exemption for “video service franchise” as defined under existing law. The Wisconsin Assembly adjourned early for the year, which may pose challenges to the Senate if that body seeks to further amend the bill. 

 

Federal Happenings

 

FTC Issues Policy Statement on Incentivizing the Use of Age Verification: A month out from January’s workshop on age verification technology, the Federal Trade Commission has issued an enforcement policy statement on COPPA to incentivize the use of age verification technology. The policy statement is directed at “mixed audience” and “general audience” websites and online services (“Relevant Operators”), pleading “not [to] bring an enforcement action under the COPPA Rule against a Relevant Operator that collects, uses, or discloses personal information for the purpose of determining a user’s age (‘Age Verification Purposes’) without first obtaining verifiable parental consent.” There are a number of conditions that a relevant operator must meet to benefit from this policy, including: strict purpose limitation requirements; disclosing information for age verification purposes to third parties only after sufficient vetting and contracted assurances to maintain confidentiality, security, and integrity of information collected and used; data retention, deletion and security requirements; adequate notice to parents and children; reasonable accuracy; and, importantly, “complying with the COPPA Rule’s requirements in every other respect with regard to personal information collected from children.” (emphasis added.) 

 

Because this is merely an FTC policy statement, it does not affect the meaning of the COPPA Rule. However, the Commission indicated in the policy statement that it “intends to initiate a review of the COPPA Rule” in the near future to address collection, use, and disclosure of minors’ data for age verification.  

 

What We’re Reading

 

NAI Releases Factor Analysis for “Health-Related Sensitive Personal Information”: The use of health-related data in advertising has come under significant regulatory scrutiny in recent years. For example, the California AG reached a record settlement under the CCPA last year with publisher Healthline concerning the sharing of webpage titles for advertising, alleging that such conduct violated the purpose limitation requirements in the CCPA regulations. The NAI has released a new factor analysis exploring some of the nuances around this issue and presenting five key factors for evaluating whether certain personal information could constitute “health-related sensitive personal information.” These factors include the source of the PI being processed, the contents of the PI being processed, the intended use of the PI, whether consumers have any heightened expectation of privacy, and the risk of consumer harm. The document includes several hypotheticals illustrating how to apply the factors.
