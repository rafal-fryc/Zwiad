Stuff from the States 

 

Alabama - Biological & Neural Data Bill Passed House (HB 263): The Alabama House has passed a bill that would prohibit the disclosure of biological data (i.e., data generated by measurement of an individual's biological, genetic, biochemical, physiological, or neural properties used for identification purposes) or neural data without a consumer's consent. The bill was amended to apply only to owners or operators of a health and fitness application. 

 

California

  • CalPrivacy Board Meeting Recap: In their latest board meeting, CalPrivacy provided updates on the DROP implementation. Data brokers will be required to access the DROP platform every 45 days and begin processing data deletion requests starting August 1. There are 575 data brokers registered with the state as of February 2026, and more than 85,000 consumers have registered to receive DROP updates. The agency has appointed its first chief privacy auditor. As a reminder, data brokers will have to start undergoing independent third party audits starting in 2028. 
  • CalPrivacy Releases CCPA Settlement with PlayOn Sports: We have our second CCPA settlement of the year, this time between CalPrivacy and PlayOn Sports, a company that operates a digital ticketing, streaming, fundraising, concessions, and merchandise sales platform targeted at schools. CalPrivacy alleged that the company: forced consumers to agree to the use of tracking technologies for advertising to use the service; did not provide its own method for consumers to opt-out of the selling/sharing of personal data, instead directing students to opt-out through third party ad partners; did not recognize opt-out preference signals; and did not adequately describe consumers’ privacy rights in its privacy notice. The settlement imposed a $1.1 million penalty and noted the company’s cooperation, engagement, and candidness with the agency. The press release emphasizes the need for strong privacy protections given students’ vulnerability: “Students are a uniquely vulnerable population whose data should be used to enhance their own learning, not to fuel advertising and commercial surveillance.” This is an interesting parallel to last year’s Healthline settlement by the California AG, which also focused on the “sensitivity” of the data used for advertising, even if the data in question isn’t sensitive personal information under the law. 
  • CalPrivacy Releases CCPA Settlement with Ford: CalPrivacy announced a second settlement of the week, this time with Ford, on March 5. This was yet another enforcement action focusing on allegedly insufficient opt-out processes, showing that the agency remains focused on reducing friction in exercising consumer rights. CalPrivacy alleged that the company unnecessarily required consumers to verify their identity (via email address) to exercise their opt-out rights. The settlement imposed a $375 thousand fine and, like the PlayOn Sports settlement, requires the company to audit the tracking technologies it utilizes and ensure compliance with opt-out preference signals.

 

Iowa - Driving Data Lawsuit Filed by AG under Consumer Fraud Act: Iowa became the latest state to sue GM for the collection, use, and disclosure of driving data. In the complaint, AG Bird alleged that the company violated the Iowa Consumer Fraud Act. Most of the allegations focus on alleged false and misleading representations about the company’s data collection and disclosure practices, data sharing with third parties, and coercive enrollment practices. For example, the AG alleged that the company failed to disclose that driving data would be sold to third parties and stated that such data would be used only to enhance and develop the company’s products. The AG also alleged that the company incentivized employees to adopt coercive practices to get consumers to enroll in data collection programs. 

 

Louisiana - Comprehensive Privacy Bill Introduced (SB 386): Sen. Connick (R) introduced a Virginia-style bill with the broader definition of sale (“or other valuable consideration”). 

 

Maine - Impending Senate Vote on Comprehensive Privacy Bill (LD 1822): Yesterday the Maine Senate voted to adopt Committee Amendment “A” on LD 1822, a Maryland-style comprehensive privacy bill, as well as an amendment to that amendment. Key changes in the Senate floor amendment include: expanding the definition of “publicly available information”; adding an exemption for political organizations; and delaying the effective date to September 1, 2027 (alongside related conforming changes to dates). The bill’s second reading is scheduled for the next legislative day. If the bill passes the Senate, it will need to be reconciled with the House version.  

 

Oregon - Legislature Passed Data Broker Immigration Enforcement Bill (SB 1587): The Oregon legislature passed a bill that would prohibit a public body from disclosing PII to a data broker “unless the data broker first provides a written attestation to the public body that the information will not be sold or otherwise transferred to any entity that will use the information to enforce federal immigration law.” This bill has yet to be transmitted to the governor. 

 

South Dakota - House Passed Genetic Privacy Bill (SB 49): The House passed a direct-to-consumer genetic privacy bill on March 4. This bill previously passed the Senate but will require a concurrence vote after being amended in a House committee. 

 

Virginia

  • Judge Enjoins One-Hour Social Media Limit for Minors: The VCDPA was amended last year to require operators of social media platforms to conduct age verification to determine which users are minors and subsequently limit a minor’s use of the platform to one hour unless adjusted by a parent. A federal judge has issued a preliminary injunction against the social media amendment, finding that plaintiff NetChoice has a substantial likelihood of success on the merits of its facial First Amendment challenge. The court found that the restriction was a content-based restriction on speech due to its inclusion of topic-specific exemptions (e.g., news, sports, entertainment, and e-commerce) and therefore subject to strict scrutiny. The court then concluded that the law is likely to fail strict scrutiny because it is not the least restrictive means of achieving the government’s interest of protecting minors from the harms of social media use, pointing to the existence of parental controls for social media. Even if intermediate scrutiny were to be applied, the court concluded that the law is underinclusive and therefore not narrowly tailored.
  • Ban on Selling Precise Geolocation Data Passed Legislature (SB 338): The Virginia legislature has passed an amendment to the VCDPA that would prohibit a controller from selling (or offering for sale) precise geolocation data concerning a consumer. The bill will head to Governor Spanberger (D) to sign or veto. 
  • Consumer Health Data Amendment Passed Legislature (SB 177): The legislature also passed SB 177, a proposed amendment to the Virginia Consumer Protect Act, late last week. If signed by Governor Spanberger, this bill will add a GLBA data-level exception to the consent requirement for obtaining, disclosing, selling, or disseminating personally identifiable reproductive or sexual health information.
  • AG Issues Press Release Reminding Virginians of Privacy Rights: As part of National Consumer Protection Week, new Virginia Attorney General Jay Jones recently issued a press release reminding Virginians of their rights under the VCDPA and how to submit a complaint. Although not stated in the press release, this could portend increased enforcement activity in VA. 

 

Federal Happenings

 

Senate Passed COPPA 2.0 by Unanimous Consent (S. 836): The Senate voted to pass COPPA 2.0 by unanimous consent on March 5. The vote took place while the House Energy & Commerce Committee was actively engaged in a markup hearing considering its own partisan-driven youth privacy and online safety bill package, which includes a COPPA 2.0 proposal. Notably, the House’s diverging take on COPPA 2.0 may be setting up a greater cross-chamber debate that could determine the future outlook of the long-awaited children’s privacy update in this Congress. Indeed, Senator Markey even opined in remarks he delivered on the floor prior to voting that the House’s “weaker partisan” version of COPPA 2.0 “strips away key protections” and “undermines the strong bipartisan compromise that members of this chamber have worked for years to develop.” 

 

Later in the day, the House Energy & Commerce Committee tabled consideration of its version of COPPA 2.0 and a proposed amendment that would have further diverged the House version from the Senate’s crossover bill. Explaining his decision not to consider COPPA 2.0 in that hearing, Chairman Guthrie stated that staffers from both sides of the aisle had been working behind the scenes to regain bipartisan support for COPPA 2.0 throughout the day and—with both sides feeling there has been “substantial progress” towards that end—the bill would be held for continued negotiation in the coming days.

 

House E&C Committee Holds Markup of Youth Privacy & Online Safety Bills: As mentioned above, the House Energy & Commerce Committee amended and voted to report several youth privacy and online safety bills during a markup yesterday. 

  • Kids Internet and Digital Safety (KIDS) Act (H.R. 7757): The Committee adopted this bill as amended by an amendment in the nature of a substitute (AINS) from Rep. Guthrie (R-KY). This is an omnibus bill combining 12 separate proposals originally introduced in the youth online privacy and safety bill package last November, including: the SCREEN Act, KOSA, the Safe Messaging for Kids Act, the SPY Kids Act, the Safer GAMING Act, and the SAFE BOTs Act, among others. Most notably, the amendment changes KOSA’s preemption language, clarifying that the bill’s broadly applicable preemption clause would not preclude state laws on: contracts, torts, or product liability; generally applicable consumer protection laws; or criminal law remedies. As Chairman Guthrie mentioned during the hearing, this approach is intended to exempt preclusion of “multiple critical state laws” such as consumer protection laws to ensure that deceptive trade practice suits against “big tech” companies involving “addictive design” and harm to minors—a theme in 2025 enforcement actions—can still go forward. The Safer GAMING Act and SAFE BOTS Act both contain matching preemption language which, when taken together, tracks with the primary industry targets of trending state consumer protection law enforcement actions over the past two years: social media companies, gaming companies, and AI companion chatbot providers.
  • Sammy’s Law (H.R. 2657): The committee adopted this bill as amended by an AINS from Rep. Carter (R-GA). This bill would impose parental monitoring obligations on large social media platform providers, requiring them to make available APIs allowing third-party safety software providers to manage children’s interactions, content, and account settings under a parent or guardian’s delegation. Changes in the amendment include: extending the timeline for creating the third-party-accessible real-time API for third-party safety software providers from 30 days to 180 days after the effective date; expanding data minimization and data security obligations; adding protections with respect to encrypted messages; prohibiting third-party safety software providers from selling user data; amongst other changes. 
  • App Store Accountability Act (H.R. 3149): The Committee adopted this bill as amended by an AINS from Rep. James (R-MI). This bill would establish a system of user age verification and parental consent for minors at the app store level similar to the laws enacted in Texas, Utah, Louisiana, and Alabama. Key changes in the amendment include: extending age verification and parental consent requirements to existing accounts; requiring parental consent for pre-installed apps; adopting a definition for “in-app purchases;” allowing a developer to request that app store providers prevent minor app downloads; and adding CA AB 1043-style language permitting developers to rely on alternative age category information than what is provided by an app store age signal if the developer has “clear and convincing” evidence that the internal information is more accurate. Interestingly, the bill also included “Judicial Review” language purporting to restrict the jurisdiction of constitutional challenges to the District Court for the District of Columbia and requiring that any constitutional challenges to the bill be brought within 60 days after its enactment.

 

FPF will provide further analysis on these bills in forthcoming member resources and communications.

 

FTC Workshop on Consumer Benefits and Injuries of the Data-Driven Economy: The Federal Trade Commission held a workshop last week on consumer injuries and benefits in the data-driven economy. Panels addressed quantifying injuries and benefits to consumers, data breaches and other negative impacts to consumers, the costs and benefits of behavioral and contextual advertising, and measuring consumer preferences. The workshop had a notably pro- ‘consumer control’ tone. FTC staff involvement also signaled a shift from the prior Commission’s privacy priorities. In his opening remarks, for example, Chairman Ferguson emphasized promoting “consumer choice” and not assuming “a priori that there is a necessary conflict between consumer choice and technological innovation that drives economic growth.” In the closing remarks, Director of the Bureau of Consumer Protection Chris Mufarrige chided past commissions for “attempting to remake markets in a planner’s vision” and stated that “government is never in a better position to make decisions about consumers’ lives than consumers themselves, and when government attempts to remake markets, such intervention hems in innovation, ultimately depriving consumers of choice.”

  • For more: Since the workshop, Director Mufarrige conducted an interview with Suzanne Smalley from The Record, in which he discussed topics such as age verification, the TAKE IT DOWN Act, data brokers, and "surveillance pricing."

‘Hard Fork’ Podcast Discusses Pentagon AI Dispute as Consequence of Failing to Pass Federal Privacy Law: The NYT’s Hard Fork podcast discussed the unfolding disagreement between the U.S. Department of Defense and major AI providers concerning companies’ prohibited uses of their own AI models.
