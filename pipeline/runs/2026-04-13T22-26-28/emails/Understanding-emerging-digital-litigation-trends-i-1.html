LAW & REGULATION—EU & U.K.

UK government considers increased alignment with EU rules

Reuters reports Members of U.K. Parliament will soon vote on government-drafted legislation to align various regulations with the EU with the aim to ease trade. Prime Minister Keir Starmer noted the U.K.'s "best interests" lie with "a stronger, closer relationship with ‌Europe," adding the objective of any reforms would be to create "less burdens for businesses." It is unclear what, if any, impact potential U.K. legal changes will have on its data protection and technology rulebooks. The U.K. left the EU in 2020 following Brexit.

LAW & REGULATION—SOUTH AFRICA

South Africa releases draft national AI policy for public comment

South Africa's Minister of Communications and Digital Technologies Solly Malatsi published the country's draft AI policy for a 60-day public comment period, TechCentral reports. Key features include proposals to create a National AI Commission to coordinate policy, an AI Ethics Board to enforce governance around bias and fairness, an AI Regulatory Authority and a National AI Safety Institute. Proposed safety requirements include mandatory human rights and gender impact assessments for AI models, human intervention requirements and obligations surrounding "sufficient explainability" and "sufficient transparency" for high-risk systems used in the public sector.

ENFORCEMENT—U.K.

UK law amendment could hold executives liable for nonconsensual imagery shared on platforms

Under a proposed amendment to the U.K. Crime and Policing Bill, senior executives of technology companies could be held criminally responsible if they do not remove nonconsensual intimate images shared on their platforms, Reuters reports. Executives whose platforms fail to comply with enforcement decisions from the Office of Communications could be subject to imprisonment under the amendment, which is anticipated to be debated in Parliament next week.

CHILDREN'S ONLINE SAFETY—AUSTRALIA

Survey: 61% of Australian minors are still accessing social media

A survey by the Molly Rose Foundation found 61% of users ages 12-15 who had their accounts restricted under Australia's social media ban still have access to their accounts. The report also found 51% of respondents claimed restrictions on social platforms have not improved their online safety. The Molly Rose Foundation said it is urging the U.K. government to "follow the evidence which shows an Australia-style ban won't deliver the immediate improvements in safety that parents and children demand and deserve."

CHILDREN'S ONLINE SAFETY—U.S.

Meta to face Massachusetts attorney general lawsuit over design features

The Massachusetts Supreme Judicial Court ruled Meta must face a lawsuit by Massachusetts Attorney General Andrea Campbell claiming the company's design features for its social media platforms are harmful and addictive for underage users, Reuters reports. Campbell said the court's ruling is a "major step in holding these companies accountable for practices that have fueled the youth mental health crisis and put profits over kids." Meanwhile, the Lowell Sun reports the Massachusetts House advanced a bill to the Senate that introduces social media age restrictions.

LITIGATION & CASE LAW—INDIA

India's Supreme Court seeks government response to petition seeking to overturn portion of DPDPA

India's Supreme Court is awaiting a response from the government to a public interest litigation petition challenging provisions of the Digital Personal Data Protection Act that claims the law weakens portions of the existing 2005 Right to Information Act, the Hindustan Times reports. The petitioners are asking the Supreme Court to strike down Section 44 of the DPDPA because they allege it erodes Section 8 of the RIA and that the DPDPA section in question also violates Articles 14, 19 and 21 of the Constitution of India.

SURVEILLANCE—U.S.

FISA Section 702 renewal deadline looms

U.S. Congress must assess whether to renew Section 702 of the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act, which expires 20 April, as concerns continue over the program's access to individuals' data without a search warrant, The Washington Post reports. Despite U.S. officials' claims that the law expands surveillance powers needed to safeguard national security, lawmakers and civil society groups have warned the program's privacy implications violate constitutional rights. President Donald Trump's administration is lobbying for a "clean" renewal of the program.
