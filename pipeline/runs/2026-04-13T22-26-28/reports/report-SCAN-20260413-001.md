# Alabama Enacts 21st State Comprehensive Privacy Law (HB 351)

## Overview [HIGH confidence]

On April 7, 2026, the Alabama Legislature passed [House Bill 351](https://alison.legislature.state.al.us/files/pdf/SearchableInstruments/2026RS/HB351-eng.pdf), the Alabama Personal Data Protection Act (APDPA), by unanimous votes of 104-0 in the House and 34-0 in the Senate. Once signed by the Governor, Alabama will become the [21st U.S. state](https://privacymatters.dlapiper.com/2026/04/u-s-alabama-becomes-21st-state-to-enact-comprehensive-privacy-law/) to enact a comprehensive consumer data privacy statute. The law takes effect May 1, 2027.

The APDPA substantively follows the Virginia Consumer Data Protection Act (VCDPA) template that has become the dominant model in U.S. state privacy legislation, but it omits several obligations that have become standard in more recent state laws — a notable trend-break in the maturation of the state privacy patchwork.

## Applicability [HIGH confidence]

The APDPA applies to persons conducting business in Alabama, or producing products or services targeted to Alabama residents, that during a calendar year either:

- Control or process the personal data of **more than 25,000 consumers** (excluding data processed solely to complete a payment transaction), **or**
- Derive **more than 25 percent of gross revenue** from the sale of personal data.

As [IAPP notes](https://iapp.org/news/a/alabama-set-to-add-variation-to-us-state-privacy-patchwork), Alabama's 25,000-consumer threshold is among the lowest in the country — lower than the 100,000-consumer threshold used in Colorado, Connecticut, Minnesota, Oregon, and New Jersey, and lower than Delaware and Maryland's 35,000-consumer thresholds. This relatively low bar means the statute will sweep in a broader set of mid-market companies than peer state laws.

## Consumer Rights [HIGH confidence]

The [APDPA grants Alabama consumers](https://privacymatters.dlapiper.com/2026/04/u-s-alabama-becomes-21st-state-to-enact-comprehensive-privacy-law/) the familiar suite of Virginia-model rights:

- **Access / confirmation** — confirm whether a controller processes the consumer's personal data and access that data.
- **Correction** — correct inaccuracies in personal data.
- **Deletion** — delete personal data provided by or obtained about the consumer.
- **Portability** — obtain a portable copy of personal data in a usable format.
- **Opt-out** — opt out of (i) targeted advertising, (ii) the sale of personal data, and (iii) profiling in furtherance of solely automated decisions producing legal or similarly significant effects.

## Departures from the Virginia Model [HIGH confidence]

According to [DLA Piper's Privacy Matters](https://privacymatters.dlapiper.com/2026/04/u-s-alabama-becomes-21st-state-to-enact-comprehensive-privacy-law/) and [Maynard Nexsen's analysis](https://www.maynardnexsen.com/publication-alabama-legislature-passes-comprehensive-data-privacy-bill), the APDPA departs from the Virginia template in four significant respects:

1. **No Data Protection Impact Assessments (DPIAs).** Unlike Virginia, Colorado, Connecticut, and most subsequent state privacy laws, the APDPA does not require controllers to conduct or document DPIAs for higher-risk processing activities (including targeted advertising, sale of personal data, certain profiling, and processing of sensitive data).

2. **Non-sunsetting 45-day cure period.** The AG must provide a 45-day written notice and opportunity to cure before initiating an enforcement action, and this cure right **does not sunset**. Most Virginia-model states originally included cure periods that expired on a set date; Alabama's is permanent.

3. **No universal opt-out / preference signal requirement.** Controllers are not required to recognize browser-based opt-out signals (e.g., Global Privacy Control), unlike Colorado, Connecticut, California, Oregon, Texas, Montana, New Jersey, Delaware, and others.

4. **No private right of action.** Enforcement is exclusive to the Alabama Attorney General. This is consistent with most Virginia-model states but explicitly forecloses consumer litigation.

## Enforcement and Penalties [HIGH confidence]

The [Alabama Attorney General](https://www.billtrack50.com/billdetail/1957511) has **exclusive enforcement authority**. Before bringing an action, the AG must provide the alleged violator with written notice and a 45-day cure opportunity. Civil penalties are capped at **$15,000 per violation**. There is no private right of action.

## AI and Automated Decision-Making [MEDIUM confidence]

As [Privacy Daily reported](https://privacy-daily.com/article/2026/02/25/alabama-house-passes-comprehensive-privacy-bill-with-no-mention-of-ai-2602250016?BC=bc_679b511912c1f), the bill contains no express references to artificial intelligence. Coverage of automated decisions is limited to the standard Virginia-model right to opt out of profiling in furtherance of solely automated decisions producing legal or similarly significant effects, without the more developed ADMT/AI rulemaking frameworks emerging in California, Colorado, and New York.

## Compliance Implications [HIGH confidence]

For organizations already compliant with the VCDPA and peer state laws, incremental build for Alabama should be relatively modest:

- **Update privacy notices** to reference Alabama rights and the AG complaint mechanism.
- **Extend consumer rights request (CRR) intake** to Alabama residents, with verification and 45-day response timeline.
- **Confirm opt-out mechanisms** for targeted advertising, sale, and profiling are exposed to Alabama consumers — even though GPC/universal signals are not mandated, multi-state operators generally will continue to honor them.
- **Data processing agreements** — the APDPA mirrors VCDPA-style controller/processor contracting requirements (processors must be bound by written contracts addressing confidentiality, subcontractor flow-downs, deletion/return, and audit rights).
- **Sensitive data consent** — processing of sensitive data requires opt-in consent (Virginia-model).
- **Revisit applicability analysis** — because of the lower 25,000-consumer threshold, organizations that fell outside VCDPA scope may be in scope for Alabama.

Companies that rely on a "VCDPA-compliant = APDPA-compliant" shortcut can do so with low risk, since the APDPA is functionally a subset of Virginia obligations. The inverse is not true: organizations that built to Alabama alone would fall short of Colorado, California, or Connecticut.
