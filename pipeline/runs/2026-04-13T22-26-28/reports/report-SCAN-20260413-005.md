# Massachusetts SJC Clears AG Campbell's Lawsuit Against Meta Over Addictive Design Features for Minors

## Overview [HIGH confidence]

On April 10, 2026, the Massachusetts Supreme Judicial Court (SJC) issued a first-of-its-kind ruling in *Commonwealth v. Meta Platforms, Inc.* (SJC-13747) holding that Section 230 of the federal Communications Decency Act does not shield Meta from a state consumer-protection lawsuit alleging that Instagram's design features are addictive and harmful to minors. The ruling allows Massachusetts Attorney General Andrea Joy Campbell's 2023 lawsuit to proceed on its merits, preserving both Chapter 93A consumer-protection claims and public-nuisance claims.

Separately, on April 9, 2026, the Massachusetts House of Representatives passed (129-25) a sweeping youth online-safety bill that would prohibit users under 14 from creating social media accounts and require parental consent for 14- and 15-year-olds. The bill now heads to the Senate.

## The SJC Ruling [HIGH confidence]

The decision, authored by Justice Dalila Argaez Wendlandt, affirmed a lower-court ruling denying Meta's motion to dismiss on Section 230 grounds. The SJC is the first state high court to rule that Section 230 does not categorically bar state-law claims premised on an online platform's own design choices — as distinct from claims seeking to impose liability for third-party content.

The court emphasized that the Commonwealth's claims "do not seek to impose liability on Meta for information provided by third parties." The claims allege "harm stemming from Meta's own conduct either by designing a social media platform that capitalizes on the developmental vulnerabilities of children or by affirmatively misleading consumers about the safety of the Instagram platform."

AG Campbell called the ruling a "major step in holding these companies accountable for practices that have fueled the youth mental health crisis."

## AG's Legal Claims [HIGH confidence]

Campbell's complaint, originally filed in 2023 alongside parallel actions by 40+ other state attorneys general, advances two principal theories:

1. **Chapter 93A (Unfair and Deceptive Trade Practices).** [M.G.L. c. 93A, § 2](https://malegislature.gov/Laws/GeneralLaws/PartI/TitleXV/Chapter93a/Section2) prohibits "unfair or deceptive acts or practices in the conduct of any trade or commerce." The AG alleges Meta (a) deployed manipulative design features — push notifications, infinite scroll, "likes," ephemeral/time-limited content, and recommendation algorithms — engineered to exploit developmental vulnerabilities of adolescent users, and (b) affirmatively misrepresented the safety of Instagram and Facebook for minors despite internal research documenting harms.

2. **Public Nuisance.** The Commonwealth asserts that Meta's platform design has materially contributed to the adolescent mental-health crisis, constituting an unreasonable interference with public health and welfare. The SJC allowed this claim to proceed alongside the 93A claim.

The complaint cites Instagram's Massachusetts footprint — over 300,000 daily active users aged 13–17 — as the basis for parens patriae standing.

## Section 230 Defense [HIGH confidence]

Meta argued that Section 230(c)(1) of the Communications Decency Act, 47 U.S.C. § 230, immunized it because the alleged harms ultimately flow from users' consumption of third-party content delivered through the platform. Meta invoked the familiar line of authority treating algorithmic curation and recommendation as protected "publisher" activity.

The SJC rejected this framing. The court held that claims targeting a platform's own features and its own statements about safety do not require the platform to be treated as a "publisher or speaker" of third-party content. Because the duty Meta allegedly breached — not to design a dangerously addictive product; not to misrepresent product safety — is independent of any duty arising from third-party speech, Section 230 does not preempt the Commonwealth's claims.

This is the first state-supreme-court-level holding on this question and reinforces an emerging consensus that Section 230 protects hosting decisions, not product-design and marketing decisions.

## Separate Legislation: Youth Online Safety Bill [HIGH confidence]

On April 8–9, 2026, the Massachusetts House passed a comprehensive youth-technology bill 129–25.

Key provisions:

- **Under 14:** Prohibited from creating or maintaining social media accounts; platforms must delete existing non-compliant accounts.
- **Ages 14–15:** Require verifiable parental consent.
- **16 and older:** No age-based restrictions.
- **School cellphones:** "Bell-to-bell" ban on student cellphone use in public K–12 schools.
- **Effective:** October 2026 if enacted.

The bill's definition of "social media" is reportedly broad enough to potentially sweep in services such as YouTube, Wikipedia, and Roblox. If enacted substantially as passed, Massachusetts would become the first U.S. state with a categorical under-14 social-media ban. Comparable statutes in Florida, Utah, Ohio, and Arkansas have faced First Amendment challenges, and similar litigation is expected here.

## Compliance Implications

- **Design-level product liability risk is live in Massachusetts.** Chapter 93A carries treble damages and attorneys' fees. Expect private 93A demand letters and class actions to follow the AG action.
- **Safety representations are high-risk.** Review marketing, help-center content, parent-facing materials, and investor disclosures concerning youth safety and addictiveness.
- **Internal research on minor harms is central evidence.** Review document-retention and legal-hold posture for internal studies of youth engagement.
- **Age assurance must be operational.** If the House bill becomes law, compliant age-assurance and parental-consent workflows must be in place before October 2026.
- **Cross-state alignment.** 40+ parallel AG actions mean a Massachusetts judgment or settlement will have national reverberations.
