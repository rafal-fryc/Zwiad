Stuff from the States 

 

Age Signals: Many states are actively pursuing and moving age signals laws–most notably App Store Accountability Act frameworks. Key developments include:

  • Alabama - HB 161: Governor Ivey signed this bill into law on February 17. The Act has an effective date of January 1, 2027. The substance of this law largely aligns with the age verification and VPC obligations included in the Texas, Utah, and Louisiana ASAA laws passed last year, but with a few key differences including: extending age verification and parental consent requirements to existing accounts, requiring parental consent for pre-installed apps, removing application exemptions, and adopting a definition for “in-app purchases” clarifying that the term only applies to purchases billed through an app store (e.g., purchases of virtual currencies or digital goods). Notably, the law does not include a private right of action.
  • Maryland - HB 1179: Introduced by Del. Nkongolo (R) on February 11, this bill proposes an App Store Accountability Act-style age assurance framework with a few divergences, including requirements for age verifying existing accounts, parental consent obligations for pre-installed apps, and the absence of application exemptions (a factor in the preliminary injunction analysis against Texas’ law).
  • Kansas - SB 372: This bill was passed out of the Senate Judiciary committee on February 17 with amendments. Committee amendments to the bill include adding in a definition for in-app purchases (limiting the meaning of that term to purchases of digital currency or goods, etc.), clarifying the effective date as January 1, 2027, and removing the proposed private right of action. 
  • Kentucky - HB 632: Introduced by Rep. Dietz (R) on February 12, this bill would establish an App Store Accountability Act framework aligning with the models enacted in Utah, Louisiana, and Texas in 2025.



Safety: There has been a strong surge in online safety proposals across state legislatures this session, with more new bill introductions and legislative movement over the past few weeks. Key developments include:

  • Arizona - HB 4082: Introduced by Rep. Marquez (D) on February 9, this bill would require social media platforms (broadly defined) to restrict minors (u16) from accessing the platform, develop and maintain reasonable safeguards mitigating foreseeable risks of harm on the platform, and publish a written safety and risk mitigation policy that details certain risk information for platform users, as prescribed.
  • Hawaii - SB 2761: This bill, which would ban social media platforms from allowing minors (u16) to create or hold accounts, was reported from the Senate Labor and Technology committee on February 6, then passed second reading before it was referred to the Senate Consumer Protection committee for additional consideration on February 11. 
  • Illinois 
    • HB 5066: Introduced by Rep. Tarver on February 4, this bill would require social media platforms (broadly defined) to implement an age assurance system and restrict children (u16) from creating or holding an account on the platform. The bill provides an exception to that restriction where a child’s parent allows the child to use an account held by the parent or guardian. The bill would allow platforms to enter into agreements with application distributors and device manufacturers to share age data for purposes of compliance with the proposed requirements. 
    • SB 3264: Introduced by Rep. Ventura on February 3, this bill would require social media platforms to establish online safety centers, subject to certain prescribed obligations, and public cyberbullying policies. It would also apply to controllers offering any online service, product or feature to consumers that the controller knows or willfully disregards are minors, and prohibits such controllers from engaging in dark patterns, offering direct messaging features to minors (unless certain safeguards are in place), or using system design features to significantly prolong a minor’s use of the service.   
  • Indiana - SB 199: This bill, sponsored by Sen. Raatz (R), passed the House with amendments on February 17, and now returns to the Senate for consideration. This bill would require platforms to implement age assurance and obtain VPC for minors (u17) to create and hold user accounts, establish certain parental controls and oversight mechanisms, prevent minor access to certain platform features, and conduct ongoing age checks on users after specified periods of platform use. 
  • Kansas - HB 2657: Introduced by the Kansas House Committee on Legislative Modernization on February 3, this bill would require social media platforms to implement age verification and obtain VPC for minors (u16) to create or hold accounts. Platforms would also be required to allow parents to revoke consent for a minor’s use of the platform at any time and request that the minor’s account be deleted.
  • Washington - HB 1834: This bill, which would prohibit providing "addictive feeds" to minors and sending notifications during specific timeframes, passed out of committee and was placed on second reading on February 10. 



Privacy: The most noteworthy update within the last two weeks is that South Carolina enacted its AADC-style law, which took immediate effect. FPF’s Daniel Hales published a new blog and comparison chart breaking down the Act’s novel requirements and significant enforcement approach. Although South Carolina was the first to pass an AADC-style law this year, it is not the only state pursuing such a framework. Many states have set their sights on AADC-inspired proposals establishing enhanced privacy and safety protections for minors (and sometimes even users or consumers more broadly) and regulating online service design. States that have introduced AADC-style frameworks over the past fortnight include: Colorado (HB 1148), Georgia (SB 495), Illinois (SB 3241), Kansas (SB 499), Kentucky (HB 633), New Jersey (S3413/A4015), Rhode Island (SB 2406, HB 762), and Wisconsin (SB 978). For a more comprehensive overview of active youth privacy bills, see FPF’s State Youth Legislation Tracker.

 

Litigation & Enforcement

 

Connecticut - Youth Focus in Annual Enforcement Report: The Connecticut Attorney General’s Office (OAG) released its third-annual CTDPA enforcement report, which emphasized an importance for “protecting kids online.” Related to entities’ minor protection obligations, the OAG sent violation notices to: a popular messaging platform provider concerning deficiencies with privacy notice disclosures and opt-out requirements; and, a gaming studio regarding deficiencies with privacy notice disclosures, consent and opt-out practices, while also noting further investigation into the company’s use of SDKs for targeted advertising. The report also highlighted that the OAG is continuing to investigate a chatbot platform “regarding alleged harm to children,” and notes that additional, standalone legislation specifically addressing the unique harms that chatbots pose to consumers, especially minors, is necessary alongside the existing applicable protections provided under the CTDPA and Connecticut’s unfair and deceptive trade practices act.   

 

South Carolina - AADC-Style Law Challenged:  As expected, NetChoice has filed a challenge to the recently enacted South Carolina Age-Appropriate Code Design Act, seeking declaratory and injunctive relief on First Amendment grounds. NetChoice contends that the law violates the First Amendment in numerous ways. For example, NetChoice alleges that the law’s requirement for covered online services to exercise reasonable care in preventing harm to minors amounts to a content-based restriction on speech that is both over- and underinclusive in its regulation of content. NetChoice also argues that the law is preempted by both Section 230 and COPPA and that it is void for vagueness. In a quick and notable development in this case, Governor McMaster filed a motion to intervene in the lawsuit as a defendant on February 13. Although the precise reasons for the governor’s intervention in this case are still subject to speculation, the move could signal the state’s strong interest in defending the constitutionality of the Act. 

 

Significant Jury Trials Kick-Off in California & New Mexico: Two separate “bellwether” jury trials started last week in California and New Mexico testing the recently emerging and novel legal theories aiming to hold technology companies accountable under existing deceptive trade practice consumer protection laws for alleged harms to minors, “addictive” design practices, deficiencies in platform safeguards, and misrepresentations about platform safety to consumers. If successful, the plaintiffs in both cases seek monetary damages and platform design changes. Both trials are expected to unfold over the course of several weeks–the outcomes of which will serve as an important indicator of this legal theory’s viability in similar suits filed in several states.

 

Texas - Lawsuit Filed Under DTPA & SCOPE Act: Attorney General Paxton filed a lawsuit against Snap on February 11. In the complaint, the AG alleges that the company violated Texas’ Deceptive Trade Practices–Consumer Protection Act (DTPA) by: (1) misrepresenting app age rating information in the App Store, (2) failing to disclose accurate and relevant information about app content and appropriateness to consumers, and (3) failing to disclose that the app is “addictive and contributes to compulsive or excessive use.” The AG further alleges that the company violated the SCOPE Act by: (1) failing to provide a commercially reasonable method for a parent or guardian to verify their identity, (2) sharing, disclosing, and selling known minors’ personal information without obtaining parental consent, and (3) failing to provide verified parents with tools required by the act allowing a parent to “supervise…the known minor’s use of a digital service.” Although some provisions of the SCOPE Act are enjoined, such as the content monitoring and age verification requirements, this lawsuit signals that the AG is still prioritizing enforcement of the provisions that remain in effect as litigation continues.
