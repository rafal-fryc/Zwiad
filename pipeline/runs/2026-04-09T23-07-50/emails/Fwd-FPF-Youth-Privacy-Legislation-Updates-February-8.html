Stuff from the States 

 

Age Signals: Despite the constitutional questions regarding App Store Accountability Act (ASAA) frameworks, reflected in the preliminary injunction against Texas’ law at the end of last year, the states remain very active on age signals proposals. Recent developments include:

  • Alabama:
    • ASAA Bill: The Alabama legislature is on track to quickly pass HB 161, a bill that would establish an App Store Accountability Act-style age assurance framework with a few divergences, including requirements for age verifying existing accounts, parental consent obligations for pre-installed apps, and the absence of application exemptions (a factor in the preliminary injunction analysis against Texas’ law). This bill has been moving quickly–it has already passed the House and is on track to pass in the Senate this week.
    • HB 219/ SB 172: Introduced by Rep. Shaw (R), this bill mirrors the “Parents Over Platforms Act,” requiring application distribution providers to ask account holders to declare their age and provide developers of covered applications the ability to call an age signal. This bill would also require additional age assurance obligations for developers, as specified.
  • Arizona - ASAA Bill: Introduced by Rep. Way (R), this bill proposes an App Store Accountability Act-style age assurance framework with the same divergences as Alabama’s HB 161. 
  • Kansas - ASAA Bill: Introduced by the Senate Judiciary Committee, this bill proposes an App Store Accountability Act-style age assurance framework with the same divergences as Alabama’s HB 161. 
  • Florida - ASAA Bill: Introduced by Sen. Calatayud (R), this bill would establish an App Store Accountability Act framework similar to the models enacted in Utah, Louisiana, and Texas in 2025. Notably, this bill would also diverge from previous models by including specific requirements to age verify and provide consent options for existing accounts and preinstalled apps. Unlike Alabama’s HB 161, this bill would maintain the application exemptions included in the 2025 models.
  • Mississippi - ASAA Bill: Introduced by Rep. Powell (R), this bill would establish an App Store Accountability Act framework aligning with the models enacted in Utah, Louisiana, and Texas in 2025. This bill also includes a separate section that would establish certain obligations for social media providers.
  • Virginia - ASAA Bill: Introduced by Del. Runion (R), this bill would establish an App Store Accountability Act framework aligning with the models enacted in Utah, Louisiana, and Texas in 2025.
  • Wisconsin - ASAA Bill: Introduced by Rep. Goeben (R), this bill proposes an App Store Accountability Act-style age assurance framework with the same divergences as Alabama’s HB 161.

 

Safety:

  • Hawaii
    • HB 2040: Introduced by Rep. Shimizu (R), this bill would establish two separate sections of requirements related to minor online protections. The first section would require social media platforms to implement age verification and obtain parental consent for minors before providing “addictive feeds.” The second section would establish heightened privacy protections for teens (ages 13-18), requiring online operators to obtain teen consent for processing, implement data minimization, and enable privacy defaults, among other requirements.
    • SB 2761: Introduced by Sen. Keohokalole (D), this bill would prohibit social media platforms from allowing known minors to create or hold accounts on the platform. The bill would require social media platforms to take "reasonable steps" to prevent individuals under 16 from holding accounts, such as implementing age screens and terminating accounts and profiles of users once the platform knows the user is under 16. The definition of "social media platform" contains various exceptions.
  • Idaho - H.542: Introduced by the House Committee on State Affairs, this bill would require covered social media providers to implement age verification and obtain parental consent for minors (u16) to create accounts. Covered social media providers would be required to implement certain parental controls, restrict certain platform functions and features by default, and conduct ongoing age estimation checks on users for 7 years after account creation, with age checks triggered after prescribed periods of use.
  • Indiana - HB 1178: Introduced by Rep. King (R), this bill would establish similar requirements as Idaho’s H.542, but diverges by defining “minor” as anyone under 14 and including additional requirements for establishing privacy defaults.
  • Missouri - HB 2392: Introduced by Rep. Murray (D), this bill would require social media platforms to conduct age verification, terminate the accounts of users under 14, allow verified parents or guardians to request deletion of a minor’s (u18) account, and disable addictive or manipulative design features targeting minors, among other restrictions.
  • Mississippi - HB 1576: Introduced by Rep. Hood (R), this bill would create two new sections of code. The first would require "interactive computer services" (which adopts the definition of the term in 47 U.S.C. § 230) to implement age verification and obtain parental consent for a minor (u18) to create and hold an account, and provide a minor's parent with access to the minor's account activity. The second section would require digital service providers (narrowly scoped to social media services, with numerous exceptions) to implement age verification and obtain parental consent for minors (u18), restrict certain data processing activities, and implement strategies to prevent minor’s exposure to “harmful content.”
  • Nebraska - LB 1175: Introduced by Sen. DeBoer, this bill would amend the Parental Rights in Social Media Act enacted in 2025 to add in language stating that a social media platform is considered a "product" for purposes of product liability actions provided under state law.
  • New York - A10008A/S9008A: Included in the budget bill, this proposal would cover operators of online platforms, defined as platforms with a minor audience that allow users to create public or semi-public profiles, communicate with other users, post content viewable by other users, create games or immersive digital environments for other users, and exchange money with digital currency. Covered online platforms would be obligated to conduct age assurance and, in the case of minors, implement specific privacy defaults, disable integrated AI companions by default, and provide parents with tools for limiting transactions on the platform. The bill would also require additional parental consent for certain features in the case of minors under the age of 13.
  • Oklahoma - SB 1871: Introduced by Sen. Jech (R), this bill would require social media companies to freeze all user accounts once this bill is effective and conduct age verification on all platform users in the state before allowing continued account use. If the age verification process determines a user to be a minor, social media companies are required to implement certain privacy defaults, limit daily platform use to 1hr per day, and provide a minor's parent with certain tools and oversight functions for the minor's account. This bill would become effective upon enactment.
  • Oklahoma - SB 1972: Introduced by Sen. Grellner (R), this bill would require "interactive computer services" (which adopts the definition of the term in 47 U.S.C. § 230) to implement age verification and obtain parental consent for a minor (u18) to create and hold an account and prohibit certain activities, such as communications between a minor and adult or access to harmful material, without parental consent.

 

Privacy: Even with a heavier legislative focus on minor online safety this year, several states are still pursuing significant youth privacy proposals, including AADCs, bespoke youth privacy frameworks, and amendments to comprehensive privacy laws. Noteworthy bills include (for more comprehensive overview of active youth privacy bills, see FPF’s State Youth Legislation Tracker):

  • South Carolina - AADC: Picking up where the legislature left off last year with HB 3431–a framework seemingly inspired by the Senate version of KOSA and a blend of state-level design code proposals–the legislature quickly passed this bill with a few amendments. Key changes incorporated just before passage include: adjustments to proposed definitions, such as removal of “child” as a defined term and related references; Nebraska AADC-style requirements for the tools a covered online service would be required to provide users and visitors of the service, and amending the bill’s effective date to be upon the Governor’s approval (rather than 90 days after enactment). The bill is in the ratification process before moving on to Governor McMaster, who will have 5 days to either sign or veto the bill.
  • New York - NYCDPA Amendment: A carryover bill introduced by Sen. Gounardes (D) last year, S4600 would add on to the "Child Data Protection Act" that passed last in 2024, including adding more data management requirements that more closely align with obligations in comprehensive privacy laws and risk assessment requirements reminiscent of AADCs, such as whether policies are written in a way a child could understand. Although this carryover bill has not yet seen any significant movement, Governor Hochul’s recent announcement regarding an online child protection proposal, which builds upon NYCDPA protections among other things, seemingly indicates that this bill may be a legislative priority this year.
  • Virginia - VCDPA Amendments: Virginia introduced several bills to amend the VCDPA this year, including three notable youth-focused proposals:
    • SB 201: This bill would require a controller or processor to obtain verifiable parental consent prior to (1) registering any child (under 18) with the controller's product or service, or (2) collecting, using, or disclosing the child's personal data. The parent or guardian must have the opportunity to consent to the collection and use of the child's data without consenting to disclosure to third parties.
    • SB 232: This bill would amend the VCDPA to add a heightened access right for minors, prohibit controllers from entering into a user agreement with minors unless the controller obtains VPC in one of several enumerated methods (including a catch-all for "any other commercially reasonable method of obtaining consent"), and a new duty towards minors with respect to advertising (disclosure requirements).
    • SB 245: This bill would amend the VCDPA to, among other things, add protections for minors with respect to social media, including preventing social media platforms from using features encouraging excessive use and requiring platforms to use reasonable care to prevent a heightened risk of harm to minors.

 

Federal 

 

FTC Age Assurance Workshop: On Data Privacy Day, January 28, the FTC hosted a workshop on age assurance technologies (video here). Chair Ferguson made notable opening remarks that emphasized the importance of companies adopting age assurance technologies, particularly to make COPPA compliance easier and more cost-effective, rather than finding "innovative ways of breaking the law." He also appeared to indicate that he may aim to address potential conflict between the use of age assurance and COPPA compliance through a non-enforcement policy or updated COPPA rulemaking. Two other key takeaways from the panel include:

  • Many panelists emphasized the importance of risk-based approaches to age assurance that are privacy preserving and technology-neutral.
  • Panelists notably highlighted challenges for implementing widespread age assurance, including differing regulatory approaches, circumvention issues, First Amendment challenges, and potential conflicts and compliance difficulties age assurance might pose for certain privacy protections, such as data minimization practices.
