Stuff from the States 

 

Arizona - ASAA Bill (HB 2920): Introduced by Rep. Way (R), this bill would establish an App Store Accountability Act-style age assurance framework with a few divergences, including requirements for age verifying existing accounts, parental consent obligations for pre-installed apps, and the absence of application exemptions (a factor in the preliminary injunction analysis against Texas’ law).

 

Alabama

  • Biological and Neural Data Bill Introduced (HB 263): Representative Bobbins (R) introduced a bill that would prohibit the disclosure of biological data or neural data without a consumer’s consent. “Biological data” includes data generated by measurement of an individual’s biological, genetic, biochemical, physiological, or neural properties used for identification purposes. 
  • ASAA Bill (HB 161): Just over a week after introduction, the Alabama House unanimously passed its App Store Accountability Act bill on January 22, moving it to the Senate for further consideration.

 

Iowa - Comprehensive Privacy Bill Introduced (HF 2048): Iowa became the second state this month to introduce a comprehensive privacy bill despite already having a comprehensive privacy law in effect. Representative Gearhart’s (R) bill would apply to any company that processes the personal data of at least 5,000 Iowa residents annually. Companies would be required to post privacy notices, obtain consent from individuals prior to processing that individual's personal data, and comply with individual rights of access, correction, and deletion. The bill includes a private right of action. 

 

Illinois

  • Comprehensive Privacy Bill Introduced (SB 2875): Senator Murphy (D) introduced a comprehensive privacy bill on January 16. This is a Minnesota-style bill in that it includes both numerical applicability thresholds and a “small business” exemption, a definition of “specific geolocation data” that is based on decimals of latitude and longitude rather than feet, requires controllers to maintain a data inventory as part of their security obligations, and includes a right to contest adverse profiling decisions. 
  • Genetics Amendments (SB 2799) (SB 2886): Two recently filed bills would amend Illinois’s Genetic Information Privacy Act. One (SB 2799) would limit the use of genetic testing information in insurance. The other (SB 2886) would expand the law’s protections to include “biomarkers” and biomarker testing. "Biomarker" is defined as "a characteristic that is objectively measured and evaluated as an indicator of normal biological processes, pathogenic processes, or pharmacologic responses to a specific therapeutic intervention" and includes "gene mutations or protein expression."

 

Maryland 

  • MODPA FAQs: As states consider whether to replicate Maryland’s substantive data minimization requirement, the Attorney General’s office has provided FAQs on the law. In response to, “How is a business supposed to figure out what is reasonably necessary and proportionate to provide or maintain a specific product or service requested by an individual consumer?” the following advice is offered: “A business can determine what is ‘reasonably necessary and proportionate to provide or maintain a specific product or service’ based on the expectations of the reasonable consumer about how the data that is collected will be used.” For more on Maryland’s unique data minimization requirements and reasonable expectations standards, see FPF’s data minimization white paper from last June. 
  • Governor Backs Data-driven Pricing Bill: Governor Moore (D) recently announced the Protection from Predatory Pricing Act (text currently unavailable) as part of his 2026 legislative agenda. This bill would regulate the use of individualized pricing in grocery stores. Regulation of data-driven pricing—often called “dynamic pricing,” “personalized pricing,” “algorithmic pricing,” or “surveillance pricing”—is a significant, early trend for the 2026 legislative session. FPF’s U.S. Legislation team is currently tracking more than forty data-driven pricing bills, including approximately two dozen bills introduced since December 1. Other recently active data-driven pricing bills include Arizona HB 2489, Hawaii SB 2036, Maryland HB 148, Nebraska LB 1006, Tennessee SB 1807, Virginia SB 615, and Washington HB 2481.

 

Mississippi - Comprehensive Privacy Bill Introduced (HB 1051): Representative Ford (R) introduced a comprehensive privacy bill on January 16. This is a Tennessee-style bill in that it includes heightened applicability thresholds (e.g., a minimum revenue requirement), a broad pseudonymous data carve-out with respect to consumer rights, and a safe harbor for controllers that comply with the NIST privacy framework or a comparable privacy framework. This bill includes a narrow definition of biometric data (excluding both data generated from photos and videos and information irreversibly converted to a mathematical representation). 

 

Nebraska - AADC Amendment (LB 1119): Senator Bosn introduced an amendment to Nebraska’s AADC on January 16. This amendment would broaden the definition of “covered online service,” expand the definition of “covered design features,” restrict covered online services from providing a single setting that easily reduces all default privacy settings and nudges that encourage minors to turn off privacy settings, and require covered online services to provide an accessible tool allowing minors to unpublish or delete their accounts. 

 

New Jersey - NJDPA Amendment Enacted (A5017): Our first enacted bill of the year is a holdover from New Jersey’s 2024–25 legislative session, amending the New Jersey Data Privacy Act. Approved on January 20, the bill will take effect immediately. The amendment is narrow and adds new exemptions for: insurance-support organizations as defined under New Jersey law; information treated like protected health information collected, used, or disclosed by a covered entity or business associate when the information is afforded all the privacy protections and security safeguards of the federal laws and implementing regulations under HIPAA; human subjects research conducted in accordance with relevant guidelines; and national securities associations registered pursuant to section 15A of the Securities Exchange Act of 1934 and applicable regulations. This amendment also expands the definition of deidentified data to include data deidentified in accordance with HIPAA where the recipient is contractually prohibited from attempting to reidentify the data. 

 

New Mexico - Novel Comprehensive Privacy Bill Introduced (SB 53): Introduced this week, the "Community and Health Information Safety and Privacy Act" is a unique comprehensive privacy bill that would regulate the collection and use of personal data by “covered entities” (defined broadly). Covered entities would be required to configure default privacy settings to the most protective by default, provide a privacy notice, comply with consumer privacy rights, and maintain data security. There are distinct default setting requirements for consumers who are minors (based on actual knowledge). There are default prohibitions, subject to exceptions and consent requirements, on profiling, processing personal data beyond what is necessary to provide a requested product or service, geofencing health care or immigration services, and more. This bill includes a PRA.

 

New York - Privacy & Safety Proposals in Budget Bill (S.9008) (A.10008): The Governor’s budget proposal was introduced in the Senate and Assembly this week. The bill includes several sections that would establish online privacy and safety obligations, including:

  • Data Brokers: The Data Broker Accountability Act is modeled on California's Delete Act and borrows CCPA terminology such as "business" and "cross-context behavioral advertising." This bill would establish a data broker registry, require the creation of a mass deletion mechanism, and includes rulemaking authority. The bill only applies to a data broker who is a "business," which either had annual gross revenues exceeding $25M in the prior year, buys/sells/shares the personal information of at least one hundred thousand consumers or households, or derives at least 50% of annual revenues from selling or sharing consumers' personal information. 
  • Safety by Design Act: This proposal would cover operators of online platforms, defined as platforms with a minor audience that allow users to create public or semi-public profiles, communicate with other users, post content viewable by other users, create games or immersive digital environments for other users, and exchange money with digital currency. Covered online platforms would be obligated to conduct age assurance and, in the case of minors, implement specific privacy defaults that restrict unconnected users from viewing the minor’s posts and profile information (configurable by the minor’s parent), disable integrated AI companions by default, and provide parents with tools for limiting transactions on the platform. The bill would also require additional parental consent for certain features in the case of minors under the age of 13. The Attorney General would be charged with rulemaking under these provisions.

 

South Carolina - AADC (HB 3431): On Jan 21, the Senate concurred to the House amendments on the bill and approved the measure for ratification. Once the bill is ratified and transmitted to Governor McMaster, he will have 5 days to sign or veto the bill.

 

Virginia: Another wave of amendments to the Virginia Consumer Data Protection Act were filed in recent days. 

  • Minors (SB 232): This bill would add a heightened access right for minors (u18), prohibit controllers from entering into a user agreement with minors unless the controller obtains verifiable parental consent in a commercially reasonable method, and add new disclosure requirements for advertising directed at minors. This bill would also establish a duty of care for controllers of online services, products, or features to prevent certain harms to minors, including self-harm, bullying and harassment, sexual exploitation, and predatory or deceptive marketing practices among others.
  • Location (SB 338): This bill would prohibit a controller from selling precise geolocation data. 
  • Data-driven Pricing (SB 615): This bill would prohibit a controller or processor from generating a price offered to a consumer through the consumer's online device based on (1) the hardware or hardware state of the online device, (2) the presence or absence of any software on the device, or (3) precise geolocation data when used to set a price based on inferences about the consumer made from the data. Subject to exceptions.
  • Social Media (HB 1368): This bill would add a private right of action against social media platforms, subject to a cure requirement. 

 

Federal Happenings 

 

FPF’s Jim Siegl to Participate in FTC Age Verification Workshop: The Federal Trade Commission has released the agenda for its January 28th workshop on age verification. FPF Senior Fellow Jim Siegl will be speaking on the panel “From Biometrics to Behavioral Signals: Age Verification Tools.” Other panels include “Understanding the Landscape: Why Age Verification Matters,” “Navigating the Regulatory Maze of Age Verification,” and “Deploying Responsible Age Verification at Scale.” Chairman Andrew Ferguson, Commissioner Mark Meador, and Director of the Bureau of Consumer Protection Christopher Mufarrige will deliver remarks.
