Stuff from the States 

 

Alabama - Age Signals: 

  • HB 161: This bill would establish an App Store Accountability Act-style age assurance framework with a few divergences, including requirements for age verifying existing accounts, parental consent obligations for pre-installed apps, and the absence of application exemptions (a factor in the preliminary injunction analysis against Texas’ law).
  • HB 219: This bill mirrors the “Parents Over Platforms Act,” requiring application distribution providers to ask account holders to declare their age and provide developers of covered applications the ability to call an age signal. This bill would also require additional age assurance obligations for developers, as specified. (For more, see FPF’s member briefing here.)

 

Florida - ASAA Bill: Florida joined the growing number of states considering age signal-based age assurance frameworks when Senator Calatayud (R) introduced SB 1722 on January 9. This bill would establish an App Store Accountability Act-style age assurance framework with the same divergences observed in Alabama’s HB 161. 

 

Kentucky - Lawsuit Filed under KCDPA: The Kentucky Attorney General announced an enforcement action against Character.ai, a companion chatbot provider, and its founders. The complaint alleges that the company (1) violated the Kentucky Consumer Protection Act by falsely marketing the product as safe and age-appropriate and deceptively repurposing user data for LLM fine-tuning; (2) violated the Kentucky Consumer Data Protection Act by failing to provide adequate child safeguards,age verification, and parental consent mechanisms that resulted in the unlawful collection of children’s data; and (3) was unjustly enriched as a result of the unlawful conduct (AG acting as parens patriae).  

 

Mississippi - ASAA Bill: This week Rep. Powell (R) introduced HB 708, an App Store Accountability Act framework aligning with the models enacted in Utah, Louisiana, and Texas in 2025. This bill would provide a PRA for harmed minors and their parents resulting from violations of this Act alongside enforcement by the Attorney General. This bill also contains a section with safety-related protections for social media platforms.

 

New Jersey

  • Regulations: Last June, the New Jersey Division of Consumer Affairs released draft regulations implementing the New Jersey Data Privacy Act. The current governor’s administration reportedly missed the last available deadline to finalize those regulations before the new governor takes office. The new administration will therefore decide the regulations’ fate—either adoption, revision, additional public input, or restarting the process.  
  • Amendment Passed on Final Day of 2024-25 Session: The New Jersey legislature’s 2024-25 session ended this week on January 12. On the final day, it appears that a minor amendment to the state’s comprehensive privacy law, A.5017, passed its concurrence. This bill would add new data-level exemptions to the law, including for personal data collected and used for the purpose of detecting/preventing fraud in connection with insurance underwriting or claim activity, information treated like protected health information by a covered entity or business associate under HIPAA, and human subjects research conducted in accordance with relevant guidelines. This bill would also expand the definition of deidentified data to include data deidentified in accordance with HIPAA where the recipient is contractually prohibited from attempting to reidentify the data. A copy of this bill (S.2956) had already been reintroduced for the new legislative session. 
  • New Comprehensive Privacy Bill and Amendments: Although New Jersey already has a comprehensive consumer privacy law, this week a GDPR-style bill (with a lawful basis requirement), S.2602, was introduced. Lawmakers also introduced a proposed amendment to New Jersey’s existing law, S.1354, which would require controllers to deidentify personal data prior to selling it. 

 

South Carolina - AADC: Picking up where the legislature left off last year with HB 3431–a framework seemingly inspired by the Senate version of KOSA–the House voted unanimously to approve and return an amended version of the bill to the Senate for further consideration. Key changes incorporated by the House include: adjustments to proposed definitions such as removal of “child” as a defined term and related references; Nebraska AADC-style requirements for the tools a covered online service would be required to provide users and visitors of the service, and amendments to the bill’s effective date to take effect upon the Governor’s approval rather than 90 days after enactment.

 

Virginia

  • Amendments: A new bill (SB 177) would add a GLBA data-level exemption to the reproductive or sexual health information amendment to Virginia’s consumer protection act passed last year. Three proposed amendments to the Consumer Data Protection Act were also introduced: 
    • SB 201: This bill would require controllers or processors to obtain verifiable parental consent for minors under the age of 18 prior to registering for the product or service or prior to disclosing the minor’s personal information to third parties. 
    • HB 654: This bill would expand the definition of biometric data to include data generated from photographs and recordings, as well as certain HIPAA-covered data and neural data;
    • SB 245: This bill would amend the VCDPA to: increase social media protections for minors, such as preventing them from using features that encourage excessive use;  provide AI-specific obligations, such as requiring controllers and processors of AI systems to exercise "reasonable care" to protect consumers from "known or foreseeable" risks of algorithmic discrimination, and new consent requirements for collecting and using personal data and sharing or selling personal data (these must be separate consents and apply specifically to controllers or processors that operate an AI system); and require registering with the Commonwealth (applicable to controllers and processors that operate a social media platform or an AI systems).
  • ASAA Bill: Del. Runion (R) introduced HB 757, an App Store Accountability Act framework aligning with the models enacted in Utah, Louisiana, and Texas in 2025. This bill would provide a PRA for harmed minors and their parents resulting from violations of this Act alongside enforcement by the Attorney General.
  • Data Brokers: Rounding out a busy week in Virginia, Del. Maldonado (D) introduced a data broker bill this week. Structurally similar to an existing law in Vermont, this bill would prohibit the acquisition of personally identifiable information through fraudulent means or for certain prohibited purposes (e.g., stalking or harassing), require data brokers to maintain a comprehensive information security program, and establish a data broker registry. The annual registration fee would be $100,000. Violations of the law would be subject to "any and all of the enforcement provisions of the Virginia Consumer Protection Act," which includes a private right of action. 

 

Federal Happenings 

 

FCC’s Privacy Penalties Under Scrutiny: The U.S. Supreme Court has taken up a case concerning the Federal Communication Commission’s authority to issue civil penalties for privacy violations, resolving a circuit split. Previously, the 5th Circuit had vacated a privacy fine against AT&T, ruling that the fine deprived the company of its constitutional right to a jury under the 7th Amendment. The 2nd Circuit, by contrast, had upheld a fine against Verizon. 

 

FTC Settles Driving Data Action: The collection and use of driving behavioral data—specifically, the collection and use of precise geolocation data for auto insurance purposes—was a major enforcement trend in 2025. On January 14, the FTC announced a settlement with GM resolving allegations that drivers’ driving data was collected and used without adequate notice and consent. The order’s requirements include: 

  • A five-year prohibition on disclosing covered driver data to consumer reporting agencies; 
  • An obligation to get affirmative express consent from consumers within 180 days for each “separate, unrelated service or feature that collects, uses, or discloses” covered driver data (subject to exceptions); 
  • A prohibition on limiting consumers’ ability to withhold or withdraw consent by degrading the quality of the service (unless such data is “technically necessary” to providing the product or service without the degradation); 
  • Data minimization obligations (i.e., not collecting more covered driver data than reasonably necessary to fulfill the specific purpose); 
  • Adopting and adhering to a retention schedule; 
  • Deleting prior-retained covered driver data (subject to exceptions) within 180 days, with an opportunity to instead obtain affirmative express consent from consumer; 
  • Providing copies of covered driver data and responding to deletion requests submitted by consumers; 
  • Requesting that third parties delete previously shared covered driver data; 
  • Allowing consumers to disable the collection of location data; 
  • Allowing consumers to disable remote data collection through the OnStar program (by declining to enroll or unenroll); and 
  • Not misrepresenting data practices.
