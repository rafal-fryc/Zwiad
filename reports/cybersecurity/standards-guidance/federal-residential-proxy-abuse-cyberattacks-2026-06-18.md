# Residential Proxy Abuse: How Hackers Route Cyberattacks Through Home Networks to Evade Detection

**Jurisdiction:** Federal | **Category:** Cybersecurity | **Date:** 2026-06-18

## Executive Summary [MEDIUM confidence]

Cybersecurity researchers have documented a sharp increase in cyberattack campaigns that route malicious traffic through compromised residential IP addresses and commercial residential proxy networks to evade enterprise security controls and threat intelligence systems. GreyNoise Intelligence's April 2026 report analyzed 4 billion malicious sessions over 90 days and found that 78% of attack traffic originating from residential IP addresses evaded IP reputation checks entirely. Both state-sponsored actors — primarily China-nexus groups — and criminal enterprises have adopted this technique at scale, using it to conduct credential stuffing, account takeover, phishing campaigns, and network intrusions while appearing to originate from ordinary consumer connections. The federal government has responded with a joint advisory from CISA and allied agencies in April 2026, alongside enforcement actions that dismantled major proxy botnet operations including 911 S5 (19 million compromised IPs), Anyproxy/5Socks, and BADBOX 2.0. These developments raise unresolved questions about whether ISPs and device manufacturers face legal or regulatory notification obligations when residential devices are enrolled into proxy networks without owner consent.

## Background [HIGH confidence]

Residential proxies are intermediary services that route internet traffic through IP addresses assigned by ISPs to residential consumers rather than through datacenter infrastructure. Legitimate residential proxy services exist for purposes such as ad verification and market research, but the same technology is widely exploited by threat actors because residential IP addresses carry inherent credibility: they are classified as low-risk by most IP reputation systems, which are tuned to block datacenter and hosting IP ranges.

Threat actors access residential IPs through two primary channels. The first is commercial residential proxy services, which aggregate IP addresses from consumers who have (often unknowingly) consented to sharing bandwidth through software bundles, free VPN applications, or browser extensions. The second channel is involuntary enrollment: malware, backdoored IoT firmware, and compromised home routers conscript devices into botnets that are then leased as proxy infrastructure without any owner knowledge or consent.

The technique is not new, but its scale has grown dramatically. DNS traffic to proxy-related domains grew from approximately 400 billion queries per month in early 2025 to over 500 billion per month by April 2026, according to Infoblox analysis as reported by [SecurityBrief](https://securitybrief.com.au/story/infoblox-finds-residential-proxies-rife-in-business-networks) and [Cybersecurity News](https://cybersecuritynews.com/hackers-abuse-residential-proxy-networks/). The [Verizon 2025 Data Breach Investigations Report](https://www.verizon.com/business/resources/reports/2025-dbir-data-breach-investigations-report.pdf) found that stolen credentials drove 22% of all confirmed breaches and that credential stuffing accounted for a median of 19% of all SSO authentication attempts — a volume that depends heavily on the ability to distribute authentication attempts across large pools of rotating IP addresses.

Law enforcement and regulatory attention has intensified in parallel. The DOJ dismantled the 911 S5 botnet in May 2024 — at the time the largest known residential proxy botnet, with 19 million compromised IPs across 190 countries and confirmed victim losses in the billions of dollars — and the [FBI issued a public service announcement](https://www.ic3.gov/PSA/2024/PSA240529) advising the public of risks from residential proxy services. In May 2025, DOJ unsealed charges against four foreign nationals for operating the Anyproxy and 5Socks botnet services, which had sold proxy access to compromised home routers since at least 2004 and generated over $46 million in revenue.

## Detailed Analysis [MEDIUM confidence]

### How Residential Proxy Abuse Works

Attack traffic routed through residential proxies is functionally indistinguishable from legitimate consumer browsing at the IP address level. Residential IPs rotate faster than threat intelligence feeds can catalog them: [GreyNoise Intelligence's "Invisible Army" report](https://www.greynoise.io/resources/invisible-army-residential-proxy-abuse-report) found that 89.7% of residential IPs observed in malicious activity were active for under one month, with only 8.7% persisting for two months and 1.6% for three months. Each residential IP averaged fewer than three malicious sessions before rotating out.

The GreyNoise dataset — 4.02 billion sessions from 5.72 million unique source IPs observed between November 29, 2025 and February 27, 2026 — found that 39% of unique IPs targeting internet-facing infrastructure came from home internet connections. Of those, 78% were invisible to reputation feeds. Traffic patterns followed human sleep cycles, dropping by approximately one-third overnight, which further obscures the automated nature of the activity.

Attackers use this evasion capability across the full attack chain. Reconnaissance, credential stuffing, account takeover, phishing delivery, malware command-and-control, and data exfiltration have all been observed through residential proxy channels, according to [CISA's April 2026 advisory AA26-113A](https://www.cisa.gov/news-events/cybersecurity-advisories/aa26-113a). [GreyNoise Intelligence's "Invisible Army" report](https://www.greynoise.io/resources/invisible-army-residential-proxy-abuse-report) found that residential attack traffic spanned 683 distinct ISP organizations, with a 46% provider overlap across those networks — meaning that nearly half of proxy IPs span multiple providers simultaneously — a structural feature that defeats ISP-level blocking because no single carrier can stem the volume on its own.

### State-Sponsored Adoption

[CISA Advisory AA26-113A](https://www.cisa.gov/news-events/cybersecurity-advisories/aa26-113a), issued April 23, 2026 in coordination with the UK NCSC and other allied agencies, documents a major shift in China-nexus threat actor TTPs. Over several years, these actors moved away from individually procured infrastructure and toward large-scale networks of compromised devices — primarily SOHO routers, but also IP cameras, NAS devices, firewalls, and other IoT hardware. The advisory states that the majority of tracked China-nexus threat actors are now using these "covert networks" and that multiple covert networks exist, each potentially shared by multiple actor groups.

The advisory identifies specific known covert network infrastructure including the Raptor Train botnet, which consisted of thousands of SOHO routers and IoT devices. These networks enable espionage, offensive operations, and persistent access to US critical infrastructure while attributing attacks to ordinary residential consumers.

### Criminal Ecosystem

The criminal residential proxy market operates as a tiered industry. At the infrastructure layer, botnet operators compromise devices and aggregate IP pools. At the service layer, proxy brokers sell access through subscription services. [Google Threat Intelligence Group disrupted IPIDEA](https://cloud.google.com/blog/topics/threat-intelligence/disrupting-largest-residential-proxy-network) in January 2026, seizing control of domains managing millions of compromised devices across 13 proxy brands. In a single seven-day period in January 2026, over 550 distinct threat groups — including groups tied to China, North Korea, Iran, and Russia — used IPIDEA exit nodes to obfuscate their activities.

Devices are enrolled without owner knowledge through multiple vectors: pre-installed malicious firmware in low-cost IoT hardware purchased through e-commerce platforms, browser extensions and free applications that bundle proxy SDKs, and malware distribution campaigns. [BADBOX 2.0](https://www.humansecurity.com/learn/blog/badbox-2-fbi-psa/), the largest known botnet of internet-connected TV devices, infected over 10 million Android-based devices including smart TVs, digital picture frames, and vehicle infotainment systems. Google filed a federal lawsuit against BADBOX 2.0 operators in July 2025, and the FBI issued a [Public Service Announcement on June 5, 2025](https://www.ic3.gov/PSA/2025/PSA250605) warning consumers about IoT device compromise.

### Why Traditional Defenses Fail

IP reputation systems — the primary technical control most enterprises deploy against bot traffic — operate on the assumption that malicious traffic comes from known-bad infrastructure. Residential proxy abuse inverts this model: the attacking IPs are in the same address space as employees, customers, and partners. The [Help Net Security coverage of GreyNoise findings](https://www.helpnetsecurity.com/2026/04/06/residential-proxy-attack-traffic-ip-reputation-enterprise-security/) summarizes the structural defense gap: residential IPs rotate before they can be cataloged, shared threat intelligence is systematically stale, and blocking residential ISP ranges wholesale would also block legitimate users. Only 0.1% of residential proxy sessions carried exploitation payloads (compared to 1.0% from hosting infrastructure), meaning behavioral analysis must distinguish malicious from benign with a very low base rate of malicious traffic.

### The Legal Gray Area for ISPs and Device Manufacturers

The finding raises unresolved questions about regulatory obligations. The [Computer Fraud and Abuse Act (18 U.S.C. § 1030)](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/R47557) prohibits unauthorized access to protected computers and the creation and use of botnets to commit crimes, but its application to residential proxy operators who argue consumers consented (even unwittingly through fine-print ToS) remains contested. A [DOJ analysis of the CFAA](https://www.justice.gov/jm/jm-9-48000-computer-fraud) notes that while creating and using botnets generally violates the CFAA or other statutes, the legality of selling or renting botnet access is less clearly established.

On the ISP notification side, [FCC data breach notification rules](https://www.fcc.gov/document/fcc-adopts-updated-data-breach-notification-rules-protect-consumers-0) adopted in December 2023 and effective March 2024 require telecommunications carriers to notify customers when their personally identifiable information is breached — but do not specifically address scenarios where the carrier's own network infrastructure is used to unknowingly enroll consumer devices into proxy networks. The [Sixth Circuit upheld these expanded FCC rules](https://www.saul.com/insights/alert/split-sixth-circuit-upholds-fcc-expansion-data-breach-notification-rules) in August 2025 in *Ohio Telecom Association v. FCC*, confirming the obligation to report PII breaches — though on split statutory grounds: the court found the FCC lacked authority under 47 U.S.C. § 222(a) to mandate PII notification, but upheld the requirement under 47 U.S.C. § 201(b), which prohibits unjust or unreasonable practices. Neither this ruling nor the underlying rules directly address residential proxy enrollment as a triggering condition.

Neither CISA Advisory AA26-113A nor the FBI's PSA series establishes mandatory notification obligations for ISPs whose customers' devices are compromised into proxy networks. The advisory framework remains voluntary, creating a gap between the documented scale of the problem and enforceable legal requirements.

## Impact Assessment [MEDIUM confidence]

**Enterprises and Security Operations:** Organizations that rely primarily on IP reputation and IP allowlisting as security controls are materially exposed. The 78% evasion rate documented by GreyNoise means that credential stuffing, account takeover, and reconnaissance activity from residential proxies will largely bypass existing first-line defenses. Security operations teams should expect increased false-negative rates for these attack categories.

**Financial Services, Healthcare, and E-Commerce:** Industries with high-value authentication endpoints are primary targets. Credential stuffing attacks that succeed through residential proxy evasion lead to account takeover, which in regulated industries may trigger breach notification obligations under state data breach laws, HIPAA (for healthcare), or the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act (for financial services), even though the attacker's use of residential proxies was itself the technical obstacle to prevention.

**ISPs and Network Operators:** ISPs face increasing pressure to develop detection capabilities for residential proxy enrollment of customer devices. While current FCC rules do not mandate notification when devices are enrolled in proxy botnets, the trajectory of regulatory attention — evidenced by repeated FBI PSAs, CISA advisories, and DOJ enforcement actions — suggests this gap may be addressed in future rulemaking.

**IoT Device Manufacturers:** The BADBOX 2.0 case and Google's resulting lawsuit illustrate that device manufacturers who ship products with pre-installed malicious firmware or inadequate security controls enabling proxy enrollment face both civil litigation and reputational risk. No federal IoT security mandate currently requires manufacturers to prevent proxy enrollment specifically, though NIST's IoT cybersecurity guidance and the Cyber Trust Mark program address baseline device security standards.

**State-Sponsored Targeting:** Critical infrastructure operators, defense contractors, and government agencies face the highest residual risk from the state-sponsored channel. CISA's advisory identifies the full attack chain — reconnaissance through data exfiltration — as conducted through covert networks, meaning perimeter defenses that rely on source IP attribution are systematically undermined.

**Compliance Implications:** The absence of a specific regulatory framework for residential proxy compromise creates ambiguity for organizations seeking to determine notification obligations when they discover their services were targeted through this vector. Legal counsel should evaluate whether successful account takeovers routed through residential proxies trigger breach notification requirements under applicable state laws and sector-specific federal regulations, regardless of the evasion technique used.

## Action Items

- **Replace or supplement IP reputation controls:** Shift from IP-centric blocking to behavioral detection — sequential probing patterns, request frequency by device fingerprint, and anomalous authentication timing — as recommended by GreyNoise's analysis of the 4 billion session dataset.
- **Audit authentication endpoints for credential stuffing exposure:** Evaluate current rate-limiting and account lockout logic against the scenario where each residential IP contributes fewer than three authentication attempts (the GreyNoise median), ensuring velocity controls account for distributed low-rate attacks.
- **Implement CISA AA26-113A defensive recommendations:** Map and baseline all network edge devices, maintain robust log collection sufficient to detect low-frequency access patterns, and require multi-factor authentication for all remote access — the advisory's core mitigations for covert-network-based intrusions.
- **Assess IoT device inventory:** Apply the FBI's BADBOX 2.0 guidance by auditing organization-managed and BYOD IoT devices for signs of proxy enrollment; remove and replace devices with known-compromised firmware lineages.
- **Evaluate regulatory notification obligations:** Confirm with legal counsel whether account takeovers that succeed via residential proxy evasion trigger breach notification under applicable state breach laws, HIPAA, GLBA, or other sector requirements.
- **Monitor CISA and FBI for updated residential proxy guidance:** Regulatory frameworks for ISP notification obligations and device manufacturer liability in proxy enrollment scenarios are in early formation; monitor for FCC rulemaking or legislative proposals that may create new mandatory obligations.
- **Review third-party vendor SDKs and bundled software:** Audit software distribution chains for inclusion of residential proxy SDKs (as seen in IPIDEA's distribution model) to avoid inadvertently enrolling end-user devices through enterprise software.
