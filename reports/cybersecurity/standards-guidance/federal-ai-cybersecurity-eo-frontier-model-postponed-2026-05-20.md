# White House AI Cybersecurity Executive Order: Draft Provisions and Postponement

**Jurisdiction:** Federal | **Category:** Cybersecurity | **Date:** May 20, 2026

## Summary [HIGH confidence]

The White House prepared a seven-page executive order establishing a voluntary pre-deployment review regime for "covered frontier models" and a suite of cybersecurity enhancements for critical sectors including healthcare and banking, with a scheduled signing ceremony on May 21, 2026. Hours before the ceremony, President Trump postponed the order after direct calls from tech industry leaders including Elon Musk, Mark Zuckerberg, and White House AI and crypto advisor David Sacks, who warned the framework would function as a de facto licensing regime and slow US AI development. The order has not been rescheduled as of June 1, 2026, and its future status remains uncertain.

## Key Facts [HIGH confidence]

- The draft executive order was divided into two sections: **"Cybersecurity"** and **"Covered Frontier Models"**, according to [Gizmodo's review of the draft text](https://gizmodo.com/heres-the-executive-order-on-ai-that-gave-trump-cold-feet-2000762751).
- Under the frontier model section, the [Treasury Secretary, NSA Director (through the Secretary of Defense), and CISA Director (through the Secretary of Homeland Security) would have had 60 days to develop a classified benchmarking process](https://ppc.land/trumps-ai-cybersecurity-order-what-the-shelved-draft-actually-says/) to assess AI models' advanced cyber capabilities and define the "covered frontier model" threshold.
- AI developers could voluntarily submit models to the US government for a review period of **up to 90 days before public release**; early access would also be extended to critical infrastructure providers in banking and healthcare, per [Axios reporting on the draft](https://www.axios.com/2026/05/20/ai-trump-executive-order-white-house-infighting).
- The draft contained an explicit anti-mandating clause: ["Nothing in this section shall be construed to authorize the creation of a mandatory governmental licensing, preclearance, or permitting requirement"](https://gizmodo.com/heres-the-executive-order-on-ai-that-gave-trump-cold-feet-2000762751) for AI models.
- The cybersecurity section aimed to secure Pentagon and national security agencies, boost federal cyber hiring, harden systems at hospitals and banks, and encourage threat-sharing about AI-related breaches between industry and government, per [Federal News Network](https://federalnewsnetwork.com/artificial-intelligence/2026/05/wh-studying-ai-security-executive-order/).
- The draft directed the Attorney General to prioritize federal criminal enforcement against anyone using AI to illegally access computer systems or engage in AI-assisted cybercrime, per [Gizmodo's draft review](https://gizmodo.com/heres-the-executive-order-on-ai-that-gave-trump-cold-feet-2000762751).
- President Trump [told reporters he postponed the order](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/05/21/trump-ai-executive-order-postponed.html) because "I didn't like certain aspects of it," and stated he did not want to impede the US lead over China: "we're leading China, we're leading everybody, and I didn't want to do anything to get in the way of that lead."
- David Sacks called Trump directly the morning of May 21 to argue that the voluntary vetting would [act as a de facto licensing regime](https://www.axios.com/2026/05/21/trump-ai-executive-order-postponed-why) and that a future administration could easily convert it to mandatory.
- [Elon Musk and Mark Zuckerberg also lobbied Trump](https://the-decoder.com/trump-pulls-ai-safety-order-after-last-minute-calls-from-musk-zuckerberg-and-sacks/) against signing the order, warning it would slow AI development.
- Axios published the [full text of the shelved draft](https://www.axios.com/2026/05/22/ai-executive-order-cancelled-white-house) on May 22, 2026, following its cancellation.
- [Lawfare described the shelved order](https://www.lawfaremedia.org/article/ai-governance-by-phone-call) as "about as mild an intervention as a serious government response to frontier-model risk could be," and called the episode a signal that AI governance stability must come from Congress rather than the executive branch.

## Background: The Draft's Provenance [HIGH confidence]

The proposed order emerged directly from the shock of [Anthropic's Claude Mythos Preview](https://www.anthropic.com/glasswing), released April 7, 2026, which demonstrated autonomous discovery and exploitation of thousands of zero-day vulnerabilities across major operating systems and critical software. Following Mythos, the White House Office of the National Cyber Director (ONCD) convened two industry meetings in late April 2026 and sent a questionnaire to major technology companies asking about AI-enhanced defensive capabilities and critical infrastructure exposure.

By early May, National Economic Council Director Kevin Hassett publicly confirmed the White House was [studying an executive order modeled on FDA drug approval](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-05-06/white-house-preps-order-to-boost-ai-security-hassett-says), with AI models required to demonstrate safety before release. The proposed order was also consistent with NIST's Center for AI Standards and Innovation (CAISI) voluntary pre-deployment evaluation program, which as of May 2026 covered all five major US AI laboratories.

The order's postponement represents a notable policy reversal within the Trump administration's own deliberations — the administration had in January 2025 revoked the Biden AI executive order establishing AI risk-management requirements, only to spend the subsequent months developing a replacement framework that it ultimately declined to sign.

## Draft Provisions in Detail [MEDIUM confidence]

### Covered Frontier Models Framework

The draft established a classification category — "covered frontier model" — defined by a capability threshold to be determined via classified benchmarking. The process was:

1. **Threshold-setting (60 days):** Treasury, NSA (via DoD), and CISA (via DHS) jointly develop a classified benchmarking process assessing AI models' advanced cyber capabilities. This process defines the designation threshold.
2. **Voluntary engagement:** Developers whose models potentially meet the threshold could voluntarily engage the US government to determine whether their model qualifies as a "covered frontier model."
3. **Pre-release access window (up to 90 days):** Qualifying developers could provide the federal government access to covered models for up to 90 days before planned public release, enabling government security review.
4. **Critical infrastructure access:** Beyond the government, early access would extend to trusted critical infrastructure providers in banking and healthcare.
5. **Anti-mandate clause:** Explicit text prohibited construing the framework as authorizing a mandatory licensing, preclearance, or permitting regime.

Sources for the above: [ppc.land draft analysis](https://ppc.land/trumps-ai-cybersecurity-order-what-the-shelved-draft-actually-says/), [Gizmodo draft review](https://gizmodo.com/heres-the-executive-order-on-ai-that-gave-trump-cold-feet-2000762751), [Washington Times analysis](https://www.washingtontimes.com/news/2026/may/22/heres-donald-trumps-postponed-ai-executive-order-would-done/).

### Cybersecurity Section

The cybersecurity portion of the draft addressed:
- Hardening Pentagon and other national security agency networks against AI-enabled threats.
- Boosting federal cyber workforce hiring.
- Shoring up cybersecurity infrastructure at hospitals, banks, and other critical sector organizations.
- Mandating (or encouraging) threat-sharing about AI-related breaches between industry and the government.
- Prioritizing criminal enforcement by the Attorney General against AI-facilitated unauthorized computer access.

Official text could not be retrieved directly from whitehouse.gov; the above is based on secondary reporting and draft analysis. This section is tagged MEDIUM confidence accordingly.

## Why the Order Was Pulled [HIGH confidence]

Multiple converging factors led to the postponement, according to [The Hill](https://thehill.com/policy/technology/5891923-trump-ai-order-scrapped-divide/), [Fortune](https://fortune.com/2026/05/22/tech-billionaires-convince-trump-to-back-off-ai-executive-order-but-much-of-maga-favors-ai-regulation/), and [Axios](https://www.axios.com/2026/05/21/trump-ai-executive-order-postponed-why):

1. **Trump's anti-regulation instinct:** Sources described Trump as fundamentally opposed to regulatory frameworks, noting he "just hates regulation" and viewed even a voluntary pre-review regime as unnecessary.
2. **David Sacks' direct intervention:** Sacks, the White House AI and crypto advisor, called Trump directly the morning of May 21. His core argument was that a voluntary government review would function as a de facto licensing regime with chilling effects on AI model release cadence, and that a future administration could convert it to a mandatory requirement.
3. **Tech industry lobbying:** Musk, Zuckerberg, and Sacks all personally lobbied Trump against signing. According to [Silicon Angle](https://siliconangle.com/2026/05/22/trump-delays-ai-executive-order-tech-industry-pushback/), industry representatives argued the review system would slow development and disadvantage the US in the AI race with China.
4. **China competition framing:** Trump repeatedly invoked US leadership over China as justification, framing any regulation — even voluntary — as a threat to that lead.
5. **Potential photo-op logistics:** Some reports (via Politico, cited in [Gizmodo](https://gizmodo.com/heres-the-executive-order-on-ai-that-gave-trump-cold-feet-2000762751)) noted that major AI CEOs could not attend the signing ceremony and would have sent lower-level executives, which may have also factored into Trump's decision.

## Current Status and Forward Outlook [MEDIUM confidence]

As of June 1, 2026, the executive order has not been rescheduled or revised. The [White House Office of the National Cyber Director](https://federalnewsnetwork.com/artificial-intelligence/2026/05/wh-studying-ai-security-executive-order/) indicated it is continuing to work on AI security initiatives, but no specific alternative timeline or approach has been announced.

[Lawfare's analysis](https://www.lawfaremedia.org/article/ai-governance-by-phone-call) characterized the episode as illustrating the fragility of executive branch AI governance: the administration's AI policy responded primarily to whoever had Trump's ear last. The authors argue that durable AI governance requires Congressional action rather than executive orders susceptible to last-minute reversal by phone calls from tech industry figures.

MAGA-aligned voices reportedly favor some form of AI regulation, creating a potential political constituency for a revised order, per [Fortune's reporting](https://fortune.com/2026/05/22/tech-billionaires-convince-trump-to-back-off-ai-executive-order-but-much-of-maga-favors-ai-regulation/). The existing NIST CAISI voluntary evaluation program — covering all five major AI labs — continues to operate and represents the current default framework for pre-deployment AI model security review.

## Action Items

- **Monitor for rescheduling.** The executive order could be revised and re-signed at any time. Organizations developing or planning to deploy frontier AI models should track White House communications weekly. Any formal announcement would come directly from President Trump.
- **Treat the shelved draft as a planning document.** Although unsigned, the draft's provisions — particularly the 90-day voluntary access window and "covered frontier model" classification — represent the administration's working model of what AI pre-deployment review would look like. Internal compliance planning should use the draft as a reference.
- **Assess CAISI program relevance.** The voluntary NIST CAISI pre-deployment evaluation program currently covers all five major US AI labs and is the operative framework in the absence of an executive order. Organizations developing frontier AI models should evaluate whether CAISI participation is appropriate.
- **Evaluate cybersecurity section applicability.** The cybersecurity section's threat-sharing and hardening provisions for healthcare and banking organizations reflect enforcement priorities that may materialize in regulatory guidance or sector-specific rules independent of the overall executive order. Compliance officers in those sectors should assess current posture.
- **Watch Congressional activity.** Given Lawfare's assessment that executive branch AI governance instability points to Congress as the necessary source of durable rules, track pending federal AI legislation for potential movement. The [bipartisan House AI preemption bill](https://www.axios.com/2026/05/19/house-bipartisan-ai-preemption-bill) introduced in May 2026 is one such vehicle.
- **Engage trade associations.** The ONCD has historically used industry associations as a channel for policy development input. Participation is the most direct way to shape any revised executive order before it is finalized.
