# State Attorneys General Pivot Toward Biometric Data, Deceptive Practices, and Critical Infrastructure in Cybersecurity Enforcement

**Jurisdiction:** Multi-state | **Category:** Cybersecurity | **Date:** July 22, 2026

## Executive Summary [LOW confidence]

An IAPP Daily Dashboard item published July 22, 2026 summarizes commentary from James Dempsey, Managing Director of the IAPP Cybersecurity Law Center, on the state of U.S. state-level cybersecurity enforcement against technology companies and social platforms. According to the summary, Dempsey describes a shift in state attorneys general enforcement priorities toward biometric data, deceptive trade practices (including "dark patterns" and misleading consent disclosures), and critical infrastructure risk, and predicts continued growth in both enforcement actions and new state legislation. This is a trend-and-commentary piece rather than a discrete new legal development: it does not announce a new statute, rule, or enforcement action. The underlying trend, however, is independently corroborated by a substantial and growing body of state AG enforcement activity already documented in this knowledge base and by contemporaneous law-firm commentary, including the Texas AG's biometric settlements with Meta and Google, the Texas AG's deceptive-practices suit against Netflix, the 42-state 23andMe genetic-data settlement, and a wave of new state genetic-data security statutes enacted in 2025-2026. This report catalogs the corroborating record and flags where Dempsey's specific framing could not be independently verified. The direct attribution of specific framing and predictions to Dempsey rests entirely on the unfetchable IAPP article; this report independently confirms Dempsey's role and the general subject matter via secondary sources (see Background section) but cannot confirm the exact quotes or predictions attributed to him.

## Background [MEDIUM confidence]

Jim Dempsey has served as Managing Director of the [IAPP Cybersecurity Law Center](https://iapp.org/about/cybersecurity-law-center/) and has published recurring analysis on U.S. cybersecurity law and policy trends, including a companion piece, "[Major trends in U.S. cybersecurity law and policy](https://iapp.org/news/a/major-trends-in-u-s-cybersecurity-law-and-policy)," and a discussion piece with former DOJ National Security Division head John Carlin, "[Top trends in cybersecurity: A discussion with James Dempsey and John Carlin](https://iapp.org/news/a/top-trends-in-cybersecurity-a-discussion-with-james-dempsey-and-john-carlin)." These IAPP pieces have consistently tracked the expansion of state attorney general authority to police data security practices, largely because most state comprehensive privacy statutes (19+ states as of mid-2026) assign enforcement authority to the state AG, and several states — including California, Connecticut, New Hampshire, New Jersey, Oregon, Texas, and Virginia — have created dedicated privacy/data-security enforcement subunits within their AG offices.

Separately, the trade publication ION Analytics / Cybersecurity and AI Law Report published a closely related survey, "[State Cybersecurity Laws: Enforcers' Growing Toolkit](https://ionanalytics.com/insights/cybersecurity-and-ai-law-report/state-cybersecurity-laws-enforcers-growing-toolkit/)" (also mirrored at [cslawreport.com](https://www.cslawreport.com/21477186/state-cybersecurity-laws-enforcers-growing-toolkit.thtml)), describing the same broad theme: state AGs increasingly combining consumer-protection statutes, sector-specific data-security laws, and newly enacted biometric/genetic-data statutes into an expanding enforcement "toolkit."

## Detailed Analysis [HIGH confidence]

### Biometric data enforcement

The clearest, best-documented example of the shift toward biometric-data enforcement is the Texas Attorney General's use of the [Texas Capture or Use of Biometric Identifier Act (CUBI)](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/Docs/BC/htm/BC.503.htm), Tex. Bus. & Com. Code Chapter 503. Texas secured a **$1.4 billion settlement with Meta** in July 2024 over unauthorized facial-geometry capture — the largest privacy enforcement recovery ever obtained by a single state AG — and a **$1.375 billion settlement with Google** in 2025 over geolocation, browsing, and biometric data collection ([Zwiad report: Texas AG Biometric CUBI Settlement](reports/privacy/enforcement-actions/texas-meta-biometric-cubi-settlement-2024-07-30.md); corroborated by [ION Analytics](https://ionanalytics.com/insights/cybersecurity-and-ai-law-report/state-cybersecurity-laws-enforcers-growing-toolkit/)). More recently, Texas has extended biometric enforcement theories to smart-TV Automated Content Recognition (ACR) technology, obtaining a settlement with LG in May 2026, and is separately pursuing Meta over its smart-glasses biometric data practices ([Zwiad report: Texas AG v. LG/Netflix](reports/privacy/enforcement-actions/texas-lg-netflix-acr-biometric-2026-05-12.md); [Zwiad report: Texas Meta Glasses Biometric Investigation](reports/privacy/enforcement-actions/texas-meta-glasses-biometric-investigation-2026-05-21.md)). The same month as the LG settlement, Texas also sued Netflix, but that suit rests on a legally distinct theory — not ACR technology or CUBI biometric-identifier liability, but general behavioral-surveillance and dark-patterns conduct under the Texas Deceptive Trade Practices Act (DTPA) and Texas Data Privacy and Security Act (TDPSA); see the Deceptive practices and dark patterns subsection below for details. Separately, the Utah Attorney General's Division of Consumer Protection sued Snap Inc. on June 30, 2025, alleging that Snapchat's "My AI" chatbot feature collected geolocation data and biometric/voice data without adequate notice or an opt-out mechanism, in violation of the Utah Consumer Privacy Act — internal Snap documents cited in the complaint reportedly show the chatbot was instructed that "if Ghost Mode is on, you still know their location" ([Utah Department of Commerce press release](https://commerce.utah.gov/2025/06/30/utah-sues-snapchat-for-unleashing-experimental-ai-technology-on-young-users-while-misrepresenting-the-safety-of-the-platform/)). This is a single-state action rather than the coordinated multistate campaign Texas has pursued, and this Zwiad knowledge base does not yet carry a dedicated report on it, but it illustrates the same biometric-adjacent enforcement theory extending to social/AI-chatbot platforms.

### Deceptive practices and dark patterns

State AGs have increasingly framed data-security and privacy failures as *deceptive trade practices* rather than relying solely on breach-notification or sector-specific statutes. Texas AG Ken Paxton's May 2026 suit against Netflix invoked the Texas Deceptive Trade Practices Act (DTPA) alongside the Texas Data Privacy and Security Act, alleging the company operated a covert surveillance apparatus through misleading disclosures and dark patterns ([Zwiad report](reports/privacy/enforcement-actions/texas-lg-netflix-acr-biometric-2026-05-12.md)). This "covert surveillance apparatus" characterization is drawn from the cited internal Zwiad source and is consistent with secondary coverage describing a "covert surveillance dragnet" / "massive surveillance program"; the exact verbatim phrasing could not be confirmed against the Texas OAG's original press release or complaint text because that primary source could not be fetched in this session, so it should not be read as a confirmed direct quotation. The California Privacy Protection Agency has likewise brought administrative dark-patterns and deceptive-design enforcement actions, per secondary reporting from [Bloomberg Law](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/legal-exchange-insights-and-commentary/california-texas-are-leading-2026-privacy-enforcement-efforts) and law-firm client alerts from [Holland & Knight](https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/06/state-attorneys-general-signal-aggressive-enforcement-agenda), which reported that state AGs at NAAG's **May 2026** Spring Consumer Protection Conference (held May 19-21, 2026 in Kansas City, MO, per [NAAG's own event listing](https://www.naag.org/event/2026-spring-consumer-protection-conference/) and law-firm recaps including [Kelley Drye's conference summary](https://www.jdsupra.com/legalnews/2026-naag-spring-consumer-protection-1672521/)) signaled continued aggressive enforcement, including coordination across state lines and shared investigative resources. Holland & Knight's client alert covering the conference was published in June 2026, but the conference itself occurred in May 2026.

### Critical infrastructure and genetic data

Dempsey's reported focus on "critical infrastructure risks" aligns with two independently verifiable developments. First, at the federal level, CISA's forthcoming final rule implementing the [Cyber Incident Reporting for Critical Infrastructure Act of 2022 (CIRCIA)](https://www.cisa.gov/circia) — which will require covered critical-infrastructure owners and operators to report cyber incidents within 72 hours and ransomware payments within 24 hours — was cited by Holland & Knight as a 2026 development affecting infrastructure, energy, and cloud operators ([Holland & Knight](https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/06/state-attorneys-general-signal-aggressive-enforcement-agenda)).

Second, and more directly tied to state action, at least **seven states** enacted new genetic-data security statutes in 2025-2026, reflecting both cybersecurity and "foreign adversary" data-localization concerns. Connecticut (SB 4, effective October 1, 2026), South Dakota (SB 49, effective July 1, 2026), and Indiana (HB 1521, enacted May 2025) apply security-program, informed-consent, and anti-discrimination requirements specifically to direct-to-consumer genetic testing companies ([Covington Inside Privacy](https://www.insideprivacy.com/health-privacy/utah-and-south-dakota-enact-genetic-privacy-laws-as-other-states-advance-bills/); [Indiana House Republicans, "Governor signs bill to protect consumer genetic data"](https://www.indianahouserepublicans.com/news/press-releases/rep.-pierce-governor-signs-bill-to-protect-consumer-genetic-data/)). Meanwhile Louisiana (Human Genomic Security Act of 2025), Texas (HB 130, the Texas Genomic Act), Florida (SB 768, prohibiting public-health laboratories from using genetic-sequencing software produced in or by a designated "foreign country of concern," effective July 1, 2025), and Utah (HB 182) impose broader genetic-data foreign-adversary restrictions ([Covington Inside Privacy](https://www.insideprivacy.com/health-privacy/utah-and-south-dakota-enact-genetic-privacy-laws-as-other-states-advance-bills/); [ION Analytics](https://ionanalytics.com/insights/cybersecurity-and-ai-law-report/state-cybersecurity-laws-enforcers-growing-toolkit/); [Florida SB 768 enrolled bill text](https://www.flsenate.gov/Session/Bill/2025/768/BillText/er/HTML)). Maryland is not included in this 2025-2026 count: its direct-to-consumer genetic-testing security-program statute, the Genetic Information Privacy Act, took effect October 1, 2022 — predating this wave by several years ([Maryland General Assembly, Ch. 501 (2022 H.B. 866)](https://mgaleg.maryland.gov/2022RS/chapters_noln/Ch_501_hb0866E.pdf)). Maryland's 2025-2026 privacy legislative activity (the Maryland Online Data Privacy Act framework and a 2026 Data Privacy and Protection Act) addresses sensitive personal data more broadly and is not a new DTC-genetic-testing-specific security statute, so it is not counted as part of this wave. This new statutory wave was punctuated by the **42-state, $150 million bankruptcy settlement with 23andMe** over its 2023 genetic-data breach, announced July 14-15, 2026 ([Zwiad report](reports/cybersecurity/enforcement-actions/multistate-23andme-ag-settlement-2026-07-15.md)), which state AGs have cited as a template for future genetic-data enforcement.

### Biometric, deceptive-practices, and Texas enforcement facts [HIGH confidence]

The biometric-enforcement and deceptive-practices claims in the preceding two subsections (Meta's $1.4B CUBI settlement, Google's $1.375B settlement, the LG ACR settlement, the pending Netflix DTPA/TDPSA suit and its "covert surveillance apparatus" characterization, and the Meta smart-glasses CID) were each cross-checked against their cited internal Zwiad reports and independently corroborated via web search (dates, dollar figures, and statutory citations all match); the exception is the exact "covert surveillance apparatus" phrase, which is corroborated only as a characterization, not confirmed as a verbatim quotation from the primary Texas OAG source (see Deceptive practices subsection above).

## Impact Assessment [MEDIUM confidence]

Companies collecting biometric identifiers (facial geometry, voiceprints, fingerprints), operating consumer-facing platforms with behavioral tracking or ACR-style technology, or handling direct-to-consumer genetic/genomic data face a materially elevated enforcement risk profile from state AGs, independent of any federal cybersecurity rulemaking. The practical consequence of the "deceptive practices" framing is that companies cannot rely on technical compliance with a specific biometric or genetic-data statute alone; AGs are increasingly examining whether public-facing privacy representations, consent flows, and UX design (dark patterns) match actual data practices. Entities designated or plausibly captured as "critical infrastructure" under CIRCIA should also anticipate parallel state-level scrutiny layered on top of the federal 72-hour/24-hour reporting regime once CISA's final rule takes effect. Because this finding is a commentary/survey piece rather than a rule or enforcement action, there are no new compliance deadlines directly triggered by it; the impact is directional (enforcement-risk trend) rather than a specific new legal obligation.

## Action Items

- Compliance and privacy teams should confirm biometric-data collection practices (facial recognition, ACR/smart-device data capture, voiceprints, and AI-chatbot voice/location data as in the Utah AG's Snap "My AI" action) have documented, informed opt-in consent mechanisms, given the CUBI precedent and its multibillion-dollar settlement exposure.
- Legal teams should audit public-facing privacy disclosures and consent UX against DTPA/UDAP-style "deceptive practices" theories, not just compliance with the specific state comprehensive privacy or biometric statute — this is a distinct and separate enforcement theory now in active use, as illustrated by the Texas v. Netflix suit, which is legally independent of the ACR/CUBI biometric theory used against LG.
- Companies handling direct-to-consumer genetic/genomic data should map exposure across the growing patchwork of state genetic-data security statutes (CT, SD, IN, LA, TX, FL, UT, and Maryland's earlier 2022 Genetic Information Privacy Act, among others), including any foreign-adversary software/vendor restrictions.
- Entities potentially covered by CIRCIA should track CISA's final rule for the 72-hour/24-hour incident and ransomware-payment reporting triggers.
- Monitor for the original IAPP article (expected at [iapp.org/news/a/major-trends-in-u-s-cybersecurity-law-and-policy](https://iapp.org/news/a/major-trends-in-u-s-cybersecurity-law-and-policy)) to confirm Dempsey's exact framing and any additional specifics once direct access is available.
