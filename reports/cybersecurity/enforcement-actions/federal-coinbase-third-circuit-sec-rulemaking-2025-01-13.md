# Coinbase Wins Partial Victory: Third Circuit Orders SEC to Justify Refusal to Promulgate Crypto Rules

**Jurisdiction:** Federal (3d Cir., S.D.N.Y.) | **Category:** Cybersecurity / Securities Enforcement | **Date:** January 13, 2025

> **Note on Categorization:** This finding was tagged "privacy" and jurisdictioned to "New York" in the pipeline. The subject matter — federal circuit court review of SEC rulemaking denial for digital assets — is a federal securities and administrative law development. It is categorized here under "cybersecurity" as the closest available project category, consistent with the companion *SEC v. Coinbase* SDNY report already in this directory. The primary venue is the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Third Circuit; the parallel SDNY stay is a distinct proceeding.

## Executive Summary [HIGH confidence]

On January 13, 2025, the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Third Circuit issued a [precedential opinion](https://www2.ca3.uscourts.gov/opinarch/233202p.pdf) in *Coinbase, Inc. v. Securities and Exchange Commission*, No. 23-3202, ruling that the SEC's one-paragraph denial of Coinbase's 2022 petition for crypto-specific rulemaking was "conclusory and insufficiently reasoned, and thus arbitrary and capricious" under the Administrative Procedure Act (APA). The court granted Coinbase's petition in part and remanded the matter to the SEC for a more complete explanation of its refusal to engage in notice-and-comment rulemaking on the application of federal securities laws to digital assets. The court stopped short of ordering the SEC to actually initiate rulemaking. Simultaneously, on January 7, 2025, Judge Katherine Polk Failla of the S.D.N.Y. certified a controlling question of law for interlocutory review by the Second Circuit in the companion civil enforcement action *SEC v. Coinbase*, staying that litigation. These twin January 2025 rulings placed maximum judicial pressure on the SEC's "regulation by enforcement" strategy toward crypto — pressure that contributed to the incoming Trump administration's Crypto Task Force announcing a policy reversal and ultimately to the full dismissal of the SDNY enforcement action in February 2025.

## Background [HIGH confidence]

### The 2022 Rulemaking Petition

In July 2022, Coinbase Global, Inc. filed a [formal petition for rulemaking](https://www.sec.gov/files/rules/petitions/2022/petn4-789.pdf) with the SEC under § 553(e) of the Administrative Procedure Act, requesting that the Commission propose and adopt rules clarifying when and how federal securities laws apply to digital assets. The petition argued that the existing securities law framework — developed for certificated, privity-dependent instruments — does not account for the technical attributes of digitally-native assets recorded on distributed ledgers. Coinbase contended that the regulatory uncertainty forced it to be over-inclusive in classifying potential securities, often excluding viable assets from its platform out of an abundance of caution.

The petition attracted considerable public comment from across the industry and academia. Harvard Law's [corporate governance blog](https://corpgov.law.harvard.edu/2022/08/01/digital-asset-securities-regulation-a-petition-for-rulemaking-from-coinbase/) characterized it as a substantive legal brief arguing that the *Howey* test's "investment contract" definition could not be applied workably to secondary market crypto transactions absent clearer agency guidance.

### The SEC's December 2023 Denial

After more than a year of inaction, the SEC denied the petition on December 15, 2023, in a [single paragraph](https://cointelegraph.com/news/sec-responds-coinbase-2022-crypto-rulemaking-petition). The denial offered three reasons: (a) the existing framework was not unworkable as Coinbase claimed; (b) the Commission's ongoing enforcement proceedings would itself inform how the regulatory framework should apply to crypto assets; and (c) the requested rulemaking would strain the Commission's resources and limit its ability to pursue competing priorities. The denial provided no quantitative analysis, no engagement with the technical arguments in Coinbase's petition, and no discussion of alternative regulatory approaches.

### Coinbase's APA Challenge

Coinbase filed a Petition for Review in the Third Circuit under §§ 706(2) and 555 of the APA, challenging the denial as arbitrary and capricious. The Chamber of Commerce filed an amicus brief supporting Coinbase's position. Oral argument was held in 2024.

## Detailed Analysis [HIGH confidence]

### The Third Circuit's January 13, 2025 Ruling

The Third Circuit's opinion, authored by Judge Ambro and joined by Judges Freeman and Bibas, applied the deferential standard of review applicable to agency denials of rulemaking petitions. Even under this highly deferential standard — which courts have described as "the most deferential end of the arbitrary and capricious spectrum" — the court found the SEC's denial insufficient.

The court identified three key failings in the SEC's reasoning:

**1. The "workability" dismissal was conclusory.** The SEC's assertion that the existing framework was not unworkable "dedicated only one sentence" to addressing the specific technical and compliance arguments in Coinbase's petition. The court held that a bare disagreement with the petitioner's characterization, unaccompanied by analysis, does not constitute reasoned decision-making under the APA.

**2. Pointing to pending enforcement actions was insufficient.** The SEC's rationale that ongoing enforcement proceedings would inform future rulemaking decisions was circular: the court found the SEC could not justify refusing to undertake rulemaking by pointing to enforcement activity that was itself generating the regulatory uncertainty the petition sought to resolve.

**3. Resource constraints cannot be a universal escape hatch.** The Third Circuit cautioned that "resource allocation is not a 'talisman that an agency may invoke to escape judicial review.'" Agencies must at minimum explain *how* scarce resources were allocated and *why* those allocations were reasonable in the specific context of the petition.

The [Jones Day analysis](https://www.jonesday.com/en/insights/2025/01/unanimous-third-circuit-sec-must-justify-crypto-rulemaking-refusal) notes that the panel's unanimous nature and the opinion's precedential designation make this a significant APA decision even beyond the crypto context. The ruling reinforces that summary denials of rulemaking petitions — regardless of how deferential the review standard — must engage with the substantive arguments presented.

### Judge Bibas's Concurrence: The Due Process Warning

Judge Stephanos Bibas authored a notable concurrence that went further than the majority, warning that the SEC's broader enforcement-over-rulemaking approach raised serious constitutional concerns. As reported by [Ropes & Gray](https://www.ropesgray.com/en/insights/alerts/2025/01/third-circuit-coinbase-decision-pressures-sec-on-crypto-rulemaking), Bibas wrote that "[t]he SEC repeatedly sues crypto companies for not complying with the law, yet it will not tell them how to comply," and that this practice "creates a serious constitutional problem; due process guarantees fair notice."

While concurrences are not binding, Judge Bibas's due process framing is analytically significant: it suggests that a court confronted with an SEC enforcement action against a company that had specifically requested regulatory guidance — and been denied — might find fair notice grounds to limit the SEC's enforcement reach. The [CLS Blue Sky Blog](https://clsbluesky.law.columbia.edu/2025/01/30/ropes-gray-discusses-third-circuit-coinbase-decision-pressuring-sec-on-crypto-rulemaking/) observed that Bibas's concurrence was deliberately calibrated to pressure the SEC into rulemaking rather than continued enforcement escalation.

### The Parallel SDNY Stay (January 7, 2025)

Six days before the Third Circuit ruling, Judge Failla of the S.D.N.Y. [granted Coinbase's motion](https://skrypto.sewkis.com/sec-v-coinbase-on-to-the-second-circuit-we-go) to certify the following controlling question of law for interlocutory appeal to the Second Circuit:

> Whether, under the *Howey* test's "investment contract" framework, secondary market transactions in crypto assets can constitute investment contracts when there is no direct contractual relationship between the issuer and the secondary buyer.

Certification of an interlocutory appeal under 28 U.S.C. § 1292(b) is granted only when the district court finds: (1) a controlling question of law, (2) on which there is substantial ground for difference of opinion, and (3) immediate appeal may materially advance the ultimate termination of the litigation. Judge Failla found all three criteria met, and stayed the SDNY enforcement action pending Second Circuit review. The [Manatt client alert](https://www.manatt.com/insights/newsletters/client-alert/get-your-popcorn-ready!-second-circuit-to-hear-cri) noted that this was the first time a federal appellate court had been directly asked to rule on this *Howey*-crypto question (an issue of first impression for the Second Circuit).

### Official Legal Text References

The primary legal authorities invoked in these rulings:

- **Administrative Procedure Act, 5 U.S.C. §§ 553(e), 555, 706(2)(A)**: The statutory framework for rulemaking petitions and judicial review of agency action. Section 553(e) grants interested parties the right to petition for the issuance, amendment, or repeal of a rule. Section 706(2)(A) empowers courts to set aside agency action found to be "arbitrary, capricious, an abuse of discretion, or otherwise not in accordance with law." Official text at [eCFR / 5 U.S.C.](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/5/553).
- **Securities Exchange Act of 1934**: The statute whose application to crypto is at issue. Relevant provisions include § 3(a)(10) (defining "security"), § 5 (unregistered exchange prohibition), § 15(a) (broker registration), § 17A (clearing agency registration). Official text at [eCFR](https://ecfr.gov) and [Congress.gov](https://www.congress.gov/bill/73rd-congress/senate-bill/3420).
- **Third Circuit Opinion (Precedential), No. 23-3202** (Jan. 13, 2025): [Official opinion PDF](https://www2.ca3.uscourts.gov/opinarch/233202p.pdf) via the Third Circuit Court of Appeals website.
- **Justia case page**: [Coinbase Inc v. Securities and Exchange Commission, No. 23-3202 (3d Cir. 2025)](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/ca3/23-3202/23-3202-2025-01-13.html).

## Subsequent Developments [HIGH confidence]

### SEC Crypto Task Force Formation (January 21, 2025)

Eight days after the Third Circuit ruling, Acting SEC Chair Mark T. Uyeda [announced the formation of a Crypto Task Force](https://www.sec.gov/newsroom/press-releases/2025-30) led by Commissioner Hester Peirce. The Task Force was charged with developing a comprehensive regulatory framework through notice-and-comment rulemaking rather than enforcement — effectively an acknowledgment of the judicial pressure created by the Third Circuit and SDNY rulings.

### Dismissal of the SDNY Enforcement Action (February 27, 2025)

The SEC [announced the dismissal with prejudice](https://www.sec.gov/newsroom/press-releases/2025-47) of *SEC v. Coinbase* on February 27, 2025, via a joint stipulation filed in the S.D.N.Y. The Second Circuit interlocutory appeal was rendered moot. As [CoinDesk reported](https://www.coindesk.com/policy/2025/02/27/coinbase-case-dropped-by-u-s-sec-as-agency-reverses-crypto-stance), the dismissal was part of a broader enforcement retreat: the SEC also moved to stay or dismiss pending actions against Binance, Ripple, and other major crypto participants. Commissioner Crenshaw, in a dissenting statement titled ["Crypto 2.0: Regulatory Whiplash"](https://www.sec.gov/newsroom/speeches-statements/crenshaw-remarks-crypto-2-0-regulatory-whiplash-022725), criticized the dismissal as abandoning investor protection obligations.

### Status of the Third Circuit Remand

As of the date of this report, the SEC has not publicly issued the supplemental explanation required by the Third Circuit's remand order. The enforcement action dismissal and Crypto Task Force formation may make the formal rulemaking petition route less relevant as a litigation vehicle, but the remand obligation formally remains pending unless the parties agree to moot it or the SEC issues a new denial with adequate reasoning.

## Impact Assessment [MEDIUM confidence]

### For the Crypto Industry

The twin January 2025 rulings represent the strongest judicial signal to date that the "regulation by enforcement" approach to crypto has real limits under existing administrative and constitutional law. Crypto firms now have:

- A precedential Third Circuit opinion confirming that the SEC cannot summarily reject rulemaking petitions without engaging substantively with the legal and technical arguments presented.
- A concurrence warning that continued enforcement without regulatory guidance creates due process vulnerabilities that could be raised as affirmative defenses.
- A Second Circuit certification (now mooted) confirming that the *Howey* question as applied to secondary crypto markets is genuinely unsettled.

For compliance purposes, the rulemaking remand — even if mooted as a practical matter by the Task Force — strengthens the hand of any future crypto firm that files a formal rulemaking petition and is denied. A bare denial will now face Third Circuit precedent requiring meaningful engagement.

### For Administrative Law Practice

The Third Circuit's opinion has significance beyond crypto. As [Davis Wright Tremaine analyzes](https://www.dwt.com/blogs/financial-services-law-advisor/2025/01/3rd-circuit-coinbase-crypto-ruling), it reinforces that even at the "most deferential end" of arbitrary and capricious review, agencies must provide reasons that are genuinely responsive to the arguments in the petition. Generic invocations of resource constraints or references to parallel proceedings are insufficient. This principle applies to any APA § 553(e) petition across all regulatory domains.

### Unresolved Legal Questions

The February 2025 enforcement dismissal leaves the central *Howey* question unresolved. No federal circuit court has issued a binding holding on whether secondary market crypto transactions constitute "investment contracts." State securities regulators retain independent authority and are not bound by the federal SEC's enforcement posture.

## Action Items

- Crypto exchanges and digital asset platforms should continue tracking the SEC Crypto Task Force's rulemaking output, including any forthcoming registration exemptions, safe harbor proposals, or token classification guidance.
- Legal counsel advising firms that received Wells Notices or are under active investigation should assess whether the Third Circuit's due process framing — the Bibas concurrence's "fair notice" argument — may serve as an affirmative defense in any future enforcement action commenced without intervening rulemaking.
- Firms considering filing formal APA rulemaking petitions with the SEC should note that the Third Circuit has now set a floor: a non-frivolous petition supported by substantive legal and technical arguments cannot be dismissed by a one-paragraph denial. The remand order constitutes precedent on petition adequacy standards.
- Monitor Congressional action on digital asset market structure legislation (FIT21 Act and potential successors) for any legislative resolution of the *Howey*-crypto question that would render judicial and agency processes moot.
- Track any further SEC action on the Third Circuit remand — either a new denial with supplemental reasoning or a formal decision to initiate rulemaking — which will clarify whether the petition track remains a live avenue for regulatory engagement.
