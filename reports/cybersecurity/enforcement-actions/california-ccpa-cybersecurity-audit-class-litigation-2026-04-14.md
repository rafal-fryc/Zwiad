# California's CCPA Cybersecurity Audit Rule: Class-Action Litigation and Discovery Risks

## Executive Summary [HIGH confidence]

The California Privacy Protection Agency's (CPPA) cybersecurity audit regulations, which took effect on [January 1, 2026](https://cppa.ca.gov/announcements/2025/20250923.html), require covered businesses to conduct annual independent cybersecurity audits and file written certifications with the CPPA. The rule imposes a staggered compliance schedule, with the first certifications due April 1, 2028, for businesses with more than $100 million in annual revenue. Beyond the operational compliance burden, the rule creates a substantial new litigation-risk vector: the resulting audit reports, drafts, and gap analyses are expected to become prime targets in data-breach class actions, where plaintiffs' counsel will seek them in discovery to establish notice of security deficiencies and support negligence claims. As [Faegre Drinker attorneys Charles Westerhaus, Lukas Stoutenour, and Craig Heeren explain](https://iapp.org/news/a/california-s-cybersecurity-audit-rule-and-its-impact-for-class-litigation), the rule's mandate that auditors identify gaps in an entity's security posture effectively requires regulated businesses to produce a written inventory of their own potential weaknesses — documents that do not benefit from any statutory discovery shield.

## Regulatory Background [HIGH confidence]

The CPPA's cybersecurity audit regulations were approved by the California Office of Administrative Law on [September 23, 2025](https://cppa.ca.gov/announcements/2025/20250923.html), alongside companion rules on automated decision-making technology (ADMT) and privacy risk assessments. The rules took effect January 1, 2026. They implement the CCPA's directive (as amended by the CPRA) to require businesses whose processing of personal information "presents significant risk to consumers' privacy or security" to undergo annual cybersecurity audits.

Under the final rule, covered businesses must:

- Engage an **objective, independent auditor** (internal or external), with safeguards designed to insulate the auditor from management influence;
- Conduct an **annual audit** that assesses, documents, and summarizes each component of the business's cybersecurity program, using a recognized control framework;
- **Identify gaps and weaknesses** in the cybersecurity program, and any plans to address them;
- Produce a written audit report signed by the auditor; and
- Submit a **written certification of completion** to the CPPA annually ([Ropes & Gray](https://www.ropesgray.com/en/insights/alerts/2026/01/californias-ccpa-cybersecurity-audit-rule-takes-effect-what-businesses-need-to-know); [White & Case](https://www.whitecase.com/insight-alert/cppa-finalizes-rules-admt-risk-assessments-and-cybersecurity-audits-requirements)).

### Staggered Compliance Timeline [HIGH confidence]

The first certification deadline phases in by revenue band:

| Annual revenue | First certification due |
|---|---|
| Over $100 million | April 1, 2028 |
| $50 million – $100 million | April 1, 2029 |
| Under $50 million | April 1, 2030 |

Source: [CPPA final regulations](https://cppa.ca.gov/regulations/ccpa_updates.html); [Brownstein](https://www.bhfs.com/insight/new-ccpa-regulations-require-submission-of-cybersecurity-audit-certifications/); [Greenberg Traurig](https://www.gtlaw.com/en/insights/2025/9/revised-and-new-ccpa-regulations-set-to-take-effect-on-jan-1-2026-summary-of-near-term-action-items).

## The Class-Action Discovery Problem [HIGH confidence]

The Faegre Drinker analysis in IAPP focuses on a feature of the rule that is easy to overlook during compliance planning: the audit creates, preserves, and in some cases transmits to the regulator, a written record of the business's own security gaps. That record is a natural target for plaintiffs in post-breach class actions.

### No Statutory Privilege Shield

California law preserves traditional evidentiary privileges (attorney-client, attorney work product), but **the CCPA provides no statutory shield or discovery limitation for audit work product created as a compliance exercise**. If the audit is conducted primarily to satisfy a regulatory obligation rather than to deliver legal advice, courts are likely to treat the audit file as fully discoverable in civil litigation ([IAPP/Faegre Drinker](https://iapp.org/news/a/california-s-cybersecurity-audit-rule-and-its-impact-for-class-litigation); [Greenberg Traurig on privilege under pressure](https://www.gtlaw.com/en/insights/2025/2/privilege-under-pressure-the-shifting-data-breach-investigation-landscape)).

The CPPA itself declined, during rulemaking, to add language affirming that businesses are not required to hand privileged materials to auditors, reasoning that the rule as written does not compel production of privileged documents. The practical effect is to **shift the burden of preserving privilege to the regulated business** ([Troutman Pepper](https://www.troutmanprivacy.com/2026/03/ccpa-cybersecurity-audits-part-2-the-how-of-ccpa-cybersecurity-audits-operationalizing-the-requirements/)).

### Regulatory Subpoena Power

Both the CPPA and the California Attorney General have statutory authority to compel production of audit reports. Plaintiffs' counsel in follow-on civil class actions are expected to pursue the same documents through third-party subpoenas to the regulator or through direct discovery against the defendant, and to cite the regulator's receipt of the report as a basis to defeat privilege claims ([Faegre Drinker](https://www.faegredrinker.com/en/insights/publications/2025/9/cybersecurity-audits-under-the-california-consumer-privacy-act-ccpa)).

### Drafts, Worksheets, and the Pre-Audit Record

Even where the final report is defensible, discovery battles frequently turn on what came before. Draft reports, internal emails, risk-scoring worksheets, and preliminary gap analyses are fertile territory for plaintiffs arguing that the company knew of specific vulnerabilities, elected not to remediate them, and minimized those risks in the final report. Because the rule requires businesses to retain audit materials for **five years**, a single breach could expose half a decade of contemporaneous records of known weaknesses.

## Mitigation Strategies [MEDIUM confidence]

Counsel advising covered businesses are converging on several practical strategies:

1. **Two-track audits.** Conduct an initial internal assessment under attorney-client privilege to identify and remediate significant gaps, then conduct the formal CCPA audit once remediation is complete. This approach has been upheld in several data-breach cases where courts distinguished between legal-advice workstreams and operational compliance workstreams ([IAPP/Faegre Drinker](https://iapp.org/news/a/california-s-cybersecurity-audit-rule-and-its-impact-for-class-litigation)).
2. **Counsel-directed engagements.** Where external auditors are used, engage them through outside counsel with clearly documented legal-advice purposes (while recognizing that post-*In re Capital One* caselaw makes this insufficient on its own).
3. **Disciplined documentation hygiene.** Limit informal commentary in draft audit materials; treat worksheets and preliminary findings as discoverable until proven otherwise; ensure remediation plans are tracked to closure.
4. **Separation of legal and compliance workstreams.** Keep post-breach forensic investigations, which plaintiffs will also seek, organizationally distinct from the annual compliance audit.

## Action Items

For covered businesses with revenue over $100 million:

- **Now through 2027:** Conduct a preliminary, privileged gap assessment and remediate identified weaknesses before the first formal audit cycle.
- **2027:** Engage an independent auditor meeting the CPPA's objectivity requirements; confirm scope against the business's information-security program and all in-scope processing activities.
- **By April 1, 2028:** Submit the first written certification to the CPPA.
- **Ongoing:** Document a litigation-ready narrative — not merely a compliance-ready one — around every identified gap, with a clear remediation record.

For businesses in the $50–$100M and under-$50M revenue bands, the same preparatory steps apply on a one- and two-year delay, respectively.
