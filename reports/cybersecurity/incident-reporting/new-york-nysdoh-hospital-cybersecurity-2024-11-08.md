# New York Imposes Stringent Cybersecurity and Cyber Incident Reporting Obligations on Hospitals

**Jurisdiction:** New York | **Category:** Cybersecurity | **Date:** 2024-11-08

## Executive Summary [HIGH confidence]

On October 2, 2024, the New York State Department of Health (NYSDOH) adopted comprehensive cybersecurity regulations for all general hospitals licensed under Article 28 of the New York Public Health Law, codified at [10 NYCRR § 405.46](https://www.law.cornell.edu/regulations/new-york/10-NYCRR-405.46). The regulation makes New York the first U.S. state to impose sector-specific, comprehensive cybersecurity governance on general hospitals, requiring hospitals to establish a formal cybersecurity program, designate a Chief Information Security Officer (CISO), and report material cybersecurity incidents to NYSDOH within 72 hours — an obligation that took effect immediately upon adoption. All remaining program requirements must be implemented by October 2, 2025. The regulation exceeds HIPAA's Security Rule by covering a broader category of nonpublic information (NPI) beyond electronic protected health information (ePHI), and by requiring specific organizational controls — such as the CISO designation and mandatory penetration testing — that federal law does not mandate.

## Background [HIGH confidence]

### Regulatory Origins and Statutory Authority

The NYSDOH derives its authority to impose these cybersecurity requirements from [Public Health Law (PHL) § 2803(2)(a)](https://www.governor.ny.gov/news/governor-hochul-announces-proposed-cybersecurity-regulations-hospitals-throughout-new-york), which authorizes the Public Health and Health Planning Council (PHHPC), with the Commissioner of Health's approval, to adopt rules establishing minimum standards for health care facilities licensed under Article 28. Cybersecurity program requirements, as a component of patient safety and data integrity, fall within this authority.

Governor Kathy Hochul announced the initial proposed cybersecurity regulations in [November 2023](https://www.governor.ny.gov/news/governor-hochul-announces-proposed-cybersecurity-regulations-hospitals-throughout-new-york) alongside a commitment of $500 million in funding to help health care facilities upgrade technology systems. The original proposal required hospitals to report cybersecurity incidents within two hours of determination — among the strictest reporting windows of any U.S. sector-specific regulation at the time.

### Rulemaking History

The November 2023 proposal drew substantial industry comment. Ten of thirteen stakeholders objected to the two-hour reporting requirement, arguing it was operationally impractical given the complexity of cybersecurity incident identification and inconsistent with frameworks such as HIPAA (which allows 60 days for breach notification to individuals) and [CIRCIA](https://www.cisa.gov/circia) (which targets a 72-hour reporting window for critical infrastructure). In response, NYSDOH published revised proposed regulations in May 2024, extending the reporting window to 72 hours. Following a comment period ending July 1, 2024, the final rule was adopted on October 2, 2024, with the 72-hour incident reporting obligation taking immediate effect and all remaining requirements subject to a one-year phase-in.

### Healthcare Cybersecurity Context

The regulation was developed against a backdrop of escalating ransomware attacks on U.S. hospitals. The [February 2024 Change Healthcare cyberattack](https://www.nixonpeabody.com/insights/alerts/2024/10/11/new-york-cybersecurity-regulations-for-general-hospitals) disrupted healthcare payments nationwide and highlighted systemic vulnerabilities in the sector. New York hospitals were among those affected by this and other healthcare-sector incidents in the 2022–2024 period, providing political and operational impetus for the NYSDOH regulatory initiative.

## Detailed Analysis [HIGH confidence]

### Scope: Who Is Covered

[10 NYCRR § 405.46](https://www.law.cornell.edu/regulations/new-york/10-NYCRR-405.46) applies to all "general hospitals" licensed under Article 28 of the New York Public Health Law — estimated at approximately 200 facilities statewide. The regulation does not extend to other Article 28 facility types such as ambulatory surgery centers, diagnostic and treatment centers, or nursing homes, though those entities may be affected indirectly through the third-party service provider requirements imposed on hospitals.

### Key Provisions of 10 NYCRR § 405.46

The regulation is organized into 15 topical subsections. The following represent the core obligations:

**Cybersecurity Program (§ 405.46(d)):** Hospitals must establish a written cybersecurity program based on a risk assessment. The program must be reviewed annually and attested by the CISO. It must encompass: information security, data governance and classification, asset inventory and device management, network security and monitoring, incident response, business continuity and disaster recovery, access controls, vendor and third-party management, and employee training. Per [Holland & Knight's November 2024 analysis](https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2024/11/ny-department-of-health-bolsters-hospital-cybersecurity-regulations), the regulation mandates 15 minimum policy categories — a level of specificity that exceeds HIPAA's Security Rule.

**Chief Information Security Officer (§ 405.46(e)):** Hospitals must designate a qualified CISO — a senior or executive-level staff member — responsible for overseeing, implementing, and recommending the cybersecurity program to the governing body, and for providing an annual written report to the governing body on material cybersecurity risks. This requirement is more specific than HIPAA's general "security official" mandate.

**Risk Assessment (§ 405.46(h)):** Hospitals must conduct and document periodic cybersecurity risk assessments — at minimum annually — that evaluate vulnerabilities to the confidentiality, integrity, and availability of nonpublic information. The cybersecurity program must be grounded in the results of these assessments.

**Testing and Vulnerability Management (§ 405.46(f)):** Annual penetration testing and ongoing automated vulnerability scanning are required. HIPAA does not mandate penetration testing by name, making this a meaningful expansion beyond federal requirements.

**Identity and Access Management / MFA (§ 405.46(k)):** Hospitals must implement multi-factor authentication (MFA) for accessing the hospital's internal network from an external network. The CISO may approve in writing an alternative compensating control where MFA is not technically feasible.

**Third-Party Service Providers (§ 405.46(j)):** Hospitals must assess the cybersecurity posture of third-party service providers with access to hospital information systems and include cybersecurity contractual requirements consistent with the regulation. Given that third-party vendor compromises are a leading attack vector in healthcare, this provision carries particular operational weight.

**Incident Reporting (§ 405.46(n)):** Hospitals must notify the [NYSDOH Surge Operations Center (SOC)](https://health.ny.gov/facilities/hospital/cybersecurity/) "as promptly as possible, but not later than 72 hours after determining that a cybersecurity incident has occurred." A "cybersecurity incident" is defined to include: (a) events with a material adverse impact on normal operations; (b) events with a reasonable likelihood of materially harming operations; or (c) ransomware deployment within a material part of the hospital's information systems. Notably, the definition captures likely-harm events before material harm is confirmed, requiring proactive reporting. This obligation was effective **immediately** upon adoption on October 2, 2024.

### Relationship to HIPAA

As analyzed by [Jones Day](https://www.jonesday.com/en/insights/2024/11/new-york-imposes-stringent-cybersecurity-regulations-on-hospitals) and [multiple other law firms](https://www.healthcarelawinsights.com/2024/11/new-york-state-department-of-health-adopts-hospital-cybersecurity-regulations/), 10 NYCRR § 405.46 is designed to supplement, not replace, HIPAA. The key distinctions include:

- **Broader information scope:** The regulation applies to "nonpublic information" (NPI), which encompasses PHI/ePHI but also other sensitive business records not covered by HIPAA's Security Rule.
- **Organizational specificity:** HIPAA requires a "security official"; the NYSDOH regulation requires a titled CISO with defined duties and annual board reporting obligations.
- **Reporting timeline:** HIPAA's breach notification allows 60 days to notify affected individuals; NYSDOH requires institution-to-regulator notification within 72 hours of the hospital's own determination.
- **Testing mandates:** HIPAA's Security Rule requires addressable implementation of testing; 10 NYCRR § 405.46 requires annual penetration testing as a required (not merely addressable) control.

### Enforcement Mechanism

The regulation does not establish its own fine schedule. Enforcement proceeds through the existing [New York Public Health Law](https://health.ny.gov/facilities/hospital/cybersecurity/) enforcement framework applicable to Article 28 facilities. As noted by [HIPAA Journal](https://www.hipaajournal.com/nys-doh-cybersecurity-regulation-deadline/) and [HIPAA Guide](https://www.hipaaguide.net/new-york-hospitals-deadline-cybersecurity-regulations/), the NYSDOH is authorized to impose civil penalties and require corrective action plans. Because cybersecurity requirements are part of the minimum standards for licensure and certification, material noncompliance could put a hospital's operating license at risk — a consequence more severe than monetary penalties alone. Significant cyber incidents causing patient harm may also trigger parallel HIPAA enforcement referrals to the HHS Office for Civil Rights (OCR).

## Impact Assessment [HIGH confidence]

### Affected Entities

Approximately 200 general hospitals licensed under NY PHL Article 28 are subject to the regulation. The requirements apply uniformly regardless of hospital size or complexity, though the NYSDOH has acknowledged that the cybersecurity program must be proportionate to each hospital's risk profile. Smaller and rural hospitals with limited IT resources face the most acute compliance burden.

Third-party service providers to hospitals — including health IT vendors, cloud services, and medical device manufacturers — face indirect compliance pressure through the hospital vendor assessment and contractual requirements under § 405.46(j).

### Industry Significance

New York's regulation is the first of its kind in the United States — a comprehensive, state-level, sector-specific cybersecurity governance mandate for hospitals. It is being watched by other states considering analogous healthcare cybersecurity frameworks. The model — combining mandatory CISO designation, written program requirements, mandatory testing, and rapid incident notification — provides a template that other state health departments may adapt as ransomware attacks on healthcare infrastructure continue.

## Action Items

- Confirm that 72-hour cybersecurity incident reporting to the NYSDOH SOC is operational; this obligation has been in effect since October 2, 2024, with no grace period.
- If not yet completed by October 2, 2025, immediately designate a qualified CISO and document the appointment; failure to have a CISO in place by that date is a compliance deficiency subject to enforcement.
- Conduct a gap analysis mapping existing cybersecurity controls against all 15 policy areas required by § 405.46(d); document findings and remediation plans.
- Review and update third-party vendor contracts to include cybersecurity obligations consistent with § 405.46(j); initiate vendor risk assessments for any provider with access to hospital information systems.
- Ensure annual penetration testing is scheduled and budget is allocated for ongoing automated vulnerability scanning.
- Review the cyber incident definition in § 405.46(n) alongside existing cyber insurance policies; the regulation's proactive definition (capturing likely-harm events and ransomware deployment) may affect insurer notification triggers and coverage applicability.
- Monitor NYSDOH enforcement actions and any FAQs or guidance published to the [official NYSDOH cybersecurity resources page](https://health.ny.gov/facilities/hospital/cybersecurity/) as the enforcement posture develops following the October 2025 deadline.
