# New York NYSDOH Revises Proposed Hospital Cybersecurity Program and Incident Reporting Requirements

**Jurisdiction:** New York | **Category:** Cybersecurity | **Date:** 2024-06-10

## Executive Summary [HIGH confidence]

On May 15, 2024, the New York State Department of Health (NYSDOH) published revised proposed regulations establishing comprehensive cybersecurity program and incident reporting requirements for all general hospitals licensed under Article 28 of the New York Public Health Law. The revised proposal represented a significant moderation of the November 2023 original — most notably extending the mandatory cybersecurity incident reporting timeline from two hours to 72 hours following industry objections during the comment period. After a comment period ending July 1, 2024, the NYSDOH adopted the regulations on October 2, 2024, as [10 NYCRR 405.46](https://www.law.cornell.edu/regulations/new-york/10-NYCRR-405.46), with the 72-hour incident reporting obligation taking effect immediately upon adoption and all other program requirements subject to a one-year compliance deadline of October 2, 2025. New York is the first U.S. state to impose this degree of sector-specific cybersecurity governance on general hospitals, and the regulation goes beyond HIPAA by covering non-public information (NPI) that extends beyond electronic protected health information (ePHI).

## Background [HIGH confidence]

### Regulatory Origins and Statutory Authority

The NYSDOH's authority to regulate hospital operations derives from [Public Health Law (PHL) § 2803(2)(a)](https://www.governor.ny.gov/news/governor-hochul-announces-proposed-cybersecurity-regulations-hospitals-throughout-new-york), which empowers the Public Health and Health Planning Council (PHHPC), subject to the Commissioner of Health's approval, to adopt rules establishing minimum standards for health care facilities licensed under Article 28. This statutory basis underpins all operational compliance requirements imposed on general hospitals, including cybersecurity standards.

On November 13, 2023, Governor Kathy Hochul announced proposed statewide hospital cybersecurity regulations as part of her administration's broader healthcare security initiative. The initial proposal was published in the New York State Register and required, among other things, that hospitals report cybersecurity incidents to NYSDOH within two hours of determining that an incident had occurred — one of the most stringent sector-specific reporting windows in any U.S. state regulation at that time.

### Industry Pushback and Comment Period Response

The November 2023 proposal drew 13 written comments. Ten stakeholders objected specifically to the two-hour incident reporting requirement, arguing that: (1) hospitals need time to assess the nature and scope of an incident before reporting it; (2) a two-hour window is operationally impractical given the complex and often ambiguous onset of cybersecurity events; and (3) the requirement was inconsistent with industry norms and other federal frameworks such as HIPAA, which requires notifications within 60 days for breaches of unsecured protected health information. Commenters consistently recommended extending the reporting window to 72 hours, which aligned with the [CIRCIA](https://www.cisa.gov/circia) framework for critical infrastructure operators.

### Funding Context

Governor Hochul's FY2024 budget included [$500 million in funding](https://www.governor.ny.gov/news/governor-hochul-announces-proposed-cybersecurity-regulations-hospitals-throughout-new-york) available to health care facilities to upgrade technology systems to meet the proposed cybersecurity requirements, signaling the administration's recognition that compliance would require substantial capital investment.

## Detailed Analysis [HIGH confidence]

### Revised Proposed Rule: Key Changes from November 2023

The May 2024 revised proposal published in the New York State Register amended the prior version in several important respects:

**Incident Reporting Timeline:** The most consequential revision was extending the reporting obligation from two hours to 72 hours. As ultimately codified in [10 NYCRR 405.46(n)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/regulations/new-york/10-NYCRR-405.46), hospitals must notify NYSDOH "as promptly as possible, but not later than 72 hours after determining that a cybersecurity incident has occurred." Notification must be made to the NYSDOH Surge Operations Center (SOC). This revision brought the regulation into closer alignment with CISA's CIRCIA framework and federal cybersecurity norms.

**Definition of Nonpublic Information (NPI):** The revised rule expanded the definition of NPI beyond what was specified in the original proposal. The cybersecurity program must now protect: (1) protected health information (PHI) and personally identifying information (PII) as defined under HIPAA; and (2) certain sensitive business-related information. This broader scope means hospital cybersecurity programs must address information assets beyond what HIPAA's Security Rule covers.

**Cybersecurity Incident Definition:** The final rule defines a "cybersecurity incident" as an event that: (a) has a material adverse impact on normal hospital operations; (b) has a reasonable likelihood of materially harming any part of normal hospital operations; or (c) results in the deployment of ransomware within a material part of the hospital's information systems. This definition intentionally captures potential incidents — including likely ransomware events — before material harm is confirmed, requiring proactive reporting.

### Structural Framework: 10 NYCRR 405.46

The adopted regulation [10 NYCRR 405.46](https://www.law.cornell.edu/regulations/new-york/10-NYCRR-405.46) is organized into the following key subsections:

- **§ 405.46(d) — Cybersecurity Policies:** Hospitals must maintain and implement written cybersecurity policies and procedures approved by the governing body upon recommendation from the CISO. Policies must address information security, data governance and classification, asset inventory, network security and monitoring, incident response, business continuity and disaster recovery, access controls, vendor and third-party management, and employee training.

- **§ 405.46(e) — Chief Information Security Officer (CISO):** Every general hospital must designate a qualified CISO — a senior or executive-level staff member — who is responsible for: recommending the cybersecurity program to the governing body; overseeing implementation; providing an annual written report to the governing body on material cybersecurity risks and program status; and approving any written exceptions to mandatory controls such as multi-factor authentication.

- **§ 405.46(f) — Testing and Vulnerability Management:** Hospitals must implement monitoring and testing programs designed to assess the effectiveness of cybersecurity controls and identify vulnerabilities. This includes: penetration testing at least annually; automated scans of information systems to identify publicly known cybersecurity vulnerabilities on a periodic basis; and ongoing monitoring of cybersecurity events.

- **§ 405.46(g) — Audit Trails:** Hospitals must maintain audit trails that capture access to and modifications of critical systems, material changes to authentication controls, and other security-relevant events. Records must be retained for defined periods.

- **§ 405.46(h) — Risk Assessments:** Covered hospitals must conduct and document periodic cybersecurity risk assessments. The cybersecurity program itself must be based on the results of those assessments.

- **§ 405.46(i) — Cybersecurity Personnel:** Hospitals must have sufficient qualified cybersecurity personnel to manage and oversee the cybersecurity program.

- **§ 405.46(j) — Third-Party Service Providers:** Hospitals using third-party service providers with access to hospital information systems must assess the cybersecurity posture of those providers and ensure contractual terms address cybersecurity requirements consistent with the regulation.

- **§ 405.46(k) — Identity and Access Management / Multi-Factor Authentication:** Hospitals must implement identity and access management procedures, including MFA for accessing the hospital's internal network from an external network. The CISO may approve in writing an alternative compensating control where MFA is not technically feasible.

- **§ 405.46(l) — Training:** Hospitals must implement regular cybersecurity awareness training for all personnel and additional role-specific training for personnel with elevated access or cybersecurity responsibilities.

- **§ 405.46(m) — Incident Response Plans:** Hospitals must maintain a written incident response plan addressing roles and responsibilities, internal and external communications, containment, eradication, recovery, and documentation.

- **§ 405.46(n) — Reporting Requirements:** The 72-hour notification obligation to NYSDOH SOC upon determining a cybersecurity incident has occurred. Immediate effect from October 2, 2024.

- **§ 405.46(o) — Confidentiality:** Clarifies that state and federal confidentiality statutes continue to apply to information shared under the regulation.

### Relationship to HIPAA

The NYSDOH regulation deliberately exceeds HIPAA's Security Rule in several dimensions. While HIPAA's Security Rule applies to electronic protected health information (ePHI) held by covered entities and business associates, [10 NYCRR 405.46](https://www.law.cornell.edu/regulations/new-york/10-NYCRR-405.46) applies to a broader category of nonpublic information — including business records that do not constitute ePHI. Additionally, HIPAA's breach notification rules allow 60 days from discovery to notify affected individuals, whereas 10 NYCRR 405.46 requires NYSDOH notification within 72 hours of the hospital's own determination that an incident occurred, a meaningfully shorter and institution-to-regulator (not patient) notification requirement.

## Impact Assessment [HIGH confidence]

### Affected Entities

The regulation applies to all general hospitals licensed under Article 28 of the New York Public Health Law — estimated to cover approximately 200 general hospitals in New York State. The regulation applies regardless of size or complexity, though the NYSDOH guidance acknowledges that the cybersecurity program must be proportionate to each hospital's risk profile.

Importantly, the regulation does not extend to other Article 28 facilities such as ambulatory surgery centers, diagnostic and treatment centers, or nursing homes. However, those facilities may be indirectly affected as hospitals are required to evaluate and contractually address the cybersecurity posture of their third-party service providers.

### Compliance Requirements and Timelines

**Immediate (effective October 2, 2024):**
- 72-hour cybersecurity incident reporting to NYSDOH SOC

**By October 2, 2025 (one-year compliance deadline):**
- Designate a qualified CISO
- Establish a written cybersecurity program based on a risk assessment
- Develop and implement cybersecurity policies and procedures covering all required areas
- Implement multi-factor authentication for external network access
- Establish an incident response plan
- Implement regular penetration testing (at minimum annually) and vulnerability scanning
- Implement an employee cybersecurity training program
- Evaluate and contractually address third-party service provider cybersecurity
- Maintain audit trails for critical system access

### Enforcement and Penalties

[10 NYCRR 405.46](https://www.law.cornell.edu/regulations/new-york/10-NYCRR-405.46) does not create standalone fine provisions. Enforcement proceeds under the existing New York Public Health Law enforcement framework applicable to Article 28 facilities, the same mechanism used for other operational deficiencies such as life safety, infection control, and staffing standards. Given that cybersecurity failures can directly endanger patient care and safety, the NYSDOH is expected to treat violations — particularly failure to report incidents within 72 hours and failure to implement basic program controls — with heightened scrutiny. Significant violations may also trigger HIPAA enforcement referrals to the Office for Civil Rights (OCR) and, in the case of patient harm or data breaches, separate regulatory and civil proceedings.

### Industry Significance

New York's hospital cybersecurity regulation is notable as the first U.S. state regulation to impose comprehensive, sector-specific cybersecurity governance on general hospitals beyond what federal HIPAA requirements demand. The regulation was developed against a backdrop of increasing ransomware attacks on healthcare systems, including the February 2024 [Change Healthcare cyberattack](https://www.nixonpeabody.com/insights/alerts/2024/10/11/new-york-cybersecurity-regulations-for-general-hospitals) that disrupted healthcare payments nationwide and prompted federal and state regulatory responses. New York's approach — combining program requirements, CISO designation, mandatory testing, and immediate incident reporting — is being watched by other states considering analogous healthcare cybersecurity regulations.

## Action Items

- General hospitals licensed under NY PHL Article 28 must verify that 72-hour cybersecurity incident reporting to the NYSDOH SOC is operational; this obligation was effective October 2, 2024, and there is no grace period.
- Hospitals without a designated CISO should begin recruitment or internal designation processes immediately; the October 2, 2025 deadline for full program compliance leaves limited implementation time.
- Conduct a cybersecurity risk assessment if not recently completed; the regulation requires the written cybersecurity program to be grounded in a documented risk assessment.
- Review and update third-party vendor contracts to ensure cybersecurity obligations are addressed consistent with § 405.46(j); vendor-related incidents are a primary attack vector in healthcare.
- Assess the gap between existing MFA controls and the new mandatory MFA requirement for external network access; document any CISO-approved written exceptions.
- Confirm annual penetration testing is scheduled and budget is allocated; the October 2025 deadline effectively requires the first test cycle to begin no later than mid-2025 to allow remediation time.
- Review cyber insurance coverage for consistency with the new incident definition; the regulation's forward-looking definition (capturing likely-harm events) may affect when reporting obligations and insurer notification obligations are triggered.
- Monitor whether NYSDOH publishes further guidance or FAQs on 10 NYCRR 405.46 implementation; enforcement posture will likely develop through initial survey and inspection cycles following the October 2025 compliance date.
