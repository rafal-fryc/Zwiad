# Email Privacy Act (H.R. 9016) Introduced in House and Senate, Requiring Warrants for Stored Electronic Communications

**Jurisdiction:** Federal | **Category:** Privacy | **Date:** June 2, 2026

## Summary [HIGH confidence]

On June 2, 2026, a bipartisan group of US House and Senate members formally introduced the [Email Privacy Act (H.R. 9016)](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-bill/9016/text), legislation that would require law enforcement to obtain a warrant before compelling third-party service providers to disclose stored electronic communications, regardless of how long those communications have been stored. The bill directly addresses the obsolete "180-day rule" embedded in the [Electronic Communications Privacy Act of 1986 (ECPA)](https://epic.org/ecpa/), which currently allows government agencies to access emails older than 180 days without a warrant. The legislation has been introduced in multiple prior congressional sessions without reaching enactment; the 2026 push is notable for its simultaneous House and Senate introduction with broad bipartisan cosponsorship.

## Key Facts [HIGH confidence]

- **House sponsors:** Rep. Suzan DelBene (D-WA-1) and Rep. Warren Davidson (R-OH-8) introduced H.R. 9016 on May 22, 2026, per [Congress.gov](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-bill/9016/text); the bill was referred to the House Committee on the Judiciary.
- **Senate companion:** Senators Mike Lee (R-UT) and Ron Wyden (D-OR) cosponsored a companion bill in the Senate, with more than 25 cosponsors including senior members from both parties, per the [Lee Senate press release](https://www.lee.senate.gov/2026/6/lee-cosponsors-bipartisan-email-privacy-act-to-protect-americans-from-warrantless-searches).
- **Core change:** The bill eliminates ECPA's 180-day rule and establishes a uniform warrant requirement for the content of stored electronic communications, regardless of how long they have been stored or whether they have been opened, per [Broadband Breakfast reporting](https://broadbandbreakfast.com/bipartisan-bill-would-protect-archived-emails-from-warrantless-searches/).
- **Customer notice provision:** The bill would allow service providers to notify customers when the government seeks access to their communications, unless a court order prohibits disclosure, per the [DelBene House press release](https://delbene.house.gov/news/documentsingle.aspx?DocumentID=4346).
- **Historical backdrop:** A prior version (H.R. 699, 114th Congress) passed the House 419-0 in 2016 but died in the Senate after Sen. Lee withdrew it from the Senate Judiciary Committee markup over a Sen. John Cornyn (R-TX) amendment expanding FBI national security letter (NSL) authority over electronic communication transactional records (ECTRs), per [IAPP historical analysis](https://iapp.org/news/a/the-email-privacy-act-what-happened-and-where-we-are-now).
- **Civil society support:** [NetChoice](https://netchoice.org/netchoice-applauds-the-bipartisan-email-privacy-act-urges-congress-to-swiftly-pass-it/) and CDT have previously organized coalition support letters for prior versions of this legislation.

## Legal Background [HIGH confidence]

ECPA was enacted in 1986, when email storage was costly and ephemeral — messages stored for more than 180 days were considered "abandoned" under the law's original logic. Under the current [Stored Communications Act (18 U.S.C. § 2703)](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/LSB/HTML/LSB10801.web.html), law enforcement must obtain a warrant only for communications stored 180 days or fewer; older communications can be obtained via a mere administrative subpoena, which the agency itself may issue without judicial review.

This two-tier system has long been criticized as a constitutional anomaly. Only the Sixth Circuit has squarely held that all emails stored in the cloud are protected by the Fourth Amendment warrant requirement, per CRS analysis. The Supreme Court's 2018 ruling in [*Carpenter v. United States*](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/585/16-402/) (585 U.S. ___ (2018)) — which rejected the third-party doctrine as applied to cell-site location data — reinforced the broader argument that digital data shared with service providers retains Fourth Amendment protection. However, *Carpenter* did not directly resolve the email warrant question, leaving the 180-day rule technically in effect for most jurisdictions.

The Email Privacy Act would resolve this circuit split and constitutional ambiguity by statute, establishing a uniform rule across all federal circuits.

## Legislative History [MEDIUM confidence]

The bill has been introduced in successive congressional sessions since at least the 113th Congress:

- **114th Congress (2015-2016):** H.R. 699 passed the House Judiciary Committee 28-0 and the full House 419-0, but stalled in the Senate when a Sen. John Cornyn (R-TX) amendment expanding FBI national security letter (NSL) authority over electronic communication transactional records (ECTRs) was attached to the bill, per [IAPP historical analysis](https://iapp.org/news/a/the-email-privacy-act-what-happened-and-where-we-are-now).
- **115th–118th Congresses:** Various versions were reintroduced, including H.R. 387 (115th Congress) and H.R. 9656 (117th Congress), none of which advanced to Senate consideration.
- **119th Congress (2026):** H.R. 9016, introduced May 22, 2026, represents the latest iteration. The simultaneous Senate introduction with a 25+ cosponsor slate is the strongest bipartisan positioning the bill has achieved since 2016, per [Washington Stand reporting](https://washingtonstand.com/article/bipartisan-group-introduces-bill-to-protect-private-citizens-4th-amendment-email-privacy).

Prior Senate failure was not due to opposition to the warrant principle itself but to secondary amendments that became political obstacles. Advocates and sponsors have structured the 2026 legislation to avoid prior pitfalls, though the Senate calendar and competing surveillance-related legislation remain significant variables.

## Compliance and Business Impact

If enacted, H.R. 9016 would affect any business or individual whose electronic communications are held by third-party service providers — in practice, virtually every organization using cloud-hosted email (Gmail, Microsoft 365, etc.). The practical effects would include:

- **Raised evidentiary burden for government investigators:** Agencies including the DOJ, SEC, and IRS that currently use administrative subpoenas to access older emails would be required to demonstrate probable cause and obtain a judicial warrant.
- **Reduced government compliance burden on service providers:** Providers would face a clearer, single legal standard and be permitted to notify users of government requests in most circumstances.
- **No direct private-sector compliance obligations:** The bill regulates government access procedures, not private sector data handling. Businesses are not required to take immediate action pending passage.

The SEC has historically opposed ECPA warrant reforms because it lacks statutory authority to obtain warrants, relying on subpoenas instead, per [CDT analysis](https://cdt.org/insights/sec-tries-to-weaken-important-ecpa-reform/). Whether the SEC or other civil regulatory agencies would be carved out remains a watchpoint in any Senate markup.

## Action Items

- **Monitor legislative status:** Track H.R. 9016 on [Congress.gov](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-bill/9016/text) for committee referral, hearing dates, and floor votes. A Senate Judiciary Committee markup is the key near-term milestone.
- **Assess law enforcement access dependencies:** Organizations (e.g., financial institutions, healthcare entities) that rely on government investigations involving stored communications should monitor how the warrant requirement would affect their cooperation obligations.
- **Engage advocacy windows:** Trade associations and civil liberties organizations will likely organize comment campaigns if markup proceeds. Prior coalition letters have influenced the bill's scope (e.g., the customer-notice provision).
- **No immediate compliance action required:** The bill is in early introduction stage. Track but do not restructure data retention practices on the basis of this introduction alone.
