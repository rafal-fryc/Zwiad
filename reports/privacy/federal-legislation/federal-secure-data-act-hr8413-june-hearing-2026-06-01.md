# SECURE Data Act (HR 8413): June 3 House Subcommittee Hearing and Legislative Outlook

**Jurisdiction:** Federal | **Category:** Privacy | **Date:** June 1, 2026

## Summary [HIGH confidence]

On June 3, 2026, the House Energy and Commerce Subcommittee on Commerce, Manufacturing, and Trade held a hearing entitled "Examining Legislation to Establish a Federal Comprehensive Privacy and Data Security Law," marking the first congressional hearing on the SECURE Data Act (HR 8413) since its April 22 introduction. The bill — sponsored by Rep. John Joyce (R-PA) and eight Republican co-sponsors — proposes the first comprehensive federal consumer privacy law of the 119th Congress, establishing consumer rights to access, correct, delete, and port personal data while deploying sweeping preemption language that would override all existing state privacy laws. The hearing surfaced persistent fault lines: industry broadly supports the single-standard approach, while civil society and Democratic members warn the bill's lack of a private right of action and absolute state-law preemption would leave consumers with weaker protections than today's state patchwork.

## Key Facts [HIGH confidence]

- **Bill and sponsors**: HR 8413, the Securing and Establishing Consumer Uniform Rights and Enforcement over Data Act, was introduced April 22, 2026 by Rep. John Joyce (R-PA), Vice Chairman of the House Energy and Commerce Committee, with co-sponsors Reps. Morgan Griffith (VA), Troy Balderson (OH), Jay Obernolte (CA), Russell Fry (SC), Nick Langworthy (NY), Tom Kean (NJ), Craig Goldman (TX), and Julie Fedorchak (ND), per the [official Energy and Commerce Committee press release](https://energycommerce.house.gov/posts/committees-on-energy-and-commerce-and-financial-services-introduce-pair-of-privacy-bills-to-establish-comprehensive-data-protections-for-all-americans).

- **June 3 hearing**: Subcommittee Chairmen Guthrie and Bilirakis convened the hearing at 10:15 a.m. EDT in the John D. Dingell Room, 2123 Rayburn House Office Building, per the [committee hearing announcement](https://energycommerce.house.gov/posts/chairmen-guthrie-and-bilirakis-announce-hearing-on-establishing-a-federal-data-privacy-law). Witnesses included Kate Goodloe, Managing Director of the Business Software Alliance (BSA), and representatives from the Electronic Privacy Information Center (EPIC), per [Bloomberg Government reporting](https://news.bgov.com/bloomberg-government-news/data-privacy-bill-hearings-witnesses-unveiled-by-house-panel).

- **Official bill text**: HR 8413 was formally introduced on April 21, 2026 and referred to both the Committee on Energy and Commerce and the Committee on the Judiciary. The [full text is available on Congress.gov](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-bill/8413/text).

- **Preemption scope**: Section 15 of the bill would preempt any state law that "relates to the provisions of this Act," a broad standard that the [Future of Privacy Forum analysis](https://fpf.org/blog/contextualizing-the-proposed-secure-data-act-in-the-state-privacy-landscape/) warns could sweep in sectoral laws including Illinois BIPA, the Washington My Health My Data Act, state data broker registration laws, and California's Delete Act — potentially eliminating hundreds of state privacy protections.

- **Consumer rights package**: The bill grants consumers rights to access, correct, delete, and port personal data; opt out of targeted advertising, data sales, and profiling with significant effects. Sensitive data categories — including biometric information and precise geolocation data — require affirmative opt-in consent before processing, per [IAPP bill analysis](https://iapp.org/news/a/secure-data-act-analysis-of-the-new-federal-privacy-bill).

- **Data minimization**: The Act requires controllers to limit collection to what is "adequate, relevant and reasonably necessary" for disclosed purposes — a standard drawn from existing state law frameworks, per [Hunton Andrews Kurth analysis](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-cybersecurity-law-blog/house-republicans-introduce-comprehensive-federal-privacy-bill-secure-data-act).

- **Minors' data**: Personal data of individuals under age 16 is classified as sensitive, requiring verified parental consent — expanding COPPA's age-13 threshold by three years, per [IAPP analysis](https://iapp.org/news/a/secure-data-act-analysis-of-the-new-federal-privacy-bill).

- **Enforcement — no private right of action**: The FTC enforces the Act as a Section 5 violation, with jurisdiction expressly extended to communications common carriers. State attorneys general may bring actions for injunctive relief and monetary redress. The bill does not include a private right of action — a significant departure from APRA and the ADPPA, per [Wiley Rein analysis](https://www.wiley.law/alert-SECURE-Act-US-House-Introduces-New-National-Privacy-Framework).

- **Small business exemption**: Companies with less than $25 million in annual gross revenue are exempt — a threshold more restrictive than APRA's $40 million floor but more generous than most state laws, per [Hunton analysis](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-cybersecurity-law-blog/house-republicans-introduce-comprehensive-federal-privacy-bill-secure-data-act).

- **Companion GUARD Act**: The GUARD Financial Data Act, introduced simultaneously by the House Financial Services Committee, modernizes Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act Title V to add data minimization, deletion rights, and expanded consumer protections. The SECURE Data Act exempts GLBA-covered entities; the two bills are designed as a coordinated pair covering all sectors, per [Akin Gump analysis](https://www.akingump.com/en/insights/alerts/house-gop-unveils-landmark-comprehensive-privacy-draft-alongside-glba-proposal).

- **Cure period**: Before the FTC or a state AG may bring an enforcement action, the Act requires a 45-day cure period, per [Wiley Rein analysis](https://www.wiley.law/alert-SECURE-Act-US-House-Introduces-New-National-Privacy-Framework).

## Comparison to APRA [MEDIUM confidence]

The SECURE Data Act is the Republican successor to the American Privacy Rights Act (APRA), the bipartisan bill that advanced through the House Energy and Commerce Committee in May 2024 but died in the full House after amendments drew opposition from both privacy advocates and industry. Key structural differences:

| Feature | SECURE Data Act (HR 8413) | APRA (2024) |
|---|---|---|
| **Private right of action** | None | Yes (limited, with safe harbors) |
| **State preemption** | Absolute — "relates to" standard | Partial — states may enact stronger laws on specific topics |
| **Small business threshold** | $25M revenue | $40M revenue |
| **FTC authority over common carriers** | Expressly extended | Extended |
| **Algorithmic decision rights** | Opt-out from profiling with significant effects | Opt-out from covered algorithms / AI decisions |
| **Age threshold for sensitive data** | Under 16 | Under 17 |
| **Bipartisan support at introduction** | Republican-only | Bipartisan (Cantwell-McMorris Rodgers) |

The most consequential divergence is preemption. APRA preserved state authority to legislate on specific topics beyond the federal floor; the SECURE Data Act's "relates to" language creates an absolute federal ceiling. This change, combined with the removal of a private right of action, has generated the strongest opposition from privacy advocates and Democratic members, per [IAPP legislative analysis](https://iapp.org/news/a/taking-stock-of-us-house-republicans-proposed-secure-data-act).

## Stakeholder Positions [MEDIUM confidence]

**Industry support**: The [Business Software Alliance welcomed](https://www.bsa.org/news-events/news/bsa-welcomes-bill-to-establish-federal-privacy-standards) the bill as a step toward a single, predictable national standard. Industry's core argument is that the current 21-state patchwork imposes excessive compliance costs without commensurate consumer benefit.

**Civil society opposition**: The [Electronic Frontier Foundation](https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2026/05/secure-data-act-not-serious-piece-privacy-legislation) called the bill "not a serious piece of privacy legislation," warning it would wipe out stronger state protections, eliminate consumers' ability to sue to enforce their rights, and preempt tools such as automated opt-out signals currently required under California law.

**State agency opposition**: The California Privacy Protection Agency Board [formally opposed](https://privacy.ca.gov/wp-content/uploads/sites/357/2026/04/CalPrivacy-Letter-HR-8413-SECURE-Data-Act.pdf) any federal measure preempting the CCPA/CPRA, calling the preemption language a "nonstarter."

**Democratic opposition**: Ranking Member Frank Pallone (D-NJ) accused Republicans of prioritizing corporate interests over consumer rights, stating the bill "protects corporations and their bottom line, not people's privacy," per [AdExchanger reporting](https://www.adexchanger.com/data-privacy/does-the-new-federal-data-privacy-bill-have-a-snowballs-chance-of-passing/).

**FPF analysis**: The [Future of Privacy Forum](https://fpf.org/press-releases/fpf-on-the-securing-and-establishing-consumer-uniform-rights-and-enforcement-over-data-secure-data-act/) noted the bill does not materially advance the preemption and enforcement debates beyond APRA and ADPPA, though it acknowledged the bill reflects substantial stakeholder engagement over 14 months.

## Legislative Prospects [LOW confidence]

The bill faces a difficult path to enactment. To become law, it must clear the House subcommittee, survive a House floor vote, pass the Senate — where 60 votes are needed to overcome a filibuster — and be signed by the President. Republicans hold slim majorities in both chambers, making some Democratic support in the Senate essential.

The bill's Republican-only sponsorship at introduction stands in contrast to APRA's bipartisan origins. Senate Democrats have not signaled support; the bill's broad preemption and absence of a private right of action are the primary sticking points that prevented APRA from advancing, and the SECURE Data Act takes more conservative positions on both. The [AdExchanger analysis](https://www.adexchanger.com/data-privacy/does-the-new-federal-data-privacy-bill-have-a-snowballs-chance-of-passing/) notes the Senate is where the bill will most likely stall, repeating the pattern of prior comprehensive privacy bills.

Notably, a Senate companion bill — the Consumer Data Privacy and Security Act of 2026 (S. 4211) — has been introduced separately, and the DATA Act of 2026 (S. 3585) is also pending in the Senate, indicating multiple competing frameworks without a unified bicameral approach.

## Action Items

- **Monitor June 3 hearing record**: Review official hearing transcripts and witness written testimony as they are published on the [Energy and Commerce Committee website](https://energycommerce.house.gov/) — these will surface industry and civil society positions that could shape amendments.

- **Assess preemption exposure**: Organizations currently operating under state-specific privacy programs (particularly California, Colorado, Illinois, and Washington) should map which obligations could be displaced if the "relates to" preemption survives. Note that preemption would not be self-executing — individual state laws would require court challenge.

- **Track markup schedule**: The committee has signaled intent to advance the bill on Republican votes in subcommittee before seeking bipartisan support. Watch for a markup notice on the [Energy and Commerce Committee calendar](https://energycommerce.house.gov/).

- **Watch Senate companion activity**: Monitor S. 4211 (Consumer Data Privacy and Security Act of 2026) and S. 3585 (DATA Act of 2026) for signs of Senate Judiciary Committee engagement that could shift the bicameral negotiating dynamic.

- **Do not pause state compliance**: Given the bill's uncertain Senate prospects and the time required for any signed legislation to take effect, organizations should continue investing in state-law compliance programs. Preemption is not imminent.
