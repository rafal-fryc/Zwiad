# House GOP Revolt Sinks Long-Term FISA 702 Renewal; April 30 Deadline Now in Sight

**Jurisdiction:** Federal | **Category:** privacy | **Date:** 2026-04-22

## Summary [HIGH confidence]

A bloc of roughly twenty House Republicans derailed both a five-year and an 18-month reauthorization of [FISA Section 702](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/R48592) in back-to-back early-morning votes on April 17, 2026, forcing Congress to retreat to a 10-day stopgap that keeps the warrantless surveillance authority alive only through **April 30, 2026**. The rebel lawmakers — joined by most House Democrats — demanded a warrant requirement before the FBI may query Americans' communications incidentally swept up under Section 702. Senate Majority Leader John Thune is now positioning the Senate to take the lead, floating a clean three-year renewal, while Speaker Mike Johnson must broker a workable compromise before April 30 or risk a full lapse of one of the U.S. Intelligence Community's most consequential collection tools.

## Key Facts [HIGH confidence]

- Section 702 of the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act, codified at [50 U.S.C. § 1881a](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/R48592), was last reauthorized on April 20, 2024, by the [Reforming Intelligence and Securing America Act (RISAA), H.R. 7888](https://www.congress.gov/bill/118th-congress/house-bill/7888/text), which set an April 20, 2026 sunset.
- On April 17, 2026, the House passed a 10-day extension by voice vote in a late-night session after two longer reauthorization proposals collapsed; the Senate cleared the same extension unanimously and President Trump signed it, pushing the new deadline to **April 30, 2026** ([NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/04/17/nx-s1-5788573/house-extends-surveillance-powers-for-10-days)).
- The failed five-year extension included new oversight provisions but did **not** include a warrant requirement for U.S.-person queries; it was opposed by most Democrats and a Freedom Caucus bloc ([CNN](https://www.cnn.com/2026/04/17/politics/house-fisa-foreign-surveillance)).
- The Trump-backed 18-month clean extension also failed, with approximately 20 Republicans joining most Democrats in a 197–228 procedural defeat ([Roll Call](https://rollcall.com/2026/04/20/congress-searches-for-path-on-surveillance-authority-renewal/)).
- GOP privacy hawks leading the revolt include Rep. Thomas Massie (R-KY), Rep. Lauren Boebert (R-CO), Rep. Warren Davidson (R-OH), and members of the House Judiciary Committee who demanded floor consideration of a warrant amendment ([The Hill](https://thehill.com/homenews/house/5835879-fisa-702-spy-powers-vote/)).
- House Judiciary Chair Jim Jordan (R-OH) — who has historically championed a warrant requirement — reversed his position during the current cycle and urged a clean 18-month extension at the White House's request ([Roll Call](https://rollcall.com/2026/04/20/congress-searches-for-path-on-surveillance-authority-renewal/)).
- Senate Majority Leader John Thune has signaled the Senate may take the lead, setting up possible consideration of a **clean three-year extension**, and stated "We can't go dark, we just can't afford to go dark" ([Axios](https://www.axios.com/2026/04/17/fisa-senate-vote-april-30-house-revolt)).
- A classified Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Court (FISC) opinion from early April 2026 reportedly identified major Section 702 compliance problems; Sen. Wyden has called for its declassification ([Brennan Center](https://www.brennancenter.org/our-work/research-reports/section-702-foreign-intelligence-surveillance-act-fisa-2026-resource-page)).
- ODNI's 2025 transparency report showed FBI queries of Americans' data under Section 702 **rose 35% in 2025**, bolstering reformers' argument that RISAA guardrails are insufficient ([Nextgov](https://www.nextgov.com/cybersecurity/2026/03/fbi-queries-americans-data-under-fisa-702-rose-35-2025/412103/)).
- In December 2024, the U.S. District Court for the Eastern District of New York (Judge LaShann DeArcy Hall) ruled in [*United States v. Hasbajrami*](https://www.justsecurity.org/106895/warrant-needed-fisa-section-702/) that U.S.-person queries of Section 702 databases require a warrant under the Fourth Amendment — the first court ruling to reach this conclusion.
- If Congress allows the authority to fully lapse on April 30, no **new** collection directives may issue; existing FISC-renewed certifications (which the court renewed April 11) would allow collection under existing directives to continue but carriers have warned they will halt compliance if the statute lapses ([Axios](https://www.axios.com/2026/04/17/house-republicans-pass-fisa-extension)).

## The Warrant Divide: Core Policy Issue [HIGH confidence]

The central fight is whether the FBI must obtain a warrant — or satisfy an equivalent Fourth Amendment showing — before querying Section 702 databases for U.S.-person communications. The program collects communications of foreign targets; because those targets communicate with Americans, U.S. persons' messages are incidentally captured and retained in searchable intelligence databases.

Reformers — spanning the ideological spectrum from Rep. Davidson (R-OH) and Sen. Mike Lee (R-UT) to Sen. Ron Wyden (D-OR) — have introduced the [Government Surveillance Reform Act (GSRA)](https://www.wyden.senate.gov/news/press-releases/wyden-lee-davidson-and-lofgren-introduce-bill-to-reform-fisa-section-702-protect-americans-constitutional-rights-and-plug-data-broker-surveillance-loophole), which would impose a warrant requirement for U.S.-person queries, ban warrantless government data-broker purchases, and narrow RISAA's expanded ECSP provider definition.

The national security establishment and the Trump administration oppose any warrant requirement, arguing it would slow foreign intelligence collection and create operational risks. Documented abuses — including warrantless searches involving a U.S. senator, journalists, 19,000 donors to a congressional campaign, and FBI employee family members — have eroded trust in the voluntary safeguards ([Center for Democracy and Technology](https://cdt.org/insights/four-reasons-fisa-702-still-needs-a-warrant-rule-for-us-person-queries/); [Brennan Center](https://www.brennancenter.org/our-work/research-reports/section-702-foreign-intelligence-surveillance-act)).

## What Happens at April 30 [MEDIUM confidence]

If Congress fails to pass any extension by April 30:

- **No new directives** may be issued to electronic communications service providers after the lapse.
- Intelligence agencies could continue collection under **existing directives** issued before the sunset, but the legal basis would be contested; litigation from carriers and technology companies is likely.
- Telecom and cloud providers have privately warned the IC they will **stop collecting** if the statutory authority lapses, creating an immediate operational gap.
- Any collection during a statutory gap would face Fourth Amendment and APA challenges in future criminal prosecutions.

If the Senate passes a clean three-year extension and sends it to the House, Speaker Johnson would face the same floor math that defeated the 18-month extension — unless the bipartisan warrant bloc is satisfied with some compromise reform package. Bipartisan talks between House GOP privacy hawks and reform-minded Democrats intensified after the April 17 revolt ([ms.now](https://www.ms.now/news/fisa-house-warrantless-surveillance-spying)).

## Action Items

- **Compliance officers at U.S. telecoms, cloud providers, and hosting companies subject to Section 702 directives**: review your organization's legal protocols for what happens if the statute lapses on April 30. Confirm standing counsel guidance on whether existing FISC certifications provide continued directive coverage during a statutory gap.
- **In-house counsel at national-security-adjacent firms** (cloud, telecom, AI, data centers): monitor the Senate floor schedule through April 30. Thune's three-year clean extension bill could move rapidly; track whether any warrant or reform amendments are introduced before a final vote.
- **Privacy and government-affairs teams**: brief leadership on the four live policy scenarios — (1) clean 3-year Senate-led extension, (2) reform-laden compromise with partial warrant requirement, (3) another short-term stopgap, or (4) full lapse — and their operational implications.
- **Data brokers and commercial intelligence vendors**: the GSRA includes a ban on warrantless government data purchases. If a compromise incorporates this provision, existing government contracts for commercial data may require renegotiation or termination.
- **Litigation teams**: preserve evidence and document any Section 702-adjacent collection activities; a lapse-and-revive scenario will generate Fourth Amendment and statutory challenges in pending and future criminal and civil proceedings.
