# US Judiciary Proposes Stronger Privacy Protections for Court Filings: Full SSN Redaction and Pseudonyms for Minors

**Jurisdiction:** Federal | **Category:** Privacy | **Date:** 2026-06-04

## Executive Summary [MEDIUM confidence]

The Judicial Conference of the United States Committee on Rules of Practice and Procedure, at its June 3–4, 2026 meeting, advanced proposed amendments to the federal court privacy rules — Federal Rules of Civil Procedure (FRCP) Rule 5.2, Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure (FRCrP) Rule 49.1, Federal Rules of Appellate Procedure (FRAP) Rule 25, and Federal Rules of Bankruptcy Procedure (FRBP) Rule 9037 — for public comment. The proposals contain two substantive changes: (1) require the **complete redaction** of Social Security numbers (SSNs) and other taxpayer identification numbers from publicly accessible court filings, eliminating the current practice of permitting the last four digits to remain visible; and (2) replace the use of **initials** for minors in court filings with **gender-neutral pseudonyms**, providing stronger protection for child victims and witnesses. A notable carve-out applies: the Bankruptcy Rules Committee determined that full SSN redaction is not practicable in bankruptcy cases, where debtors' last four SSN digits will continue to be permitted. The amendments, if approved, would affect all parties filing documents in federal civil, criminal, and appellate courts, including self-represented litigants, attorneys, and government agencies.

## Background [HIGH confidence]

The existing federal court privacy rules trace their origin to Section 205 of the E-Government Act of 2002, Pub. L. 107-347, which directed the Judicial Conference to adopt rules requiring parties to omit or limit the inclusion of personal data identifiers in public electronic filings. In response, the Judicial Conference promulgated coordinated amendments to Appellate Rule 25, Bankruptcy Rule 9037, Civil Rule 5.2, and Criminal Rule 49.1, all of which took effect on December 1, 2007 ([LII, Rule 5.2](https://www.law.cornell.edu/rules/frcp/rule_5.2)).

Under the rules as they have stood for nearly two decades, filers are required to redact certain personal identifiers to partial form only:

- Social Security or taxpayer identification numbers: only the **last four digits** may appear
- Dates of birth: only the **year** may appear
- Names of minor children: only the **initial letters** may appear
- Financial account numbers: only the **last four digits** may appear
- Home addresses in criminal cases: only the **city and state** may appear

([USCOURTS, Privacy Policy for Electronic Case Files](https://www.uscourts.gov/privacy-policy-electronic-case-files))

Responsibility for redaction rests solely with the filing party. Clerks of court are not required to review filings for compliance, and failure to redact can expose counsel and parties to sanctions. Any personal information not otherwise protected by federal or local rule is made available to the public via PACER (Public Access to Court Electronic Records).

The E-Government Act further requires the Judicial Conference to submit to Congress a biennial report on the adequacy of the privacy rules. The most recent such report — the [2024 Judicial Conference Report on Privacy Rules](https://www.uscourts.gov/sites/default/files/2024-12/2024-jcus-report-on-privacy-rules-final-with-attachment.pdf) — covers the period from June 2022 to June 2024 and catalogs ongoing implementation efforts and identified gaps. It was the 2024 biennial report cycle and associated advisory committee activity that led directly to the current rulemaking.

The privacy rules project addressing SSN redaction and minors' pseudonyms has been under deliberation for several years. In 2022, the Committee on Rules of Practice and Procedure appointed a cross-committee working group of reporters to examine a suggestion by Senator Ron Wyden (D-OR) to reconsider whether complete redaction of SSNs should be required. The working group's January 2025 memorandum to the Standing Committee concluded that "there is no real-world problem that we need to solve right now," a conclusion that drew pushback from advocacy groups. The American Association for Justice (AAJ) submitted comments disagreeing with that assessment, arguing that the risk of data misuse "is an omnipresent, pervasive, and evolving threat in every sector of American life, including the judicial system" ([AAJ Informal Comment, Justice.org](https://www.justice.org/advocacy/legal-affairs/federal-rules/ic---privacy-and-ssns)).

Separately, the Department of Justice proposed replacing the use of initials for minors in public criminal filings with pseudonyms, arguing that initials provide inadequate protection, particularly for child victims of sexual exploitation. The DOJ proposal garnered support from bar associations representing crime victims, including a joint letter from AAJ and the National Crime Victims Bar Association (NCVBA) filed in March 2024 calling for the use of gender-neutral pseudonyms and pronouns ([AAJ suggestion, uscourts.gov](https://www.uscourts.gov/sites/default/files/2025-03/suggestion_from_aaj_-_redaction_of_ssns_2.pdf)).

## Detailed Analysis [MEDIUM confidence]

### SSN and Taxpayer Identification Number Redaction

The proposed amendment to FRCP Rule 5.2(a), FRCrP Rule 49.1(a), and their appellate counterparts would eliminate the longstanding practice of permitting the last four digits of SSNs to remain in public court documents. Under the proposal, SSNs must be **completely redacted** — no partial disclosure permitted.

The policy rationale is that partial SSNs, especially combined with dates of birth, financial account numbers, and names that appear in the same court filing, can enable identity theft and fraud. Critics of the existing rules have argued that even the last four digits represent a meaningful privacy risk when aggregated with other publicly available information via PACER's mass download functionality.

The Bankruptcy Rules Committee reached a different conclusion. It determined that full SSN redaction is **not practicable** in bankruptcy cases, given the operational necessity of allowing creditors to accurately identify debtors. Under the bankruptcy exception, Rule 9037 will continue to permit the last four digits of the debtor's SSN to appear in court filings ([COMMITTEE ON RULES OF PRACTICE AND PROCEDURE June 3-4, 2026](https://www.uscourts.gov/sites/default/files/document/2026-06_standing_committee_meeting_agenda_book_final.pdf)).

This creates a split treatment across procedural contexts: civil, criminal, and appellate courts would require full redaction, while bankruptcy courts would remain under the existing partial-redaction regime. Attorneys and litigants who practice across multiple contexts will need to maintain awareness of this distinction.

### Pseudonyms for Minors

The proposed amendment to FRCrP Rule 49.1(a)(3) — and corresponding provisions in the civil, appellate, and bankruptcy rules — would change how known minors are referenced in publicly accessible court filings. Currently, the rules require the use of "the initial letters" of a minor's name (e.g., "A.B."). The proposal would require the use of a **gender-neutral pseudonym** instead.

The DOJ's original suggestion highlighted a specific failure mode of the initials approach: in cases involving sexual exploitation of children, use of initials may still allow community members familiar with the case to identify the child, particularly in small communities or cases with significant media attention. Gender-neutral pseudonyms (e.g., "Alex" instead of "A.B.") delink the filing from the child's actual name and biological sex, providing an additional layer of protection.

The Bankruptcy, Civil, and Appellate Advisory Committees are also incorporating pseudonym requirements into their respective rules so that the protection carries forward consistently across all stages of federal court proceedings.

### Rulemaking Process and Timeline

The proposed amendments were presented to the Standing Committee at the June 3–4, 2026 meeting for approval to publish for public comment. The Judicial Conference's standard rulemaking cycle runs a public comment period from **August to February**, followed by consideration of comments and possible revision, approval by the Standing Committee and the Judicial Conference, and ultimate adoption by the Supreme Court, typically taking effect December 1 of the following year. If the amendments follow the standard schedule:

- Public comment period: August 2026 – February 2027
- Standing Committee consideration: Spring/Summer 2027
- Judicial Conference approval: Fall 2027
- Supreme Court adoption: December 1, 2027 (tentative)

The public comment period, once open, allows any person to submit written comments to the appropriate advisory committee. Comments may be filed through [uscourts.gov/rules-policies/records-rules-committees/rules-comments](https://www.uscourts.gov/rules-policies/records-rules-committees/rules-comments).

### Legislative Mandate and Reporting Obligations

The E-Government Act, Section 205(c)(3)(C), [H. Rept. 108-239](https://www.congress.gov/committee-report/108th-congress/house-report/239/1), requires the Judicial Conference to report to Congress every two years on the adequacy of the privacy rules. The 2024 biennial report — the most recent filed — documented ongoing compliance research, outreach efforts, and identified rule changes under consideration. The current rulemaking is a direct output of that biennial review process.

## Impact Assessment [MEDIUM confidence]

### Who is Affected

**All filers in federal civil, criminal, and appellate courts** will be subject to the enhanced redaction requirements if adopted, including:

- Private litigants and self-represented parties
- Attorneys and law firms
- Federal, state, and local government agencies filing in federal court
- Corporate filers in civil litigation

The bankruptcy exception means that **debtors and creditors in federal bankruptcy proceedings** will not face the new SSN redaction standard and can continue the existing partial-redaction practice.

### Compliance Requirements

The practical burden of the SSN change is low for most filers: the shift from redacting all but the last four digits to redacting the entire SSN requires a minor procedural update to document preparation workflows. Law firms with standardized document templates or e-filing automation tools will need to update those templates.

The pseudonym change for minors will require more careful attention in cases involving child witnesses or victims. Attorneys in criminal cases — particularly those involving crimes against children — will need to establish internal protocols for generating and consistently applying pseudonyms across filings, and for communicating those pseudonyms to opposing counsel and the court.

The existing requirement to file unredacted copies under seal (derived from E-Government Act Section 205(c)(3)(iv)) will remain in place, ensuring courts retain access to complete identifying information when needed.

### Enforcement and Non-Compliance Risk

The current rules already authorize sanctions for failure to redact. Courts have discretion to strike non-compliant filings or impose disciplinary measures on counsel. Clients whose SSNs are exposed due to counsel's failure to redact may have civil remedies depending on the circumstances.

The enhanced specificity of the new rules — complete SSN redaction, pseudonyms rather than initials — reduces ambiguity and may lower the threshold for enforcement action on technical violations.

### PACER and Mass Data Access

A key driver of the rulemaking is the recognition that PACER enables bulk downloading of court records at scale. Even where individual documents comply with partial-redaction rules, aggregated access to millions of documents creates data sets in which partial SSNs can be matched across filings to reconstruct full identifiers. Complete SSN redaction substantially reduces this risk, even though it does not eliminate all PACER-based privacy concerns.

## Action Items

- **Monitor comment period opening**: When the public comment period opens (expected August 2026), litigants, bar associations, and civil liberties organizations interested in commenting should submit written comments to the appropriate advisory committee via the [uscourts.gov comment portal](https://www.uscourts.gov/rules-policies/records-rules-committees/rules-comments).
- **Update document templates**: Begin reviewing standard form documents, pleadings, and e-filing macros to identify where partial SSNs are currently permitted. Plan updates to enforce complete redaction if the rule is adopted.
- **Review minor-identification protocols**: Attorneys in criminal, civil rights, immigration, and family law matters who regularly file documents referencing minors should develop pseudonym generation and tracking protocols aligned with the proposed gender-neutral pseudonym standard.
- **Track bankruptcy distinction**: Organizations with practice spanning both general federal court and bankruptcy court should develop separate compliance checklists reflecting the SSN carve-out for bankruptcy.
- **Monitor Judicial Conference proceedings**: Watch for Standing Committee minutes from the June 2026 meeting confirming formal publication of the proposed amendments and the opening of the comment period. Key source: [uscourts.gov/forms-rules/proposed-amendments-published-public-comment](https://www.uscourts.gov/forms-rules/proposed-amendments-published-public-comment).
