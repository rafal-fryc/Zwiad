# House Republicans Introduce SECURE Data Act (HR 8413): Comprehensive Federal Privacy Framework with Full State Preemption

**Jurisdiction:** Federal | **Category:** Privacy | **Date:** April 22, 2026

## Summary [HIGH confidence]

House Energy and Commerce Committee Vice Chairman John Joyce (R-PA) introduced HR 8413, the Securing and Establishing Consumer Uniform Rights and Enforcement over Data (SECURE) Act, on April 22, 2026 — the first comprehensive federal consumer privacy bill of the 119th Congress. The legislation proposes to preempt all state consumer privacy laws with a single national standard, granting consumers rights to access, correct, delete, and port their data and to opt out of targeted advertising and data sales, while exempting small businesses and notably omitting a private right of action. Republican committee leadership intends to advance the bill through committee on GOP votes first and seek bipartisan support afterward; the accompanying [GUARD Financial Data Act](https://financialservices.house.gov/uploadedfiles/2026-04-21_-_ec_fsc_data_privacy_final.pdf) covers the financial sector so the two bills together form a single national standard.

## Key Facts [HIGH confidence]

- **Introduction**: HR 8413 was introduced by Rep. John Joyce (R-PA), Vice Chairman of the House Energy and Commerce Committee, accompanied by eight Republican co-sponsors including Reps. Morgan Griffith (VA), Troy Balderson (OH), Jay Obernolte (CA), Russell Fry (SC), Nick Langworthy (NY), Tom Kean (NJ), Craig Goldman (TX), and Julie Fedorchak (ND), per the [official committee press release](https://republicans-energycommerce.house.gov/posts/committees-on-energy-and-commerce-and-financial-services-introduce-pair-of-privacy-bills-to-establish-comprehensive-data-protections-for-all-americans).

- **Legislative origin**: The bill embodies the work of a Privacy Working Group established in February 2025 by Chairman Brett Guthrie (R-KY), which issued a Request for Information on February 21, 2025 and received responses from at least 45 stakeholders including civil society organizations, state legislators, small businesses, industry groups, and state enforcement agencies, per [IAPP analysis](https://iapp.org/news/a/secure-data-act-analysis-of-the-new-federal-privacy-bill).

- **Applicability thresholds**: The bill covers any company that processes personal data of more than 200,000 US consumers, and exempts businesses with less than $25 million in adjusted gross annual revenue, per [IAPP analysis](https://iapp.org/news/a/secure-data-act-analysis-of-the-new-federal-privacy-bill).

- **Consumer rights**: Grants rights to access, correct, delete, and port personal data; opt out of targeted advertising, data sales, and automated profiling decisions made without human review, per [IAPP analysis](https://iapp.org/news/a/secure-data-act-analysis-of-the-new-federal-privacy-bill).

- **Sensitive data and children**: Treats personal data of children under 13 as sensitive data (alongside health and geolocation data), requiring opt-in consent; data about teens under age 16 triggers additional protections requiring parental consent for sensitive processing, per [IAPP analysis](https://iapp.org/news/a/secure-data-act-analysis-of-the-new-federal-privacy-bill).

- **Data broker registry**: Creates a registration and public registry system for data brokers administered by the FTC, per [IAPP analysis](https://iapp.org/news/a/secure-data-act-analysis-of-the-new-federal-privacy-bill).

- **Cross-border data flows**: Positions the Secretary of Commerce as the principal US advisor on cross-border data flows and the protection of personal data in international commerce, per [IAPP analysis](https://iapp.org/news/a/secure-data-act-analysis-of-the-new-federal-privacy-bill).

- **Preemption scope**: Section 15 of the bill would preempt any state law or provision that "relates to" its provisions — broadly covering state consumer privacy laws, data broker registries, and potentially some sectoral state laws — representing total preemption, per [IAPP analysis](https://iapp.org/news/a/secure-data-act-analysis-of-the-new-federal-privacy-bill).

- **Enforcement**: Enforcement shared between the FTC and state attorneys general; no private right of action, per [CNBC coverage](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/04/22/data-privacy-bill-congress-states.html) and [IAPP analysis](https://iapp.org/news/a/secure-data-act-analysis-of-the-new-federal-privacy-bill).

- **Implementation timeline**: If enacted, consumer rights, data security, and data broker requirements would take effect one year after enactment; most other provisions take effect two years after enactment, per [IAPP analysis](https://iapp.org/news/a/secure-data-act-analysis-of-the-new-federal-privacy-bill).

- **GUARD Financial Data Act**: The companion bill, introduced by House Financial Services Committee Chairman French Hill (R-AR), modernizes the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act (GLBA), expands covered data categories to include biometric data, geolocation, and access credentials, allows customers to request access and deletion of their financial data, and preempts state financial privacy laws, per [The Hill](https://thehill.com/policy/technology/5843269-secure-data-guard-financial-data-acts/) and the [Energy & Commerce / Financial Services joint fact sheet](https://financialservices.house.gov/uploadedfiles/2026-04-21_-_ec_fsc_data_privacy_final.pdf).

- **Industry support**: The National Association of Manufacturers welcomed the bills, stating that "a uniform national framework that is both forward-looking and adaptable to new technology" would "keep the industry innovating," per [NAM statement](https://nam.org/manufacturers-back-long-awaited-federal-action-on-data-privacy-36272/?stream=policy-legal).

- **Official bill text**: HR 8413 was introduced on April 22, 2026. The congress.gov page was not yet indexed at time of writing; the expected URL for manual verification is `https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-bill/8413`.

## Notable Omissions vs. Prior Federal Proposals and State Laws [HIGH confidence]

The SECURE Data Act makes several deliberate departures from the 2022 American Data Privacy and Protection Act (ADPPA) and the 2024 American Privacy Rights Act (APRA):

- **No private right of action**: The bill contains no private right of action, a provision Democrats have consistently demanded. Both the ADPPA and APRA included (in varying forms) a limited private right of action; its absence is the principal Democratic objection, per [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/04/22/data-privacy-bill-congress-states.html).

- **No universal opt-out mechanism (GPC)**: The bill does not require controllers to recognize universal opt-out signals such as the Global Privacy Control, which at least 11 of the 21 states with comprehensive privacy laws now mandate in some form, per [IAPP analysis](https://iapp.org/news/a/secure-data-act-analysis-of-the-new-federal-privacy-bill).

- **No data protection impact assessments or data protection officers**: The bill omits requirements for DPIAs and DPOs that feature in many state laws and the EU GDPR, per [IAPP analysis](https://iapp.org/news/a/secure-data-act-analysis-of-the-new-federal-privacy-bill).

- **Limited algorithmic decision-making rules**: Full ADMT (automated decision-making and profiling) rules are absent; a narrow opt-out from fully automated decisions with no human review is included, but broader algorithmic impact assessment obligations are not, per [IAPP analysis](https://iapp.org/news/a/secure-data-act-analysis-of-the-new-federal-privacy-bill).

- **Data minimization standard**: The bill requires data collection to be "adequate, relevant, and reasonably necessary" — similar to state law language — but critics argue this standard is weaker than genuine data minimization because companies can satisfy it through cookie banners and lengthy terms of service, per [IAPP analysis](https://iapp.org/news/a/secure-data-act-analysis-of-the-new-federal-privacy-bill).

## Legislative Strategy and Political Prospects [MEDIUM confidence]

Republican leadership's stated strategy — advance through committee on GOP votes, then court Democrats — mirrors the approach taken by the ADPPA in 2022, which passed committee 53-2 but died before a House floor vote due to a jurisdictional dispute with the Senate Commerce Committee and opposition from Speaker Pelosi over California preemption. The SECURE Data Act's even broader preemption (total, not floor-based) and absence of a private right of action make bipartisan passage harder, not easier:

- **Democratic objections**: The absence of a private right of action is a bright-line Democratic demand. Both Rep. Ro Khanna (D-CA) and Senate Democrats have previously insisted on private enforcement as a condition for support, per [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/04/22/data-privacy-bill-congress-states.html).

- **State opposition**: Total preemption language will face opposition from California and other states with strong privacy frameworks. The California Privacy Protection Agency has historically opposed federal preemption that reduces state protections; California's CPPA submitted comments to the Working Group's RFI opposing preemption.

- **Republican majority math**: With the House majority at razor-thin margins, Republican leaders need near-total GOP unity to clear committee and the floor without Democratic votes. Privacy remains a bipartisan issue where some Republicans (particularly from states with enacted privacy laws) may prefer keeping state flexibility, per [The Hill](https://thehill.com/policy/technology/5843269-secure-data-guard-financial-data-acts/).

- **Baker Botts analysis**: Characterized the bill as Republicans "reigniting efforts to pass a federal privacy law," noting the Working Group's extensive pre-legislative stakeholder engagement as a positive signal for legislative process, per [Baker Botts](https://ourtake.bakerbotts.com/post/102k15g/u-s-house-republicans-reignite-efforts-to-pass-federal-privacy-law).

## Comparison with ADPPA (2022) [MEDIUM confidence]

| Feature | ADPPA (2022) | SECURE Data Act (2026) |
|---|---|---|
| Preemption | Floor-based (states may go higher) | Total preemption |
| Private right of action | Limited (delayed 2 years) | None |
| Data minimization | Strong algorithmic loyalty duties | "Adequate, relevant, reasonably necessary" |
| Universal opt-out | Required | Not required |
| DPIA/DPO | Required for large covered entities | Not required |
| Sensitive data | Broad category including health, genetic, biometric | Health, geolocation, biometric; children under 13 |
| Youth data | KOSA-aligned protections | Under-13 = sensitive; under-16 = parental consent for sensitive |
| ADMT rules | Full opt-out + impact assessments | Narrow opt-out from fully automated decisions only |
| Enforcement | FTC + state AGs + private plaintiffs | FTC + state AGs only |
| Bipartisan | Bipartisan (Rodgers/Cantwell) | Republican-only introduction |

## Action Items

- **Immediate (now)**: Monitor the House Energy and Commerce Committee's markup schedule — Republican leadership has signaled an intent to advance quickly through committee. Subscribe to the [Energy & Commerce Committee](https://energycommerce.house.gov) press releases for hearing and markup announcements.

- **30 days**: If your organization operates in multiple states, map your compliance obligations under the 21 current state privacy laws against the SECURE Data Act's provisions. Total preemption would eliminate most state obligations, but the bill could change materially before any vote.

- **60 days**: Prepare two compliance scenarios: (1) SECURE Data Act passes substantially as introduced, and (2) the bill fails or is substantially amended, leaving state patchwork in place. The absence of a private right of action in the current draft means your litigation exposure posture would change significantly if enacted.

- **Key provision to watch**: Whether a private right of action is added during markup or floor debate. Its inclusion would substantially change the compliance and litigation risk calculus. Democrats have made this a condition for support.

- **Financial sector**: Separately track the GUARD Financial Data Act through the House Financial Services Committee — it would modernize GLBA and preempt state financial privacy rules including the California Financial Information Privacy Act (CalFIPA) and related state financial data laws.

- **Official text monitoring**: Once congress.gov indexes HR 8413, verify the official text at `https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-bill/8413`. The GUARD Financial Data Act companion bill is HR 2978 in the 119th Congress.
