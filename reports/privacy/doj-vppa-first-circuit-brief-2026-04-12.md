# DOJ Urges First Circuit to Uphold VPPA Against First Amendment Challenge

## Executive Summary [HIGH confidence]

The U.S. Department of Justice has intervened in the First Circuit appeal of a Video Privacy Protection Act (VPPA) class action against Hearst Television — *Therrien v. Hearst Television, Inc.*, No. 25-1487 (1st Cir., appeal filed May 21, 2025) — filing a brief that urges the court to reject Hearst's argument that the VPPA violates the First Amendment. The DOJ characterizes the VPPA as a content-neutral privacy statute that protects sensitive information "without regard to the content of any message in which the disclosure might be contained" and advances a substantial government interest in consumer privacy and intellectual freedom. The brief was first reported by MediaPost on April 6, 2026 ([MediaPost, "Trump Admin To Court: Don't Strike Down Video Privacy Act"](https://www.mediapost.com/publications/article/414084/trump-admin-to-court-dont-strike-down-video-priv.html)).

## Background: The Hearst / Saunders / Therrien Litigation [HIGH confidence]

The appeal arises from VPPA class-action litigation originally captioned *Saunders v. Hearst Television, Inc.*, No. 1:23-cv-10998, filed in the U.S. District Court for the District of Massachusetts in 2023 ([CourtListener docket](https://www.courtlistener.com/docket/67334038/saunders-v-hearst-television-inc/)). The named plaintiffs alleged that Hearst's television news apps disclosed users' video-viewing histories, device identifiers, and geolocation data to Google Ad Manager and Braze without consent, in violation of the VPPA, 18 U.S.C. § 2710 ([FindLaw case page](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/us-dis-crt-d-mas/115708870.html)).

Judge Richard Stearns (D. Mass.) **granted summary judgment** to Hearst in April 2025, holding that plaintiff Therrien failed to present sufficient evidence that the data Hearst disclosed constituted personally identifiable information (PII) under the VPPA ([Law360, "Hearst Wins Toss Of VPPA Suit Over News App Data Sharing"](https://www.law360.com/articles/2330320/hearst-wins-toss-of-vppa-suit-over-news-app-data-sharing)). This is a summary-judgment disposition on the PII element, procedurally distinct from a Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal and materially relevant to the scope of the appeal. Therrien appealed to the First Circuit on May 21, 2025, where the case is now docketed as *Therrien v. Hearst Television, Inc.*, No. 25-1487 (1st Cir.).

On appeal, Hearst is pressing a First Amendment argument as an alternative ground for affirmance. Specifically, Hearst contends that the VPPA is both a **content-based** restriction on speech (because it applies only to disclosures concerning prerecorded video materials, and not to other categories of expressive content) and a **speaker-based** restriction (because it burdens only "video tape service providers" and not other disclosers of comparable information). On Hearst's theory, either feature — and certainly both together — triggers strict scrutiny, which the statute could not survive. Hearst further argues that applying the VPPA to its disclosures would impose a "sweeping restriction on routine, non-public communications essential to modern digital journalism." It is precisely this content-based / speaker-based framing that the DOJ's brief is designed to rebut.

## The DOJ's Position [HIGH confidence]

Per reporting on the filing, the DOJ brief makes three principal points ([MediaPost, April 6, 2026](https://www.mediapost.com/publications/article/414084/trump-admin-to-court-dont-strike-down-video-priv.html); earlier district-court-phase reporting at [MediaPost, "DOJ Sides Against Hearst In Battle Over Video Privacy Law"](https://www.mediapost.com/publications/article/404945/doj-sides-against-hearst-in-battle-over-video-priv.html)):

1. **Constitutional avoidance.** The government urges the First Circuit to decide the appeal on non-constitutional grounds if possible, noting that "it is not at all clear that any constitutional question is presented here."
2. **Content neutrality.** If the court reaches the First Amendment question, the DOJ argues the VPPA is a privacy statute that regulates disclosure without regard to message content, and therefore is not subject to strict scrutiny as a content-based speech restriction. This directly answers Hearst's content-based / speaker-based framing: DOJ characterizes the statute as regulating the act of disclosure of a defined category of sensitive personal information, not the communicative content of any particular video or message.
3. **Substantial government interest.** The VPPA, the DOJ contends, "serves the government's substantial interest in protecting consumer privacy and intellectual freedom, directly advances that interest, and is no more extensive than necessary to serve that interest" — tracking the intermediate-scrutiny standard under *Central Hudson* / *Sorrell*-type frameworks.

The DOJ had previously taken a similar position at the district court level, emphasizing that even business-to-business disclosures can reveal sensitive inferences — for example, weather videos revealing location or travel plans, and political videos revealing affiliation ([MediaPost district-court coverage](https://www.mediapost.com/publications/article/404945/doj-sides-against-hearst-in-battle-over-video-priv.html)).

## Why This Matters [HIGH confidence]

The VPPA, 18 U.S.C. § 2710, originally enacted in 1988 in response to the disclosure of Judge Robert Bork's video rental records ([Video Privacy Protection Act — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Video_Privacy_Protection_Act)), has become one of the most heavily litigated federal privacy statutes of the past three years. Hundreds of class actions are pending against streaming services, publishers, and app operators based on disclosures of viewing data to advertising and analytics partners such as Meta Pixel, Google, and Braze ([WilmerHale, "2025 Year In Review: VPPA Litigation Trends"](https://www.wilmerhale.com/en/insights/blogs/wilmerhale-privacy-and-cybersecurity-law/20260123-2025-year-in-review-video-privacy-protection-act-litigation-trends); [SeyfarthShaw / Privacy World, "2025 VPPA Litigation Year in Review"](https://www.privacyworld.blog/2025/12/2025-video-privacy-protection-act-litigation-year-in-review/)).

A First Amendment ruling striking down the statute, or narrowing it on constitutional grounds, would eliminate or dramatically curtail this litigation wave. The DOJ's intervention signals that the executive branch — notwithstanding the administration's broader preemption-focused approach to state privacy laws — views the VPPA as a valid federal privacy baseline worth defending.

The First Circuit case also converges with a parallel track at the Supreme Court. On January 26, 2026, the Court granted certiorari in *Salazar v. Paramount Global*, a Sixth Circuit case that will resolve a circuit split over who qualifies as a "consumer" under the VPPA, with argument expected in OT2026-27 ([Privacy World, "Supreme Court Agrees to Resolve VPPA Circuit Split"](https://www.privacyworld.blog/2026/02/supreme-court-agrees-to-resolve-vppa-circuit-split/); [Ropes & Gray, "Supreme Court to Consider the Video Privacy Protection Act"](https://www.ropesgray.com/en/insights/alerts/2026/02/supreme-court-to-consider-the-video-privacy-protection-act); [SCOTUSblog, "Supreme Court agrees to hear case on digital privacy"](https://www.scotusblog.com/2026/01/supreme-court-agrees-to-hear-case-on-digital-privacy-reverses-ruling-ordering-new-murder-trial/)). Together, *Salazar* and *Therrien* may reshape VPPA exposure across standing, scope, and constitutionality in the next 12 months.

Hearst Television, Inc.*, No. 25-1487 (1st Cir.), has not been independently retrieved from the First Circuit docket or PACER for this report; quotations and characterizations of the brief are drawn from contemporaneous news reporting. The First Circuit appellate docket should be checked directly (via PACER or the court's CM/ECF system) for the full text of the DOJ filing and Hearst's merits briefs articulating the content-based / speaker-based First Amendment theory.

## Action Items

- **VPPA defendants and their counsel** in pending class actions (particularly in the First Circuit) should monitor the *Therrien v. Hearst*, No. 25-1487 appeal docket for the DOJ brief itself and any responsive briefing, and assess whether to preserve or coordinate First Amendment defenses of their own — including the content-based and speaker-based framing Hearst has advanced.
- **Media, streaming, and app operators** should not assume that a First Amendment defense will remain viable; the DOJ's brief significantly raises the bar for any court to adopt Hearst's strict-scrutiny theory.
- **Compliance teams** should continue VPPA-focused data-sharing audits (Pixel, SDKs, ad tech disclosures) on the assumption that the VPPA remains enforceable in its current form, while tracking *Salazar v. Paramount Global* at the Supreme Court for scope changes to the "consumer" definition.
- **Privacy class-action plaintiffs' counsel** gain a strong amicus-like endorsement from DOJ that can be cited in parallel VPPA matters raising First Amendment defenses. Note, however, that the summary-judgment posture of *Therrien* means the First Circuit's review will focus on the PII evidentiary record as well as the constitutional question.
