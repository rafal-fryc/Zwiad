# Ninth Circuit Partially Maintains Block of California Kids' Privacy Law: DPIA Requirement Struck Down as Compelled Speech

**Jurisdiction:** California | **Category:** Privacy | **Date:** August 20, 2024

## Executive Summary [HIGH confidence]

On August 16, 2024, the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit issued a divided ruling in [*NetChoice, LLC v. Bonta*, No. 23-2969](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/ca9/23-2969/23-2969-2024-08-16.html), partially affirming and partially vacating a district court preliminary injunction against California's Age-Appropriate Design Code Act (CAADCA), Assembly Bill 2273. The court affirmed the injunction as to the law's Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA) requirement, finding it likely unconstitutional compelled speech under the First Amendment that fails strict scrutiny. For all remaining CAADCA provisions — including default privacy settings, geolocation restrictions, and dark-pattern prohibitions — the court vacated the district court's broader injunction and remanded for further analysis. The practical effect is that the DPIA mandate remains blocked indefinitely, while most other CAADCA obligations now face fresh constitutional scrutiny in the district court on remand. Businesses that were preparing for the law's July 1, 2024 enforcement date must reassess compliance timelines as the litigation continues.

## Background [HIGH confidence]

### The California Age-Appropriate Design Code Act (AB 2273)

California enacted AB 2273, the California Age-Appropriate Design Code Act (CAADCA), in September 2022 when [Governor Gavin Newsom signed it into law](https://www.gov.ca.gov/2022/09/15/governor-newsom-signs-first-in-nation-bill-protecting-childrens-online-data-and-privacy/) with unanimous legislative support. The CAADCA is modeled on the United Kingdom's Age-Appropriate Design Code (commonly called the "Children's Code") and represented the first law of its kind enacted in the United States. The law was scheduled to take effect on July 1, 2024.

The CAADCA applies broadly to any business subject to the California Consumer Privacy Act (CCPA) that provides an online service, product, or feature "likely to be accessed by children" — defined as users under age 18. Crucially, the Act does not limit its reach to platforms specifically directed at children; instead, liability attaches based on whether children routinely or foreseeably access a platform, determined by factors including audience composition data, design elements known to appeal to minors, and internal research. This broad scope was expected to sweep in social media platforms, video streaming services, gaming platforms, search engines, and many other services not traditionally classified as children's platforms.

### Key CAADCA Obligations

The [official text of AB 2273](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/billTextClient.xhtml?bill_id=202120220AB2273) imposes several categories of requirements on covered businesses:

1. **Data Protection Impact Assessments (DPIAs):** Before offering any online service or feature likely to be accessed by children, businesses must complete a DPIA identifying the purpose of the service, how children's personal information will be used, risks of material detriment to children, and a plan to mitigate identified risks. DPIAs must be reviewed biennially and produced to the California Attorney General within five business days of a written request.

2. **Default Privacy Settings:** All default privacy settings must be configured to the highest level of privacy protection unless the business can demonstrate a compelling reason that a less protective default is in children's best interests.

3. **Geolocation Restrictions:** Precise geolocation collection is prohibited by default and may only occur where strictly necessary to provide the service, for the minimum duration necessary, and with an active on-screen indicator provided to the child during collection.

4. **Data Minimization and Prohibitions:** Businesses may not collect, sell, share, or retain personal information beyond what is necessary to provide the service; may not use dark patterns to encourage children to provide more personal data than necessary; and may not profile children in ways that could harm their physical, mental, or emotional health without a compelling reason.

5. **Harmful Content Restrictions:** Businesses may not recommend content to children that could harm them or expose them to harmful contacts unless strictly necessary to provide the service.

### The NetChoice Lawsuit and District Court Injunction

[NetChoice, LLC](https://netchoice.org/netchoice-v-bonta/), an industry trade association whose members include major platforms, filed suit in the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California in December 2022. NetChoice argued that the CAADCA violates the First Amendment by compelling speech, chilling lawful expression, and deputizing private businesses as censors. The case was assigned to Judge Beth Labson Freeman.

On September 18, 2023, Judge Freeman granted NetChoice's motion for a [preliminary injunction (Case No. 5:22-cv-08861)](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/district-courts/california/candce/5:2022cv08861/406140/74/), blocking the entire CAADCA from taking effect pending further proceedings. The district court found that NetChoice was likely to prevail on its First Amendment claims and that the Act's DPIA requirement in particular constituted impermissible compelled speech. California appealed.

## Detailed Analysis [HIGH confidence]

### The August 16, 2024 Ninth Circuit Opinion

The Ninth Circuit panel, in an opinion authored by Judge Milan D. Smith, Jr., applied a carefully bounded analysis to each category of CAADCA provisions challenged by NetChoice.

#### DPIA Requirement: Affirmed as Unconstitutionally Compelled Speech

The court's most decisive holding concerned the DPIA obligation. The panel concluded that the DPIA requirement "clearly compels speech by requiring covered businesses to opine on potential harms to children" and "deputizes covered businesses into serving as censors for the State." Because the DPIA mandate forces platforms to analyze and document whether their content and design features could harm children — a content-based evaluation — it implicates the First Amendment's protections against compelled speech.

Applying strict scrutiny (the most demanding constitutional standard), the Ninth Circuit found that the DPIA requirement likely fails:

- **Compelled speech:** The requirement that businesses identify whether their platforms expose children to harmful content is, in effect, an order to engage in content evaluation — activity protected by the First Amendment.
- **Not least restrictive means:** The court found that California "could have easily employed less restrictive means to accomplish its protective goals" without mandating that private businesses produce written assessments of their own content's potential harms. This failure of narrow tailoring is fatal under strict scrutiny.

The court therefore affirmed the district court's injunction against the DPIA requirement and all provisions that are not grammatically severable from it. The DPIA block remains in effect indefinitely pending the underlying merits proceedings.

The [Electronic Frontier Foundation and ACLU](https://www.aclu.org/press-releases/aclu-cheers-ninth-circuit-decision-to-block-content-based-provisions-of-california-age-appropriate-design-code-act) publicly supported this aspect of the ruling, viewing the DPIA's content-evaluation component as an impermissible government mandate to identify and police speech.

#### Remaining Provisions: Injunction Vacated, Remanded for Further Analysis

For all provisions other than the DPIA requirement — including default privacy settings, geolocation restrictions, dark-pattern prohibitions, and content recommendation rules — the Ninth Circuit vacated the district court's preliminary injunction and remanded the case for further analysis.

The appellate court found the district court had taken an insufficiently granular approach to the facial challenge. Rather than conducting provision-by-provision analysis, the district court had broadly enjoined the entire statute after finding the DPIA unconstitutional. The Ninth Circuit held that this was error: the constitutionality of each provision must be assessed on its own terms, including whether unconstitutional applications (if any) substantially outweigh constitutional ones.

Specifically regarding provisions like the dark-pattern prohibitions and high-default-privacy requirements, the court found that the record did not support the conclusion that their unconstitutional applications — to the extent any exist — would substantially outweigh their constitutional ones. The court left open the question of whether any remaining provisions might ultimately be severable or independently unconstitutional, directing the district court to conduct that analysis on remand.

The practical consequence of vacating the broader injunction is significant: with the district-court injunction covering non-DPIA provisions lifted, California could theoretically begin enforcing those provisions unless the district court reimposed an injunction on remand. [Wiley Law's analysis](https://www.wiley.law/alert-Injunction-on-California-AADC-Partially-Vacated-Key-Provisions-May-Take-Effect-on-April-2) noted the potential for provisions to become effective on specific remand dates depending on court scheduling, adding compliance uncertainty.

### Legal Framework: Strict Scrutiny and Compelled Speech Doctrine

The DPIA ruling applies two interlocking First Amendment doctrines:

**Compelled speech:** Under *Wooley v. Maynard* and its progeny, the government generally may not compel private actors to express views they would not otherwise hold. The Ninth Circuit treated the DPIA's requirement to identify and document content-based harm as a form of compelled editorial judgment, not merely a ministerial compliance record.

**Strict scrutiny for content-based speech regulations:** Because the DPIA directs businesses to make assessments based on the content of their platforms, it is a content-based regulation triggering strict scrutiny. Under that standard, the law must serve a compelling interest through the least restrictive means available. The court did not dispute the compelling interest (protecting children online), but found the means insufficiently tailored.

### Relationship to *Moody v. NetChoice*

The August 2024 CAADCA opinion was issued against the backdrop of *Moody v. NetChoice, LLC*, 144 S. Ct. 2383, decided by the U.S. Supreme Court in June 2024. In *Moody*, the Supreme Court remanded related social media regulatory challenges with guidance on how courts should conduct facial challenges under the First Amendment — including the requirement for provision-level, application-by-application analysis. The Ninth Circuit's CAADCA opinion implicitly reflected this framework by criticizing the district court's broad, undifferentiated injunction and demanding more granular analysis on remand.

## Impact Assessment [MEDIUM confidence]

### Immediate Effects

The August 16, 2024 ruling produced two immediate practical effects:

1. **DPIA requirement remains blocked.** No covered business is required to complete a data protection impact assessment under the CAADCA during the pending litigation. The California Attorney General may not demand DPIA production.

2. **Uncertainty about other provisions.** With the district court's broader injunction vacated for non-DPIA provisions, businesses face ambiguity about whether and when other CAADCA requirements will be enforced. [Wilson Sonsini's analysis](https://www.wsgr.com/en/insights/ninth-circuit-ruling-paves-the-way-for-california-age-appropriate-design-code-to-partially-come-into-effect.html) characterized the ruling as "paving the way" for the remaining provisions to come into effect, contingent on district court proceedings.

### Affected Businesses

The CAADCA's coverage of services "likely to be accessed by children" — rather than services directed at children — casts a wide net. Potentially covered businesses include:

- Major social media platforms (Instagram, TikTok, YouTube, Snapchat)
- Search engines and general-purpose internet services
- Gaming platforms and mobile app stores
- Streaming services with broad audience demographics
- News and educational platforms with significant minor user bases

The law's enforcer is the California Attorney General, with civil penalties of up to $2,500 per affected child per negligent violation and $7,500 per intentional violation.

### Broader State Law Landscape

The CAADCA's legal troubles have not deterred other states. Maryland enacted its own [Age-Appropriate Design Code (HB 603)](reports/privacy/childrens-privacy/maryland-hb603-aadc-kids-code-2024-04-22.md) in 2024, and [New York](reports/privacy/childrens-privacy/new-york-safe-kids-child-data-anprm-2024-08-19.md) has advanced similar children's privacy legislation. These laws face analogous constitutional arguments, and the CAADCA litigation's outcomes will likely be cited as precedent in those jurisdictions. If the DPIA requirement in CAADCA is ultimately invalidated on the merits, DPIA requirements in other state children's codes face similar First Amendment exposure.

## Action Items

- **Suspend DPIA preparation under the CAADCA.** The DPIA requirement remains enjoined. Companies need not prepare or submit DPIAs to the California AG under CAADCA during the pending litigation. (Note: DPIAs may still be required under other legal frameworks, such as emerging CPPA regulations on Automated Decision-Making Technology.)
- **Assess compliance with non-DPIA CAADCA provisions.** The injunction against provisions such as default-privacy settings, geolocation restrictions, and dark-pattern prohibitions has been vacated. Evaluate exposure under these provisions and monitor district court proceedings on remand for new injunction rulings.
- **Monitor the remand proceedings in the Northern District of California (Case No. 5:22-cv-08861).** The district court may issue a new, more targeted preliminary injunction covering specific provisions. Compliance deadlines depend on the remand schedule.
- **Review coverage analysis.** Confirm whether your online services are "likely to be accessed by children" under the CAADCA's indicators-based standard. This threshold determination governs whether the law applies at all.
- **Track parallel state legislation.** Maryland's AADC and analogous legislation in other states may have different constitutional postures and enforcement timelines. Monitor each jurisdiction separately.
- **Prepare contingency DPIA documentation.** Although DPIAs are currently enjoined under the CAADCA, if the injunction is ultimately lifted on the merits, enforcement exposure is retroactive. Building compliant DPIA processes now reduces future compliance risk.
