# AI Apps Enabling Teenage Non-Consensual Deepfakes: Federal and State Regulatory Response

**Jurisdiction:** Federal, Multi-state | **Category:** Privacy — Children's Privacy | **Date:** 2026-06-15

## Executive Summary [MEDIUM confidence]

AI applications accessible to minors — most prominently xAI's Grok chatbot — have been used by teenagers to generate non-consensual intimate deepfake images of peers, triggering a wave of federal and state regulatory action. The problem crystallized publicly in late December 2025 when researchers documented that Grok generated an estimated three million sexualized images in eleven days, approximately 23,000 of which depicted apparent minors. Federal and state enforcement has since accelerated on multiple tracks: the TAKE IT DOWN Act (signed May 2025) imposed criminal penalties and a platform removal-duty framework now being actively enforced by the FTC; the DEFIANCE Act, which passed the Senate in January 2026 and remains pending in the House, would add federal civil remedies; and the KIDS Act, on which the House Energy and Commerce Committee struck a bipartisan deal on June 22, 2026, consolidates more than a dozen child-safety bills including the DEFIANCE Act's deepfake provisions. State attorneys general from 47 jurisdictions have demanded action from search engines and payment processors, and California issued a cease-and-desist order to xAI directly. Multiple private class-action lawsuits are proceeding in federal and state courts. The regulatory landscape is developing rapidly, and compliance obligations for platforms offering generative AI tools will tighten materially once pending legislation clears Congress.

## Background [HIGH confidence]

The generation of non-consensual intimate imagery (NCII) — including AI-fabricated "deepfake" depictions — predates the current wave of teen-focused incidents, but the problem scaled dramatically as consumer-grade generative AI became widely accessible. By late 2025, AI image-generation tools capable of producing photorealistic sexualized images were embedded in mainstream commercial products rather than confined to specialized technical communities.

The incident that catalyzed the current enforcement wave involved xAI's Grok chatbot. Between December 29, 2025 and January 8, 2026, researchers at the [Center for Countering Digital Hate (CCDH)](https://counterhate.com) documented Grok generating approximately three million sexualized images, including an estimated 23,000 depicting apparent children. (On January 9, 2026, xAI restricted Grok's image-generation feature to paid users.) An investigation by [Reuters](https://www.reuters.com) found that Grok bypassed its own safety filters in 45 of 55 controlled test attempts to generate sexualized images of real people. Grok's late-2025 updates had integrated a more permissive image-generation model susceptible to "jailbreaking" through coded language.

The Grok incident was not isolated. AI-powered "nudify" applications — tools that generate nude or sexualized versions of clothed images of real people — have proliferated as web-accessible services. A [bipartisan coalition of 47 state and territory attorneys general](https://www.naag.org/press-releases/state-and-territory-attorneys-general-urge-tech-and-payment-platforms-to-address-deepfake-exploitation/) found that 98% of fake videos circulating online are deepfake NCII. Teenagers have used these tools to victimize peers, with perpetrators sourcing source images from school yearbooks, social media accounts, and homecoming photos before creating and distributing the fabricated images on platforms such as Telegram and Discord.

The federal statutory framework addressing this harm prior to 2025 was thin. Child Sexual Abuse Material (CSAM) laws covered some AI-generated imagery of minors, but coverage of non-nude sexualized images, distribution via novel platforms, and creation tools themselves was legally contested. State legislatures began filling this gap beginning around 2022; by August 2025, [48 states had enacted some form of NCII/sexually explicit deepfake legislation](https://www.multistate.ai/nonconsensual-deepfake-laws) — Michigan was the 48th state, enacting HB 4047-4048 on August 26, 2025 — though coverage and penalty structures varied widely across jurisdictions.

## Detailed Analysis [HIGH confidence]

### The TAKE IT DOWN Act (Signed Law)

The most significant federal legislative development to date is the [TAKE IT DOWN Act](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/senate-bill/146) (Tools to Address Known Exploitation by Immobilizing Technological Deepfakes on Websites and Networks Act), [S. 146, 119th Congress](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/senate-bill/146), signed into law by President Trump on May 19, 2025. The law has two main operative tracks:

**Criminal prohibitions (effective immediately upon signing):** The Act criminalizes the publication of NCII, including AI-generated deepfakes. Violations carry up to two years of imprisonment; offenses where the victim is a minor carry enhanced penalties of up to three years.

**Platform notice-and-removal obligations (effective May 19, 2026):** Covered platforms — broadly defined to include websites and social media services — must provide a mechanism for victims to request removal of NCII, process those requests within 48 hours, and delete known identical copies. The [FTC began enforcing these obligations on May 19, 2026](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2026/05/ftc-begins-enforcing-take-it-down-act), simultaneously launching [takeitdown.ftc.gov](https://takeitdown.ftc.gov) for victim complaints. Violations of the notice-and-removal requirements are treated as unfair or deceptive trade practices under the FTC Act, with civil penalties of up to $53,088 per violation. [Congressional Research Service analysis](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/LSB11314) confirms this enforcement structure.

FTC Chairman Andrew Ferguson immediately invoked the new authority: on May 19 and 20, 2026, the [FTC sent warning letters to 12 "nudify" websites](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2026/05/ftc-sends-warning-letters-companies-about-compliance-take-it-down-act) asserting apparent noncompliance with the removal-request obligation. The letters asserted that these tools — which generate sexualized images of clothed individuals without consent — fall squarely within the Act's scope.

### The DEFIANCE Act (Senate-Passed, House-Pending)

The [DEFIANCE Act of 2025](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/senate-bill/1837/text) (S. 1837, 119th Congress) complements the TAKE IT DOWN Act's criminal and platform-removal framework by creating a federal civil cause of action. The Senate passed the DEFIANCE Act unanimously on January 13, 2026. As of late June 2026, the bill remains held at the desk in the House pending further consideration.

Key civil remedy provisions include statutory damages up to $150,000 per violation (or $250,000 when linked to sexual assault, stalking, or harassment), as well as a ten-year statute of limitations that is tolled until the victim discovers the violation or turns 18, whichever is later. The tolling provision is particularly significant for minor victims, who frequently do not discover the existence of fabricated imagery for months or years after creation.

### The KIDS Act — June 2026 House Deal

On June 22, 2026, [House Energy and Commerce Committee Chairman Brett Guthrie (R-KY) and Ranking Member Frank Pallone Jr. (D-NJ) announced a bipartisan deal on the Kids Internet and Digital Safety (KIDS) Act](https://energycommerce.house.gov/posts/chairman-guthrie-and-ranking-member-pallone-announce-agreement-on-bipartisan-kids-safety-package). The announcement date is confirmed by the [Washington Times](https://www.washingtontimes.com/news/2026/jun/22/house-committee-leaders-strike-elusive-bipartisan-deal-kids-online/), [Roll Call](https://rollcall.com/2026/06/22/bipartisan-changes-expected-to-kids-internet-safety-bill/), and [Congressman Pallone's own press release](https://pallone.house.gov/media/press-releases/pallone-strikes-bipartisan-deal-protect-kids-online-and-rein-big-tech). The consolidated package, H.R. 7757, incorporates more than a dozen child-safety bills into a single legislative vehicle, including:

- **KOSA** (Kids Online Safety Act) — requires platforms to establish policies to prevent harms to minors, though the House version omits the "duty of care" provision that was in prior Senate versions;
- **COPPA 2.0** — extends federal children's online privacy protection from under-13 to under-17, strengthens the knowledge standard, bans targeted advertising to covered minors, and creates deletion rights;
- **DEFIANCE Act** provisions — the deepfake civil remedies bill is incorporated into the KIDS Act package;
- **SCREEN Act** — requires age verification for access to adult content websites;
- **SAFE Bots Act** — requires AI chatbots interacting with minors to provide disclosures and safety resources;
- **STOP CSAM Act** — creates civil liability for platforms that host child sexual abuse material.

The [Axios](https://www.axios.com/2026/06/24/kids-online-safety-push-complicated) and [The Hill](https://thehill.com/policy/technology/5938935-house-breakthrough-on-kids-online-safety-faces-long-odds-in-senate/) report that the House and Senate remain far apart on key provisions. A significant complicating factor: [White House and Senate efforts to attach state AI law preemption language to the kids' safety package](https://www.biometricupdate.com/202606/white-house-senate-revive-push-to-block-state-ai-laws-through-kids-safety-deal) have created a fresh political flashpoint. The KIDS Act has not yet passed the full House, and Senate floor time remains uncertain.

### COPPA 2.0 — Senate Passage

Separately, [COPPA 2.0 (S. 836, 119th Congress)](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/senate-bill/836/text) passed the Senate by unanimous consent on March 5, 2026. Senator Markey's office [confirmed](https://www.markey.senate.gov/news/press-releases/senator-markey-celebrates-unanimous-senate-passage-of-his-bipartisan-children-and-teens-online-privacy-protection-legislation) the legislation represents the most significant update to federal children's online privacy law since the original COPPA in 1998. Key changes include: extending protections to users under 17 (up from the current under-13 threshold), replacing "actual knowledge" of a user's age with a "knowledge fairly implied on the basis of objective circumstances" standard, prohibiting targeted advertising to covered minors, and creating rights of deletion. COPPA 2.0 has not yet been taken up in the House as a standalone bill; it is subsumed within the KIDS Act consolidation effort.

### State Enforcement Actions

**California AG cease-and-desist against xAI:** On January 16, 2026, [California Attorney General Rob Bonta sent a cease-and-desist letter to xAI](https://oag.ca.gov/news/press-releases/attorney-general-bonta-sends-cease-and-desist-letter-xai-demands-it-halt-illegal), demanding immediate cessation of Grok's generation and distribution of NCII and CSAM. The letter demanded a compliance response within five business days and cited California's existing criminal and civil statutes governing NCII.

**47-State AG coalition:** A [bipartisan coalition of 47 state and territory attorneys general](https://www.naag.org/press-releases/state-and-territory-attorneys-general-urge-tech-and-payment-platforms-to-address-deepfake-exploitation/) issued coordinated letters to major search engines (Google, Microsoft/Bing, Yahoo) and payment processors (Visa, Mastercard, American Express, PayPal, Apple Pay, Google Pay) demanding stronger action against deepfake NCII platforms. The coalition was co-led by Pennsylvania AG Dave Sunday, Kentucky AG Russell Coleman, Massachusetts AG Andrea Campbell, New Jersey AG Matthew Platkin, and Utah AG Derek Brown, with letters authored by Vermont AG Charity Clark. The letters urged search platforms to de-prioritize deepfake NCII content and warning pages, and called on payment processors to enforce acceptable-use policies against platforms selling deepfake tools.

**New Jersey AG — xAI direct letter:** New Jersey Attorney General Matthew Platkin sent a separate [letter to xAI](https://www.njoag.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/2026-0123_Letter-to-xAI_FINAL.pdf) in January 2026, demanding an accounting of safeguards in place to prevent Grok from generating CSAM and sexualized depictions of minors.

**Illinois AG urges industry action:** Illinois AG Kwame Raoul separately [urged](https://illinoisattorneygeneral.gov/news/story/attorney-general-raoul-urges-tech-companies-to-stop-the-spread-of-deepfake-nonconsensual-intimate-imagery) major tech companies to adopt stricter controls against deepfake NCII.

### Private Litigation

Multiple class-action lawsuits against xAI are proceeding:

- Three Tennessee teenagers filed a [proposed class action in California federal court](https://www.findlaw.com/legalblogs/courtside/tennessee-teens-bring-class-action-suit-against-elon-musk-and-xai-for-fake-sexualized-images/) in March 2026 alleging xAI's Grok was used to generate explicit images of minors sourced from yearbooks and social media. The complaint asserts 13 counts including distribution of child pornography and intentional infliction of emotional distress.
- [Baltimore filed a separate suit](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/03/24/musk-xai-sued-baltimore-grok-deepfake-porn.html) in Baltimore City Circuit Court, becoming the first municipality to sue over Grok deepfake pornography.
- A broader class action coordinated by [Lieff Cabraser](https://www.lieffcabraser.com/ai-deepfakes/) encompasses additional minor victims.

As of late June 2026, [reporting by TechTimes](https://www.techtimes.com/articles/319142/20260626/grok-adult-content-tops-10-billion-images-monthly-xai-engineers-admit-csam-has-no-fix.htm) indicates that Grok's adult content generation has reached ten billion images monthly, with xAI engineers acknowledging unresolved CSAM filtering problems — a disclosure that significantly strengthens plaintiffs' negligence and products-liability theories.

## Impact Assessment [MEDIUM confidence]

### Affected Platforms and Industries

The regulatory and litigation exposure falls most heavily on:

1. **Generative AI image tools and chatbots:** Any consumer-facing AI platform with image-generation capabilities is now subject to the TAKE IT DOWN Act's criminal prohibition on NCII production, the platform removal-duty (enforced by FTC since May 19, 2026), and potential civil liability under the pending DEFIANCE Act. Platforms with demonstrably inadequate safeguards — including content filters that can be bypassed — face compounded legal exposure.

2. **"Nudify" and NCII-as-a-service sites:** The FTC's May 2026 warning letters specifically targeted 12 such websites. These operators face imminent FTC enforcement action if they have not implemented compliant removal processes, with penalties of up to $53,088 per violation. Continued operation of nudify services as currently structured appears legally untenable.

3. **Social media and content-hosting platforms:** Platforms on which deepfake NCII is distributed (Telegram, Discord, X, Reddit) face overlapping obligations under the TAKE IT DOWN Act's removal duty, state NCII laws, and potential secondary liability under STOP CSAM provisions if the KIDS Act passes.

4. **Payment processors and search engines:** The 47-AG coalition's letters put payment processors and search platforms on notice that failure to act against deepfake NCII platforms may be treated as facilitation and could be used to justify regulatory scrutiny under existing unfair-practices authorities.

### Compliance Timeline

| Obligation | Legal Basis | Deadline / Status |

### Enforcement Outlook

FTC enforcement is already underway and will intensify. The Commission has limited its initial TAKE IT DOWN Act actions to warning letters and the consumer complaint portal, but formal enforcement actions with civil penalties are expected against non-responsive nudify operators. Under Commissioner Andrew Ferguson, the FTC has signaled an expansive view of its unfair-and-deceptive-practices authority over AI tools that harm users, including minors.

State AG enforcement is active and multifront. California's cease-and-desist against xAI and the 47-state coalition letters represent coordinated pressure that can lead to formal investigative subpoenas and state-law enforcement actions under existing NCII and consumer protection statutes. As of August 2025, 48 states have enacted NCII/sexually explicit deepfake legislation (with Michigan becoming the 48th state on August 26, 2025); of those, at least 45 had enacted such laws as of mid-2025 before Michigan and two other states acted. Enforcement resources are scaling across these jurisdictions.

Private litigation against xAI is in early stages but has gathered significant momentum. If the Tennessee class action survives a motion to dismiss, discovery into Grok's content moderation failures could produce evidence damaging to xAI across all pending suits.

## Action Items

- **Audit generative AI image features immediately** to verify that existing content filters cannot be bypassed using coded language ("jailbreaking"), and document the audit results to establish a due-diligence record against negligence claims.
- **Implement TAKE IT DOWN Act-compliant removal processes** if not already in place — the May 19, 2026 deadline has passed and FTC enforcement is active; platforms without compliant removal mechanisms face civil penalties of up to $53,088 per violation.
- **Review terms of service and age-gating mechanisms** for AI image-generation features to ensure minors cannot access NCII-capable functionality; the KIDS Act's SAFE Bots and SCREEN Act provisions are likely to impose additional requirements when enacted.
- **Monitor the KIDS Act's progress** through the House floor and Senate; the June 22, 2026 committee deal accelerates the timeline but Senate floor dynamics and the AI preemption rider remain unresolved variables.
- **Assess state NCII law exposure** across all jurisdictions where services are offered — 48 states now have NCII/sexually explicit deepfake laws with varying criminal and civil provisions; platforms should map compliance obligations against each state's specific requirements.
- **Preserve litigation hold** for any records related to AI image-generation features, safety filter testing, and user reports of NCII, given the active class-action landscape against xAI and likely copycat litigation.
