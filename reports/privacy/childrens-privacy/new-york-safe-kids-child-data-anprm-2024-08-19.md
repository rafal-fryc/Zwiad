# New York Attorney General Opens Public Consultation on Child Online Safety Laws

**Jurisdiction:** New York | **Category:** Privacy | **Date:** August 19, 2024

## Executive Summary [HIGH confidence]

On August 1, 2024, the Office of the New York State Attorney General (OAG) released two Advanced Notices of Proposed Rulemaking (ANPRMs) to solicit public input as it develops implementing regulations for the [Stop Addictive Feeds Exploitation (SAFE) for Kids Act](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/bills/2023/S7694/amendment/A) (S7694-A) and the [New York Child Data Protection Act](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/bills/2023/S7695/amendment/A) (S7695-A). Both statutes were signed by Governor Kathy Hochul on June 20, 2024, and delegate broad rulemaking authority to the OAG to specify commercially reasonable age-verification methods, verifiable parental consent mechanisms, and language access requirements before regulated obligations become enforceable. The comment period closed September 30, 2024. The rulemaking process proceeded in two stages: an NPRM for the SAFE for Kids Act was released in September 2025, and the Child Data Protection Act took effect June 20, 2025 — with the OAG issuing implementation guidance ahead of that date. Operators of online services with significant New York user bases should treat this rulemaking as an ongoing compliance-preparation obligation.

## Background [HIGH confidence]

### Legislative Context

New York's two child-safety statutes — the SAFE for Kids Act and the Child Data Protection Act — are part of a national wave of state-level children's digital privacy and online-safety laws. By mid-2024, states including Maryland (Age-Appropriate Design Code, signed May 2024), Virginia (VCDPA minors amendments, SB 361), Utah (social media youth protections), Arkansas, and others had enacted or substantially updated children's online safety legislation.

New York's laws are more prescriptive and broader in some respects than their federal baseline. At the federal level, the Children's Online Privacy Protection Act (COPPA), administered by the FTC, applies only to children under 13 and only to operators with actual knowledge of a child user. New York's Child Data Protection Act extends comparable protections to all users under 18, and the SAFE for Kids Act imposes affirmative restrictions on algorithmic content delivery to the same age group.

The New York Assembly passed both bills on June 7, 2024. Governor Hochul signed them on June 20, 2024, the same day, in a joint ceremony with Attorney General Letitia James, who will be the sole enforcement authority under both statutes. Per [official statements from the Governor's office](https://www.governor.ny.gov/news/governor-hochul-joins-attorney-general-james-and-bill-sponsors-sign-nation-leading-legislation), the laws were billed as "nation-leading legislation" on children's online safety.

### Prior Rulemaking Activity

The OAG's ANPRMs were issued approximately six weeks after the bills became law. Rather than immediately promulgating binding regulations, the OAG sought industry and public comment through the less formal ANPRM mechanism, signaling its intent to develop technically grounded rules sensitive to implementation feasibility. The September 30, 2024, comment deadline generated submissions from stakeholders including the [Future of Privacy Forum](https://fpf.org/blog/fpf-submits-comments-to-inform-new-york-childrens-privacy-rulemaking-processes/) and privacy advocacy organizations. Following the ANPRM comment period, the OAG issued a formal Notice of Proposed Rulemaking (NPRM) for the SAFE for Kids Act in [September 2025](https://ag.ny.gov/press-release/2025/attorney-general-james-releases-proposed-rules-safe-kids-act-restrict-addictive), with a public comment period closing December 1, 2025.

## Detailed Analysis [HIGH confidence]

### The SAFE for Kids Act (S7694-A)

The [Stop Addictive Feeds Exploitation (SAFE) for Kids Act](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/bills/2023/S7694/amendment/A) (Senate Bill 7694-A) prohibits "covered operators" of social media platforms from providing an "addictive feed" to a user the operator knows — or reasonably should know — is a minor (under 18), unless the operator has:

1. Used commercially reasonable and technically feasible methods to determine the user is not a minor; or
2. Obtained verifiable parental consent.

**"Addictive feed" definition:** An addictive feed is defined broadly as an online service, or portion thereof, where multiple pieces of media generated or shared by users are recommended, selected, or prioritized for display to a user based (in whole or in part) on information associated with the user or the user's device. This captures standard algorithmic content recommendation systems used by social media platforms.

**Nighttime notification restriction:** Without verifiable parental consent, covered operators may not send nighttime push notifications regarding an addictive feed to minor users between 12:00 a.m. and 6:00 a.m. Eastern Time.

**Scope limitation:** The Act covers conduct that takes place "wholly or partly in New York" and applies to platforms where users spend a significant proportion of their time (the 2025 NPRM proposed a 20% threshold) on addictive feeds. It does not apply when a user accesses the platform from a physical location outside the state.

**Non-degradation requirement:** Operators may not withhold, degrade, lower the quality of, or increase the price of any product or service to a user solely because the operator is not permitted to provide an addictive feed to that user.

**Rulemaking authority:** The OAG has authority to identify which age-verification methods qualify as "commercially reasonable and technically feasible," and to promulgate standards for verifiable parental consent. The SAFE for Kids Act does not itself take effect until 180 days after the OAG finalizes regulations — meaning the statute's enforcement depends on the completion of the rulemaking process. As noted by [Hunton Andrews Kurth](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-information-security-law/new-york-attorney-general-opens-public-consultation-on-child-online-safety-laws), the ANPRMs were designed precisely to inform these critical regulatory choices.

**ANPRM focus areas for SAFE for Kids Act:**

- Methods to determine whether a user is a minor ("commercially reasonable and technically feasible")
- Methods to obtain verifiable parental consent
- Language access requirements for required notices and consent flows

Comments were directed to: protectnykidsonline@ag.ny.gov by September 30, 2024.

### The New York Child Data Protection Act (S7695-A)

The [New York Child Data Protection Act (NYCDPA)](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/bills/2023/S7695/amendment/A) (Senate Bill 7695-A) amends New York General Business Law by adding Article 39-FF. Unlike the SAFE for Kids Act, the NYCDPA is not conditioned on finalization of OAG regulations — it took effect on June 20, 2025, one year after signing.

**Operator definition:** "Operator" means any person who operates or provides a website, online service, online application, mobile application, or connected device, and who, alone or jointly with others, controls the purposes and means of processing personal data.

**Core prohibition:** Operators may not collect, use, share, sell, or otherwise process the personal data of a user the operator knows or reasonably should know is under 18, except:

- For users **under 13**: processing must comply with the federal COPPA standard (i.e., verifiable parental consent or specific COPPA exceptions apply).
- For users **ages 13-17**: processing is prohibited unless it is (a) strictly necessary for the purpose of the website or service, or (b) the user provides informed consent.

**Third-party disclosure restriction:** Operators may not disclose the personal data of a minor to a third party unless a binding written agreement ensures the third party will not process the data for any purpose other than strictly necessary to fulfill the agreement.

**Data deletion obligation:** Once an operator knows or reasonably should know a user is a minor, it must delete that user's personal data within 14 days, unless processing is otherwise permitted under the NYCDPA or COPPA.

**Enforcement:** Enforcement authority rests exclusively with the OAG, which may seek injunctions, damages, and civil penalties of up to $5,000 per violation. No private right of action exists under the statute.

**ANPRM focus areas for Child Data Protection Act:**

- Methods to determine whether a user is a minor
- Methods to obtain verifiable parental consent (for under-13 users)
- Language access requirements

Comments were directed to: childdataprotection@ag.ny.gov by September 30, 2024.

### The OAG's Three-Area Rulemaking Framework

Across both ANPRMs, the OAG sought comment on three overlapping regulatory areas:

1. **Age determination:** What methods constitute "commercially reasonable and technically feasible" age verification? The OAG's September 2025 NPRM for the SAFE for Kids Act proposed standards for secure, privacy-protective age assurance — reflecting feedback from the ANPRM process that age verification technologies must be privacy-minimizing and must not create new data risks. Any information collected for age-determination purposes must be deleted or de-identified immediately after use.

2. **Verifiable parental consent:** What mechanisms qualify? The OAG's proposed rules (as later described in the 2025 NPRM) contemplate a minor-first consent flow: a platform must first obtain the minor's approval to request parental consent, and only then seek verifiable consent from the parent. This reflects a design principle of minors' agency within the consent process.

3. **Language access:** The OAG sought input on what language access requirements should apply to required disclosures and consent mechanisms, recognizing New York's significant non-English-speaking population.

### Subsequent Regulatory Developments

The OAG's August 2024 ANPRMs were the first step in a multi-stage rulemaking. As of the date of this report:

- The formal NPRM for the SAFE for Kids Act was published in [September 2025](https://ag.ny.gov/press-release/2025/attorney-general-james-releases-proposed-rules-safe-kids-act-restrict-addictive), with the public comment period closing December 1, 2025.
- The Child Data Protection Act took effect on **June 20, 2025**. The OAG issued [implementation guidance](https://ag.ny.gov/child-data-protection-act-guidance) in advance of that date, and stated it would exercise enforcement discretion while accounting for operators' good-faith compliance efforts until final rules are promulgated.
- The SAFE for Kids Act's 180-day effective period means enforcement of the addictive-feed restrictions will commence 180 days after the OAG finalizes regulations — a date that had not yet arrived as of the publication of this report.

## Impact Assessment [MEDIUM confidence]

### Affected Entities

The two statutes have substantially different scope:

**SAFE for Kids Act** applies to "covered operators" — social media platforms whose services include an addictive feed as a significant component. The 2025 NPRM proposed that platforms qualify when users spend at least 20% of their time on addictive feeds. This would cover major social media platforms including Instagram, TikTok, YouTube, Snapchat, and similar services with algorithm-driven feeds, but likely not general-purpose websites or platforms without personalized content recommendation. The law is geographic: it applies only to New York users.

**Child Data Protection Act** has broader operator coverage — any website, online service, mobile application, or connected device that processes personal data of New York users under 18 falls within scope, unless strictly necessary processing exemptions apply. This includes e-commerce platforms, gaming services, educational technology, health apps, and any other online service with minor users in New York.

### Compliance Requirements and Timelines

**NYCDPA (effective June 20, 2025):**
- Operators must implement policies to identify and refrain from processing minor users' data except as permitted.
- On discovering a user is a minor, data must be deleted within 14 days (absent a NYCDPA exception).
- Third-party data sharing requires binding contractual restrictions.
- No private right of action; AG enforcement only.
- The OAG has stated it will exercise discretion in enforcement while final rules are pending, taking good-faith compliance into account.

**SAFE for Kids Act:**
- The statute's substantive obligations (restricting addictive feeds and nighttime notifications) take effect 180 days after the OAG finalizes implementing regulations.
- Operators should monitor the final rule publication timeline; the NPRM comment period closed December 2025.
- Pre-compliance preparation should include: age-verification technology assessment, parental consent mechanism design, and feed-restriction implementation planning.

### Enforcement Outlook

Both statutes are enforced exclusively by the OAG under AG Letitia James, who has been active in children's privacy enforcement. The $5,000-per-violation civil penalty structure for the NYCDPA could generate significant aggregate exposure for large platforms with millions of minor users. The absence of a private right of action limits litigation risk from individuals but concentrates enforcement in the OAG, which has demonstrated willingness to bring large-scale enforcement actions against tech companies on consumer protection grounds.

The OAG's decision to release ANPRMs before promulgating binding rules signals a pragmatic approach that seeks technically grounded, workable standards — but it also delays the timeline for final rulemaking. Operators should not interpret the ANPRM process or the OAG's enforcement discretion statement as suggesting these laws will not be enforced.

## Action Items

- **Monitor SAFE for Kids Act rulemaking:** The NPRM comment period closed December 1, 2025. Track OAG announcements for publication of final rules; the SAFE for Kids Act's obligations take effect 180 days after finalization.
- **Assess NYCDPA compliance status immediately:** The Child Data Protection Act has been in effect since June 20, 2025. Operators with New York minor users should evaluate whether their data processing practices satisfy the "strictly necessary" exception or require obtaining informed consent.
- **Implement data deletion workflows:** Develop processes to identify known-minor users and delete their personal data within the 14-day window required by the NYCDPA, unless a processing exception applies.
- **Review third-party data agreements:** Audit agreements with third-party vendors to ensure binding contractual restrictions on processing minor users' data exist where required.
- **Evaluate age-verification capabilities:** Review existing age-determination and parental-consent mechanisms against the standards proposed in the OAG's September 2025 NPRM, particularly the privacy-protective deletion requirement for data used solely for age verification.
- **Watch for language access requirements:** Both laws may include language access components in final rules; operators serving non-English-speaking populations in New York should anticipate potential localization requirements for consent and disclosure interfaces.
- **Consult OAG implementation guidance:** Review the OAG's [Child Data Protection Act implementation guidance](https://ag.ny.gov/child-data-protection-act-guidance) published in May 2025, and the [Protecting Children Online resource page](https://ag.ny.gov/resources/individuals/consumer-issues/technology/protecting-children-online) for current OAG positions.
