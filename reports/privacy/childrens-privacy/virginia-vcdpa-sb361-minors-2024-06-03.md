# Virginia Amends VCDPA to Add Heightened Privacy Protections for Children (SB 361 / HB 707)

**Jurisdiction:** Virginia | **Category:** Privacy | **Date:** 2024-06-03

## Executive Summary [HIGH confidence]

On May 17, 2024, Virginia Governor Glenn Youngkin signed [SB 361 / HB 707](https://legacylis.virginia.gov/cgi-bin/legp604.exe?241+sum+SB361=) into law as Acts of Assembly Chapter 844, amending the Virginia Consumer Data Protection Act (VCDPA) to impose heightened protections for the personal data of minors. The legislation — effective January 1, 2025 — applies to controllers that process the personal data of a "known child," defined as a consumer under 13 years of age. Without first obtaining verifiable parental consent, controllers are now prohibited from processing a known child's personal data for targeted advertising, data sales, or profiling for decisions with legal or similarly significant effects. The law also imposes default restrictions on collecting a known child's precise geolocation and expands the VCDPA's data protection assessment (DPA) requirements to cover services directed to children. While Governor Youngkin expressed dissatisfaction that the bill did not extend equivalent protections to minors aged 13–17, the legislation represents a meaningful expansion of Virginia's existing privacy framework and aligns it more closely with the federal Children's Online Privacy Protection Act (COPPA). A follow-on bill, [SB 854 (2025)](https://lis.virginia.gov/bill-details/20251/SB854), extended social media time-limit restrictions to users under 16 and became the subject of First Amendment litigation in 2026.

## Background [HIGH confidence]

### The Virginia Consumer Data Protection Act

Virginia enacted the [Consumer Data Protection Act (VCDPA)](https://law.lis.virginia.gov/vacode/title59.1/chapter53/) in 2021, effective January 1, 2023, making it the second state after California to pass a comprehensive consumer privacy statute. The VCDPA establishes rights for Virginia consumers — access, correction, deletion, portability, and opt-out — and imposes obligations on controllers that process personal data. The statute is codified at Title 59.1, Chapter 53 of the Code of Virginia (§§ 59.1-571 through 59.1-585).

Under the original VCDPA, "sensitive data" included personal data of a "known child," defined at § 59.1-575 as a consumer who is under 13 years of age — mirroring COPPA's threshold. Processing sensitive data required an opt-in consent, but the statute did not separately enumerate child-specific prohibitions beyond the sensitive-data consent requirement and the existing DPIA mandate for sensitive data processing.

### Legislative History of SB 361 / HB 707

Virginia's 2024 General Assembly session saw parallel Senate and House bills — [SB 361](https://legacylis.virginia.gov/cgi-bin/legp604.exe?241+sum+SB361=) (patron: Sen. Schuyler T. VanValkenburg (D-16)) and HB 707 — aimed at strengthening child data protections within the VCDPA framework. Governor Youngkin had initially proposed expanding protections to all minors under 18, but the General Assembly declined to go that far, limiting heightened protections to the under-13 cohort consistent with COPPA's long-standing threshold. The conference substitute was chaptered as [Acts of Assembly Chapter 844](https://legacylis.virginia.gov/cgi-bin/legp604.exe?241+ful+SB361+hil=).

When signing the bill, [Governor Youngkin stated](https://www.dwt.com/blogs/privacy--security-law-blog/2024/05/virginia-data-privacy-law-amended-for-under-18) that "the bill does not go as far as it should, particularly by excluding minors over 13 from protection," and committed to working with the legislature and stakeholders to further strengthen these protections in future sessions. That commitment bore fruit in 2025 when SB 854 expanded social media restrictions to users under 16.

## Detailed Analysis [HIGH confidence]

### Scope and Applicability

SB 361 amends three sections of the Code of Virginia: § 59.1-575 (definitions), § 59.1-578 (controller obligations), and § 59.1-580 (data protection assessments). The child-specific provisions apply to any "controller" — an entity that determines the purpose and means of processing personal data — that processes the personal data of a "known child," meaning a consumer the controller has actual knowledge is under 13.

Controllers providing "online services, products, or features" that are directed to children also fall within scope of the expanded DPA requirements, which apply based on the nature of the offering rather than solely on actual knowledge of a specific user's age.

### Key Provisions

**1. Processing Prohibitions (§ 59.1-578)**

Without first obtaining verifiable parental consent, a controller may not process a known child's personal data for:

- **Targeted advertising** — behavioral advertising based on the child's profile or activity;
- **Sale of personal data** — transferring the child's data to third parties for monetary or other valuable consideration; or
- **Profiling for significant decisions** — profiling used to make decisions that produce legal or similarly significant effects concerning the consumer.

These prohibitions are absolute absent parental consent. The [Future of Privacy Forum](https://fpf.org/blog/little-users-big-protections-colorado-and-virginia-pass-laws-focused-on-kids-privacy/) notes that they mirror, at the state level, the spirit of COPPA's prohibition on conditioning children's participation in activities on disclosure of more information than reasonably necessary.

**2. Data Minimization (§ 59.1-578)**

Controllers are prohibited from processing a known child's personal data that is:

- Not reasonably necessary to provide the online service, product, or feature; or
- For purposes other than those disclosed at the time of collection, or not reasonably necessary for and compatible with those disclosed purposes.

This data minimization obligation tracks the VCDPA's general purpose-limitation principle but applies it with heightened rigor to children, regardless of whether parental consent was obtained.

**3. Precise Geolocation Restrictions (§ 59.1-578)**

Controllers may not collect a known child's precise geolocation data unless:

- The geolocation collection is reasonably necessary to provide the requested online service, product, or feature, and only for the time necessary to do so; **and**
- The controller provides the known child with a real-time signal — visible for the duration of collection — indicating that precise geolocation is being collected.

The [Davis Wright Tremaine analysis](https://www.dwt.com/blogs/privacy--security-law-blog/2024/05/virginia-data-privacy-law-amended-for-under-18) notes this signal requirement parallels similar provisions in Connecticut and Colorado children's privacy legislation.

**4. Parental Consent Standards and COPPA Safe Harbor (§ 59.1-578)**

For children under 13, controllers and processors that comply with COPPA's verifiable parental consent (VPC) requirements — 15 U.S.C. § 6501 et seq. — are deemed compliant with the VCDPA's parental consent obligation. For children ages 13 and older who nonetheless require parental consent under the controller's practices, compliance is achieved by adhering to FTC-promulgated methods for obtaining parental consent under COPPA.

This safe harbor is practically significant: entities already operating COPPA-compliant programs for under-13 users need not build a separate consent mechanism for Virginia.

**5. Expanded Data Protection Assessments (§ 59.1-580)**

The amendment expands the VCDPA's DPA requirement to cover any controller that provides online services, products, or features "directed to consumers whom such controller has actual knowledge are children." Required DPA contents include: the purpose of the online offering, the types of children's personal data being processed, and the reasons for that processing.

This is additive to the existing VCDPA requirement for DPAs before processing sensitive data (which already included data of known children under 13) and before processing for targeted advertising, sale, or certain profiling activities.

### Enforcement

The [VCDPA's enforcement mechanism](https://law.lis.virginia.gov/vacode/title59.1/chapter53/) vests exclusive enforcement authority in the Virginia Attorney General, with a 30-day cure period after notice of non-compliance, prior to imposition of civil penalties of up to $7,500 per violation. There is no private right of action. As of the effective date, January 1, 2025, businesses processing known children's data under the VCDPA are subject to AG enforcement of the new provisions.

The [Hunton Andrews Kurth analysis](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-information-security-law/virginias-new-protections-for-children-go-into-effect) confirmed the January 1, 2025 effective date and noted that Virginia's AG office had signaled active enforcement interest in minors' data protections, consistent with the subsequent February 2026 enforcement announcement targeting the social media time-limit provisions under SB 854.

## Impact Assessment [MEDIUM confidence]

### Affected Entities

SB 361 / HB 707 applies to any business subject to the VCDPA — broadly, those that (a) conduct business in Virginia or produce products or services targeted to Virginia residents, (b) process personal data of at least 100,000 Virginia consumers per year, or (c) derive 50% or more of gross revenue from the sale of personal data while processing data of at least 25,000 Virginia consumers. Within that universe, the child-specific provisions apply to any controller processing data of known children under 13.

Industries most directly affected include:

- **Social media platforms and consumer apps** with audiences that include minors;
- **Educational technology (EdTech)** operators offering tools to K–12 students;
- **Gaming platforms** with under-13 user bases;
- **Advertising technology companies** whose customers target audiences that may include children; and
- **Data brokers** that compile and sell profiles including children's data.

### Compliance Requirements and Timelines

Controllers had approximately seven months from signing (May 17, 2024) to prepare for the January 1, 2025 effective date. Compliance obligations include:

1. **Age detection / actual knowledge standard**: Implement processes to identify when a user is a known child under 13. COPPA's "actual knowledge" standard guides this inquiry; controllers do not bear an absolute duty to verify age but must respond once they gain actual knowledge.
2. **Parental consent mechanisms**: For operations requiring parental consent, ensure COPPA VPC procedures are in place or adopt FTC-approved consent methods for the under-13 cohort.
3. **Processing restriction controls**: Technical and operational controls to block targeted advertising, data sales, and profiling for significant decisions absent parental consent for known children.
4. **Data minimization review**: Audit data collection and processing pipelines to ensure children's data is not collected or used beyond what is reasonably necessary and disclosed.
5. **Geolocation signal implementation**: For products that collect children's precise geolocation, implement UI signals indicating active collection.
6. **Updated DPAs**: Conduct new or updated data protection assessments for any online services directed at children.

According to [TrueVault's compliance guide](https://www.truevault.com/learn/virginia-passes-new-privacy-protections-for-minors), the most operationally demanding element for many platforms is implementing robust age verification and parental consent mechanisms, which require significant engineering investment.

## Comparison with Other Children's Privacy Laws

### COPPA (Federal, 1998 / Updated 2025) [HIGH confidence]

The Virginia amendments were consciously designed to complement rather than conflict with COPPA. The COPPA safe harbor means that COPPA-compliant entities are largely deemed compliant with Virginia's consent requirements. However, Virginia goes beyond COPPA in key respects:

- **Broader scope of prohibited activities**: COPPA prohibits collection without consent; Virginia additionally prohibits targeted advertising, sale, and profiling for significant decisions absent consent — activities COPPA does not address directly.
- **Data minimization**: The VCDPA's child-specific minimization rules extend beyond COPPA's collection-limitation framework to reach ongoing processing.
- **DPA requirements**: Virginia mandates documented assessments; COPPA has no equivalent requirement.

The [FTC's 2025 COPPA Rule amendments](https://www.ftc.gov/legal-library/browse/rules/childrens-online-privacy-protection-rule-coppa) (published April 22, 2025, with most provisions requiring compliance by April 22, 2026) strengthened COPPA in ways that now partially close these gaps at the federal level.

### California Age-Appropriate Design Code (CA AADC) [MEDIUM confidence]

California's AB 2273 (2022), the Age-Appropriate Design Code Act, took a materially different approach — requiring privacy-by-design features, default high-privacy settings, Data Protection Impact Assessments, and a ban on "dark patterns" for any service "likely to be accessed" by minors under 18. A federal district court [enjoined enforcement](https://fpf.org/blog/little-users-big-protections-colorado-and-virginia-pass-laws-focused-on-kids-privacy/) on First Amendment grounds; subsequent legislative revisions were underway as of this writing. Virginia's narrower approach — focusing on consent and processing restrictions for known under-13 users — is less constitutionally vulnerable but also less comprehensive than the AADC's design-mandate model.

### Colorado SB 41 (2024) and Connecticut SB 3 (2023) [MEDIUM confidence]

The [Future of Privacy Forum's comparative analysis](https://fpf.org/blog/little-users-big-protections-colorado-and-virginia-pass-laws-focused-on-kids-privacy/) highlights that unlike Colorado SB 41 and Connecticut SB 3, Virginia HB 707 does not create a standalone "duty of reasonable care" owed to child users. Colorado and Connecticut imposed affirmative design-and-development obligations; Virginia's approach is prohibition-based, limiting processing rather than mandating design standards. Virginia also lacks Colorado's and Connecticut's expansions to cover users under 18 rather than only under 13.

### Maryland Age Appropriate Design Code / MODPA (2024) [MEDIUM confidence]

Maryland enacted both the Maryland Age Appropriate Design Code (HB 603) and the Maryland Online Data Privacy Act (MODPA) in 2024, which together impose duty-of-care and data minimization requirements reaching all minors under 18. Virginia's SB 361 is comparatively narrower in age scope (under 13) but aligned on the data minimization and consent principles.

## Action Items

- **Audit age-detection processes**: Verify that your platform's systems accurately capture "actual knowledge" of users under 13 and trigger child-data handling protocols upon detection.
- **Review COPPA VPC compliance**: Confirm that existing COPPA-compliant parental consent mechanisms meet the VCDPA safe harbor standard; document this alignment.
- **Block prohibited processing for known children**: Ensure that known children under 13 are excluded from targeted advertising, data sale pipelines, and profiling workflows unless explicit parental consent is documented.
- **Implement geolocation UI signals**: For any products collecting children's precise geolocation, add a visible, real-time signal during collection. Limit duration of collection to what is operationally necessary.
- **Update DPAs**: Prepare or update data protection assessments for any online service directed to children, documenting processing purposes and necessity.
- **Data minimization review**: Map data flows for known children and eliminate collection or processing not reasonably necessary for the disclosed purpose.
- **Monitor SB 854 / social media age-restriction litigation**: The follow-on SB 854 (effective January 1, 2026) imposes one-hour-per-day limits and age verification for social media users under 16. Enforcement has been challenged on First Amendment grounds; monitor the Virginia AG's appeal of the preliminary injunction.
- **Watch for under-18 expansions**: Governor Youngkin signaled intent to expand protections to 13–17 year olds; the 2025 and 2026 legislative sessions are likely vehicles for such expansion.
