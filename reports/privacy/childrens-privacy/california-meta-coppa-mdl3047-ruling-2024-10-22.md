# Federal Court Denies Meta's Motion to Dismiss COPPA and State Consumer Protection Claims in MDL 3047

**Jurisdiction:** Federal / California (N.D. Cal.) / Multi-state | **Category:** Privacy | **Date:** October 2024

## Executive Summary [HIGH confidence]

On October 15, 2024, U.S. District Judge Yvonne Gonzalez Rogers of the Northern District of California issued a [102-page order](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/us-dis-crt-n-d-cal/116637171.html) largely denying Meta's motion to dismiss in *In re Social Media Adolescent Addiction/Personal Injury Products Liability Litigation*, MDL No. 3047, Case No. 4:22-md-03047-YGR. The ruling allows a [bipartisan coalition of 33 state attorneys general](https://oag.ca.gov/news/press-releases/attorney-general-bonta-secures-court-decision-largely-denying-meta%E2%80%99s-attempt) — led by California AG Rob Bonta — to proceed with claims that Meta designed Facebook and Instagram to be addictive to minors. Key holdings include: (1) Meta's COPPA violation claims survive because Meta conceded actual knowledge of under-13 users; (2) Meta's Section 230 immunity defense largely fails as to claims grounded in the company's own deceptive conduct; and (3) specific design features — such as appearance-altering filters and time-restriction tools — are not shielded by Section 230 because they are Meta's own product design rather than alterations of third-party content. The ruling is a significant procedural milestone for children's online privacy enforcement and signals that platforms may not use Section 230 to categorically foreclose state consumer-protection litigation over product design choices.

## Background [HIGH confidence]

### COPPA Statutory Framework

The [Children's Online Privacy Protection Act of 1998](https://www.ftc.gov/legal-library/browse/statutes/childrens-online-privacy-protection-act) (COPPA), codified at [15 U.S.C. §§ 6501–6506](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?path=/prelim@title15/chapter91&edition=prelim), prohibits operators of websites and online services that are "directed to children" — or that have "actual knowledge" they are collecting personal information from children under 13 — from doing so without first providing notice to parents and obtaining verifiable parental consent. The FTC implements COPPA through [16 C.F.R. Part 312](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-C/part-312). Whether a service is "directed to children" has long been a contested question; services that target a general audience but attract significant child users have historically argued COPPA does not apply to them absent direct evidence of knowing collection.

### The States' Lawsuit

In October 2023, a bipartisan coalition of 42 state attorneys general filed coordinated lawsuits against Meta alleging that Facebook and Instagram were designed to addict children and teenagers while Meta publicly concealed the harms it knew its platforms were causing. Thirty-three AGs filed jointly in the Northern District of California, where the case was consolidated into MDL 3047 before Judge Gonzalez Rogers. The remaining states, including Massachusetts, filed parallel suits in their own state courts. The joint federal complaint alleged: (1) Meta violated COPPA by collecting data from users it knew or should have known were under 13 without parental consent; (2) Meta engaged in unfair and deceptive acts and practices under state consumer-protection statutes by publicly downplaying addiction and mental-health risks while internally documenting them; and (3) certain Instagram and Facebook features were themselves defective products. According to the [states' complaint](https://epic.org/documents/california-et-al-v-meta-inc-et-al/), internal Meta research from 2015 estimated that four million Instagram users were under 13, representing roughly 30 percent of all U.S. children aged 10 to 12 — yet Meta continued to deny knowledge of underage users.

### MDL 3047 and Prior Proceedings

MDL 3047 is one of the largest multi-district litigations in U.S. history concerning platform harm, consolidating thousands of individual personal-injury claims alongside the state AG actions. The [case docket](https://cand.uscourts.gov/cases-e-filing/cases/422-md-03047-ygr/re-social-media-adolescent-addictionpersonal-injury-products) is maintained by the Northern District of California. Prior orders had addressed bellwether discovery and case management; the October 2024 order on the states' motion to dismiss was the first major ruling on the substantive legal theories.

## Detailed Analysis [HIGH confidence]

### COPPA: "Directed to Children" and "Actual Knowledge"

Judge Gonzalez Rogers [denied Meta's motion to dismiss the COPPA claims](https://www.courthousenews.com/meta-fails-to-knock-out-states-claims-that-facebook-instagram-are-addictive-for-young-users/) on two independent grounds.

First, she rejected Meta's argument that Facebook and Instagram are not "directed to children" under COPPA, and therefore cannot be subject to the statute's data-collection restrictions. The judge held that third-party content hosted on a platform — including child-oriented posts, videos, and communities — can be considered when determining whether the platform or a portion of it is "directed to children" under the statutory test. Meta argued that such reliance on third-party content was inconsistent with the spirit of Section 230 (though not technically prohibited by it). The court rejected this argument and held that the COPPA "directed to children" inquiry is not constrained by Section 230's framework.

Second, and independently, the court held that Meta conceded it has actual knowledge that users under 13 are on its platforms. Under [15 U.S.C. § 6502(a)(1)](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?path=/prelim@title15/chapter91&edition=prelim), an operator with "actual knowledge" that it is collecting information from a child under 13 is covered by COPPA regardless of whether the service is directed at children. Meta's own internal documents, as pleaded in the complaint, showed millions of underage users were known to the company. The court found this allegation sufficient at the pleading stage to sustain the COPPA claims.

### Section 230 and Deceptive Practices Claims

The ruling provides a detailed analysis of the interplay between Section 230 of the Communications Decency Act and state consumer-protection claims. Judge Gonzalez Rogers acknowledged that [Section 230](https://www.courthousenews.com/meta-fails-to-knock-out-states-claims-that-facebook-instagram-are-addictive-for-young-users/) provides "a fairly significant limitation" on consumer-protection claims but held it does not bar claims rooted in the platform's own conduct or representations.

The court permitted the states' deceptive-acts-and-practices claims to proceed, finding that Meta's "alleged yearslong public campaign of deception as to the risks of addiction and mental harms to minors from platform use fits readily within these states' deceptive acts and practices framework." Critically, the court characterized this deception as Meta's own speech and conduct — not liability for hosting third-party content — and therefore outside Section 230's immunity.

The states' failure-to-warn theory also survived. The court held this theory involves a "constellation of acts which, viewed both individually and holistically, constitute an unfair trade act or practice" — Meta's own design, deployment, and marketing decisions — not conduct immunized by Section 230.

### Product Design Features and Section 230 Limits

The court drew a significant distinction between platform features that facilitate third-party content and features that represent Meta's own product design. Features such as [appearance-altering filters, time-restriction tools, and Instagram's multiple-account function](https://www.courthousenews.com/meta-fails-to-knock-out-states-claims-that-facebook-instagram-are-addictive-for-young-users/) are not shielded by Section 230 because they do not involve the publication or alteration of third-party content. Meta created these features; Meta can be held liable for their alleged harmful effects on minors.

This reasoning tracks a growing body of case law distinguishing between liability for third-party content (immunized by Section 230) and liability for the platform's own design choices (not immunized). The ruling aligns with similar conclusions reached in [parallel state-court proceedings](https://www.techpolicy.press/social-media-lawsuits-by-state-attorneys-general-surmount-section-230-other-challenges/).

### What Was Dismissed

Not all claims survived. The court acknowledged that certain claims implicating Meta's curation and recommendation of third-party content remain subject to Section 230 constraints. Where the states' theories depended on holding Meta liable for failing to moderate or remove third-party content — as opposed to Meta's own product design or deceptive statements — Section 230 provided a defense. The court's order thus represents a partial rather than total denial of Meta's motion.

## Impact Assessment [MEDIUM confidence]

### Implications for Platform Liability

The ruling is one of the most consequential child-privacy decisions in years. It establishes, at least at the pleading stage, that:

- Platforms with documented "actual knowledge" of underage users cannot rely on age-gate policies alone to avoid COPPA exposure. Internal metrics, engineering reports, and demographic analyses that show underage users can be used to establish actual knowledge.
- Section 230 does not provide categorical immunity for state consumer-protection claims grounded in a platform's own product design or public misrepresentations. Platforms must defend the substantive merits of such claims.
- Third-party content on a platform can be relevant to whether the platform is "directed to children" for COPPA purposes — a significant expansion of how the test has traditionally been applied to general-audience platforms.

### Industry Compliance Implications

For social media operators and other consumer-facing platforms, the ruling creates several compliance pressure points:

1. **Age verification and data hygiene**: Platforms that have internally documented knowledge of underage users but maintained permissive onboarding practices face substantial COPPA exposure. The ruling signals that internal research and engineering documents will be discoverable and used to establish actual knowledge.
2. **Design-feature review**: Product features that are not inherently content-hosting functions — algorithmic feeds, engagement nudges, time-on-platform tools, filters — should be reviewed for COPPA and consumer-protection compliance. These features sit outside Section 230's protection.
3. **Public-facing disclosures**: The court's deception holding means that public statements minimizing addiction or mental-health risks — while internal research reflects awareness of harms — carry significant litigation risk under state UDAP statutes.

### Relationship to 2025 COPPA Rule Amendments

The FTC's [January 2025 amendments to the COPPA Rule](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/04/22/2025-05904/childrens-online-privacy-protection-rule) (16 C.F.R. Part 312), with a compliance deadline of April 22, 2026, expand parental consent requirements, impose new data-minimization obligations, and restrict targeted advertising to minors. The MDL 3047 ruling's "actual knowledge" analysis will remain highly relevant under the amended rule, which retains the actual-knowledge trigger. Platforms preparing for COPPA rule compliance should treat the MDL 3047 findings as a preview of enforcement scrutiny.

## Action Items

- **Audit internal research and reporting**: Identify any internal reports, analytics, or communications that estimate or document underage user populations. These documents are likely discoverable in COPPA enforcement proceedings and litigation; privilege and litigation-hold protocols should be reviewed.
- **Review age-gate and parental-consent workflows**: Evaluate whether current age-verification mechanisms meet the FTC's COPPA Rule requirements as amended in 2025. Passive age-gates (e.g., self-reported birthdate with no verification) are unlikely to be sufficient.
- **Map product features to Section 230 exposure**: Conduct a legal review of platform features to distinguish content-hosting functions (potentially Section 230-protected) from product design choices (not protected). Document the distinction with engineering input.
- **Monitor MDL 3047 proceedings**: The case proceeds to discovery and eventually to trial on surviving claims. Bellwether trial scheduling and summary judgment motions will generate additional precedent. Track the docket via the [N.D. Cal. case page](https://cand.uscourts.gov/cases-e-filing/cases/422-md-03047-ygr/re-social-media-adolescent-addictionpersonal-injury-products).
- **Track parallel state-court proceedings**: Massachusetts, New Mexico, and other states filed separate suits not consolidated in MDL 3047. The Massachusetts SJC ruling in April 2026 (see Related Reports) confirms that state courts are reaching similar conclusions on Section 230 immunity.
- **Assess COPPA rule compliance deadline**: The FTC's 2025 COPPA Rule amendments take effect April 22, 2026. Align internal compliance programs with both the rule's updated requirements and the evidentiary standards the MDL 3047 ruling signals will be applied in enforcement.
