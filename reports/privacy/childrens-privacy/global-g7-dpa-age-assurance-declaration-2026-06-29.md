# G7 Data Protection Authorities Adopt Joint Declaration on Privacy-Preserving Age Assurance

**Jurisdiction:** Global (France, United States, United Kingdom, Canada, Germany, Italy, Japan, European Union) | **Category:** Privacy | **Date:** June 29, 2026

## Executive Summary [HIGH confidence] [VERIFIED]

On June 25–26, 2026, the data protection and privacy authorities of the G7 nations convened in Paris for their sixth annual roundtable, hosted by France's [Commission Nationale de l'Informatique et des Libertés (CNIL)](https://www.cnil.fr/en/g7-2026) as part of France's G7 presidency. <!-- verified: independent search confirms 2026 was the 6th roundtable (2023 Tokyo=3rd, 2024 Rome=4th, 2025 Canada=5th, 2026 Paris=6th) and dates June 25-26 --> The delegations adopted three documents: a general joint communiqué, a [statement on privacy-preserving age assurance](https://www.cnil.fr/sites/default/files/2026-06/g7_dpa_statement_on_age_assurance.pdf), and a [joint paper on children's privacy in connected home devices](https://www.cnil.fr/sites/default/files/2026-06/g7_children_s_privacy_in_connected_home_devices_paper.pdf). <!-- verified --> The age-assurance statement establishes data protection principles — including data minimization, purpose limitation, and privacy-by-design — that should govern any age-verification or age-estimation technology, arriving at a moment when the United States, United Kingdom, Australia, and EU member states are separately advancing binding age-verification mandates. <!-- verified: matches CNIL statement text retrieved via search --> The roundtable also produced a [CNIL compendium on smart glasses](https://www.cnil.fr/sites/default/files/2026-06/g7_dpas_compendium_of_approches_on_smart_glasses.pdf) and held discussions on agentic AI, though no formal statement was adopted on either topic. <!-- verified: independent search confirms smart glasses and agentic AI were discussion topics, not subjects of formal statements --> None of the G7 documents are binding; they function as soft-law guidance intended to shape national regulators' approaches and technical standards (e.g., ISO/IEC 27566 and IEEE 2089.1) as governments move toward mandatory age-assurance regimes. <!-- verified: IEEE 2089.1-2024 confirmed published May 2024; ISO/IEC 27566-2 and -3 confirmed in active development as of mid-2026 -->



## Background [HIGH confidence] [VERIFIED]

The G7 Data Protection and Privacy Authorities Roundtable is an annual forum, rotating with the G7 presidency, that brings together the privacy regulators of Canada, France, Germany, Italy, Japan, the United Kingdom, and the United States, along with the European Union as observer/participant. The 2026 edition — the sixth roundtable — was convened by CNIL in Paris on June 25–26, 2026, under the French G7 presidency, and followed the [2025 roundtable statement](https://www.priv.gc.ca/en/opc-news/speeches-and-statements/2025/js-dc-g7_20250619/) hosted by Canada and a 2023 roundtable (under the Japanese presidency) that produced a joint statement on generative AI ([OPC Canada announcement](https://www.priv.gc.ca/en/opc-news/news-and-announcements/2023/an_230622_g7/)). <!-- verified: independent search confirms 2023 roundtable (3rd) was held in Tokyo June 20-21 under Japan's PPC presidency, producing the Generative AI joint statement; 2025 roundtable (5th) confirmed hosted by Canada June 18-19, 2025 --> This year's meeting arrives against a backdrop of rapidly proliferating national age-verification mandates: the UK's Online Safety Act age-assurance duties took effect in 2025, Australia's social-media minimum-age law is being implemented, several US states (Texas, Colorado, and others) have enacted device- or platform-level age-verification statutes, and the EU is developing an age-verification app under the Digital Services Act. Age-assurance technology vendors have proliferated in parallel, raising DPA concerns that verification systems built to protect minors could themselves become new vectors for mass collection of biometric and identity data. The roundtable statement responds directly to this tension, positioning data protection authorities as a check on age-assurance mandates being enacted by other parts of government without privacy safeguards built in.

Separately, the joint paper on connected home devices continues a recognized DPA concern about smart TVs, voice assistants, and connected toys collecting children's data absent meaningful consent — an issue flagged in earlier CNIL and other national-authority guidance but not previously the subject of a joint G7 statement, per [CNIL's summary](https://www.cnil.fr/en/emerging-technologies-and-protection-children-g7-data-protection-authorities).

## Detailed Analysis [HIGH confidence] [VERIFIED]

**Age assurance statement.** According to [CNIL's official summary](https://www.cnil.fr/en/emerging-technologies-and-protection-children-g7-data-protection-authorities) and the [primary text of the statement](https://www.cnil.fr/sites/default/files/2026-06/g7_dpa_statement_on_age_assurance.pdf), the G7 DPAs call for age-assurance technologies that are "interoperable, trustworthy and privacy-preserving." Reporting from [Biometric Update](https://www.biometricupdate.com/202607/g7-backs-privacy-preserving-approach-to-age-assurance) indicates the statement builds on seven earlier "Common Principles" for age assurance separately adopted by G7 governments (as opposed to DPAs) — Principle One of which calls for "robust, reliable, privacy-preserving and interoperable age assurance solutions" to enable an "age-appropriate digital experience" for minors while protecting freedom of expression and privacy, including recommending parental consent as an alternative verification pathway. The DPA-level statement translates these political principles into specific data protection requirements:

- Age assurance must not create additional privacy risk and must not enable identification, tracking, profiling, or ongoing monitoring of individuals beyond the immediate age-determination purpose.
- Results of an age-assurance check should be usable only for the specific purpose that triggered the check (purpose limitation) — for example, a "yes/no" age-threshold signal rather than an exact birthdate or persistent identity credential.
- Collection, use, and retention of data for age assurance should be limited to what is strictly necessary (data minimization).
- Privacy-by-design should govern the technology throughout its lifecycle, including ongoing evaluation of effectiveness and proportionality.
- Age-assurance measures, because they restrict access, must be balanced against other rights, including freedom of expression and access to information.

The statement also notes the "continuing development of ISO/IEC 27566-2 and ISO/IEC 27566-3" and welcomes the publication of IEEE 2089.1-2024 as evidence of "growing alignment between international standards bodies" on age-assurance methodology, per [Biometric Update's reporting](https://www.biometricupdate.com/202606/g7-backs-privacy-preserving-age-assurance-as-japan-proposes-social-media-access-limits). <!-- verified: independent search confirms IEEE 2089.1-2024 published May 2024 and ISO/IEC 27566-2/-3 in active development stages as of 2026 -->

**Children's privacy and connected home devices.** The companion [joint paper](https://www.cnil.fr/sites/default/files/2026-06/g7_children_s_privacy_in_connected_home_devices_paper.pdf) addresses smart TVs, voice assistants, and connected toys. Per CNIL's summary, the G7 DPAs flagged that children frequently interact with these devices without awareness that their voices, images, or behavioral data are being collected, and that embedded online tracking technologies in such devices raise consent problems distinct from those on traditional web and app platforms.

**Smart glasses.** CNIL produced a [compendium of G7 DPA approaches to smart glasses](https://www.cnil.fr/sites/default/files/2026-06/g7_dpas_compendium_of_approches_on_smart_glasses.pdf), summarizing use cases, previously expressed data protection risks, and existing regulatory recommendations across member authorities, according to [law firm analysis from A&O Shearman](https://www.aoshearman.com/en/insights/ao-shearman-on-data/g7-data-protection-and-privacy-authorities-discuss-key-data-protection-issues). Concerns discussed include covert collection of third parties' images and voices, large-scale personal data collection, and heightened surveillance risk in public and private spaces. No standalone statement was adopted on smart glasses; the compendium is descriptive rather than prescriptive.

**Agentic AI.** The roundtable also discussed agentic AI systems' autonomy, increasingly complex supply chains, automated decision-making, and reduced human oversight, per both [CNIL](https://www.cnil.fr/en/emerging-technologies-and-protection-children-g7-data-protection-authorities) and [A&O Shearman's summary](https://www.aoshearman.com/en/insights/ao-shearman-on-data/g7-data-protection-and-privacy-authorities-discuss-key-data-protection-issues). As with smart glasses, this was framed as knowledge-sharing among DPAs rather than the basis for a formal joint statement — a contrast with 2023, when the G7 roundtable did issue a formal joint statement on generative AI. The Office of the Privacy Commissioner of Canada's [participation announcement](https://www.priv.gc.ca/en/opc-news/news-and-announcements/2026/nr-c-g7_260626/) confirms Canada's Privacy Commissioner attended and characterizes the meeting as strengthening "international cooperation" generally, without adding further substantive detail beyond CNIL's own materials.

None of these G7 outputs — the communiqué, the age-assurance statement, or the connected-devices paper — are legally binding on any jurisdiction. They function as soft-law guidance and signal likely convergence points for national regulators and technical standards bodies as individual G7 members finalize their own binding age-verification rules.

## Impact Assessment [MEDIUM confidence] [VERIFIED]

The immediate audience for this declaration is national and supranational regulators — data protection authorities in the G7 member states and the EU — who may cite the statement's principles (data minimization, purpose limitation, privacy-by-design, non-identifiability) when reviewing or enforcing against age-assurance implementations under existing law (e.g., GDPR, UK GDPR/DPA 2018, US state comprehensive privacy statutes, PIPEDA). Platforms and vendors building age-assurance products — including biometric age-estimation vendors, digital ID/wallet providers, and device manufacturers — should expect DPAs in G7 jurisdictions to apply these principles as an interpretive benchmark even though the statement itself creates no new legal obligation. Companies operating in jurisdictions actively legislating age verification (UK Online Safety Act enforcement, EU age-verification app rollout, and numerous 2026 US state laws such as Colorado's OS-level age-attestation statute <!-- verified: independent search confirms Colorado SB26-051, signed June 3, 2026, requires OS-level age attestation, effective January 1, 2028 -->) should treat the G7 principles as an indicator of the compliance expectations DPAs will bring to enforcement of those separate binding laws — particularly the requirement that age-assurance results not be repurposed or retained beyond the immediate verification need, and that biometric/identity data collected for age checks not create new tracking or profiling capability. The connected-home-devices paper likewise signals increased DPA scrutiny of smart TV, voice assistant, and connected toy manufacturers regarding children's data practices, an area with comparatively less binding regulation than social media age verification. No compliance deadlines attach to any of the June 2026 G7 documents; their significance lies in signaling future enforcement posture and standard-setting influence rather than imposing new immediate obligations.

## Action Items

- Age-assurance and biometric age-estimation vendors should benchmark product design against the G7 statement's data minimization, purpose-limitation, and privacy-by-design principles, particularly ahead of anticipated binding age-verification enforcement in the UK, EU, Australia, and multiple US states.
- Companies deploying connected home devices marketed to or used by children (smart TVs, voice assistants, connected toys) should review data collection and consent practices against the G7 joint paper's concerns regarding covert or unconsented data collection from minors.
- Legal and compliance teams should monitor whether national DPAs (CNIL, UK ICO, Canada OPC, US state AGs, Japan's PPC, Italy's Garante, Germany's federal/state DPAs) formally incorporate the G7 age-assurance principles into enforcement guidance or rulemaking over the next 6–12 months.
- Monitor the finalization of ISO/IEC 27566-2 and 27566-3 age-assurance standards, which the G7 statement references as an alignment point for future technical compliance.
- Track parallel binding age-verification developments (UK Online Safety Act enforcement, EU Digital Services Act age-verification app, US state legislation) for how closely implementing regulators hew to the G7 principles.
