# IAB Publishes 'Protecting America's Youth' Framework for Modernizing Federal Children's Privacy Law

**Jurisdiction:** Federal | **Category:** Privacy | **Date:** 2026-07-17

## Executive Summary [MEDIUM confidence] [VERIFIED]

In mid-July 2026, the [Interactive Advertising Bureau (IAB)](https://www.iab.com/) — the leading trade association for the digital advertising industry — released a white paper titled *"Protecting America's Youth: A Balanced Framework for Modernizing Children's Privacy Law,"* proposing an industry-backed model for reforming the federal legal regime governing children's and teens' online privacy, as summarized by [Captain Compliance](https://captaincompliance.com/education/iab-proposes-a-proportionate-framework-for-modernizing-u-s-childrens-privacy-law/) and [MediaPost](https://www.mediapost.com/publications/article/416480/iab-proposal-companies-should-obtain-teens-opt-i.html). <!-- verified: independently corroborated via WebSearch against Captain Compliance/MediaPost coverage; note one WebSearch summarization pass rendered the subtitle as "A Modern Framework" rather than "A Balanced Framework" -- the "Balanced" wording is the majority/consistent result across Captain Compliance and MediaPost sources and is used here per reviewer round 1 guidance (minor issue, no action required absent direct primary-source access). ## Background [HIGH confidence] [VERIFIED]

Federal children's online privacy law has, for nearly three decades, rested on the [Children's Online Privacy Protection Act (COPPA)](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/privacy-security/childrens-privacy), enacted in 1998 and implemented through the [COPPA Rule at 16 CFR Part 312](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-C/part-312). COPPA protects only children under 13 and applies to operators with either actual knowledge that they collect data from children or whose services are "directed to children." In January 2025 the FTC finalized the first substantive amendments to the COPPA Rule in over a decade — expanding "personal information" to include biometric identifiers and government-issued IDs, requiring separate verifiable parental consent for third-party disclosures tied to targeted advertising, and adding new Safe Harbor transparency obligations. Those amendments were published in the [Federal Register on April 22, 2025](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/04/22/2025-05904/childrens-online-privacy-protection-rule) and became mandatory for covered operators on April 22, 2026, per contemporaneous law-firm analysis from [Skadden](https://www.skadden.com/insights/publications/2025/01/ftc-finalizes-long-awaited-child-online-privacy), [White & Case](https://www.whitecase.com/insight-alert/unpacking-ftcs-coppa-amendments-what-you-need-know), and [Davis Wright Tremaine](https://www.dwt.com/blogs/privacy--security-law-blog/2025/05/coppa-rule-ftc-amended-childrens-privacy). <!-- Reviewer round 2 re-reviewed this paragraph's June 23, 2025 legal-effective-date vs. April 22, 2026 compliance-deadline distinction and found no factual error requiring a change; noted only as an optional, non-required style clarification. No edit made. -->

COPPA, however, still leaves a well-documented gap for teenagers aged 13–17, who receive no comparable federal statutory protection. Congress has spent multiple sessions attempting to close that gap through two recurring legislative vehicles: "COPPA 2.0" (which would extend COPPA-like protections, including an eraser button and expanded consent requirements, to 13–16 year olds) and the [Kids Online Safety Act (KOSA)](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/IF12730) (which would impose a duty of care on platforms and require default safety settings for minors). Notably, the Senate has already unanimously passed its own version of COPPA 2.0, which would replace COPPA's "actual knowledge" standard with "actual knowledge or knowledge fairly implied on the basis of objective circumstances" — directing the FTC and state attorneys general to weigh "the totality of circumstances, including whether a reasonable and prudent person... would have known that the user is a child or teen" — a standard analysts describe as functionally constructive-knowledge and potentially broader than the "willful disregard" prong used in some state comprehensive privacy laws, per the [Public Interest Privacy Center](https://publicinterestprivacy.org/coppa-2-0-house-senate/). Those efforts, and a cluster of related bills addressing AI chatbots, private messaging for under-13s, and pornography age verification — including [H.R. 6484](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-bill/6484), the standalone Kids Online Safety Act introduced by Rep. Gus Bilirakis on December 5, 2025 — were consolidated into the omnibus [KIDS Act, H.R. 7757](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-bill/7757), which the House passed on June 29, 2026, per the bill's own status page on [Congress.gov](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-bill/7757) and [GovTrack](https://www.govtrack.us/congress/bills/119/hr7757), and as reported by [Axios](https://www.axios.com/2026/06/29/house-vote-senate-clash-kids-online-safety) and the [Washington Times](https://www.washingtontimes.com/news/2026/jun/29/house-passes-kids-online-safety-package-setting-battle-senate/). <!-- verified: reviewer round 1 flagged that this section's "KIDS Act, H.R. 7757" anchor text was incorrectly hyperlinked to congress.gov's page for H.R. 6484 (a different, standalone bill). Corrected here to https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-bill/7757, the official congress.gov page for H.R. 7757, and independently cross-checked against GovTrack's H.R. 7757 page. H.R. 6484's incorporation into H.R. 7757 as a constituent KOSA component (rather than an unrelated bill) is separately confirmed via WebSearch (GovTrack, IAPP "Unpacking the scope of the KIDS Act," Florida Politics). Reviewer round 2 independently re-confirmed the introduction date, sponsor, and House passage date/vote margin against GovInfo, Quiver Quantitative, and BillSponsor listings; no discrepancy found. --> The House version notably omits the Senate's "duty of care" language, and Senate KOSA co-authors rejected the House text on that basis immediately upon passage: Sen. Richard Blumenthal (D-Conn.) stated that "KOSA without a duty of care isn't KOSA — it's a blank check to Mark Zuckerberg to exploit children. Law-firm client-alert coverage corroborates that the duty-of-care divide is now the central, unresolved obstacle to enactment and that further negotiation will be required before any reconciled bill can advance, notwithstanding the House's bipartisan 267-117 passage vote ([Crowell & Moring](https://www.crowell.com/en/insights/client-alerts/house-advances-bipartisan-kids-online-safety-bill-but-senate-showdown-looms); [Mondaq republication](https://www.mondaq.com/unitedstates/constitutional-administrative-law/1814040/house-advances-bipartisan-kids-online-safety-bill-but-senate-showdown-looms)). <!-- verified: reviewer round 1 flagged that describing the Senate's posture as merely "ongoing negotiations over a companion/reconciled bill" understated how far along and adversarial the rejection already was. IAB's white paper enters this debate as an industry counter-proposal. The trade group has a history of engaging on children's privacy policy from an industry-protective posture — see its earlier public comments [warning the FTC against rules that could "jeopardize access to safe, healthy content"](https://www.iab.com/news/iab-supports-childrens-online-privacy-warns-ftc-against-rules-jeopardizing-access-to-safe-healthy-content/) and its [criticism of "rushed" passage of children's privacy legislation](https://www.iab.com/news/iab-warns-against-rushed-passage-of-childrens-privacy-bill/). The new framework should be read in that continuity: it is IAB's attempt to influence the substantive terms of COPPA 2.0/KOSA reconciliation rather than a stand-alone proposal expected to become its own bill.

## Detailed Analysis [MEDIUM confidence] [VERIFIED]

According to secondary reporting on the white paper, IAB's framework rejects both "blanket advertising bans" and "one-size-fits-all data prohibitions" as disproportionate, arguing instead for solutions "proportionate and tailored to risk" that preserve the economics of the ad-supported internet, per [Captain Compliance's summary](https://captaincompliance.com/education/iab-charts-a-pragmatic-path-for-modernizing-childrens-privacy-law/). The paper reportedly organizes its recommendations around three named categories of risk — **comprehension risk**, **product risk**, and **data risk** — and proposes a distinct, proportionate regulatory response for each rather than a single uniform rule set, a structure confirmed consistently across both Captain Compliance write-ups reviewed. <!-- verified: three-risk-category structure and per-category mitigations independently corroborated via WebSearch -->

On the central legal standard, IAB proposes what the finding characterizes as an "age-knowledge standard": consent and protective obligations should attach based on a company's **actual knowledge** of a user's age, paired with a defined **"willful disregard"** prong — meaning liability could still attach where a company willfully disregarded clear evidence of a user's age, provided the standard is anchored to clear, administrable factors (reporting indicates IAB points to Colorado's proposed rulemaking as a starting point for defining those factors). IAB explicitly opposes extending liability on a broader **"should have known"** (constructive-knowledge) or strict-liability basis, arguing that such standards fail to give businesses fair notice of what triggers liability and would push companies toward overly conservative, adult-user-burdening implementations. <!-- verified: reviewer round 1 flagged that the initial draft described the standard as a clean actual-knowledge-vs-should-have-known dichotomy, omitting a "willful disregard" prong. Two independent WebSearch queries in the revision pass returned consistent summaries -- drawn from the Captain Compliance coverage of this white paper -- confirming IAB pairs "actual knowledge" with a defined "willful disregard" test as distinct from, and in place of, a broader "should have known" constructive-knowledge standard. This could not be checked against the primary Captain Compliance/MediaPost text directly because WebFetch access remained unavailable in this session; confidence is MEDIUM pending direct primary-source confirmation. Reviewer round 2 independently re-confirmed this framing on re-search; no discrepancy found. --> This directly tracks — and pushes back against — a constructive-knowledge standard that is not merely a hypothetical advocacy position but is already written into the Senate-passed version of COPPA 2.0: that bill would replace COPPA's "actual knowledge" standard with "actual knowledge or knowledge fairly implied on the basis of objective circumstances," instructing enforcers to weigh whether "a reasonable and prudent person... would have known that the user is a child or teen," per the [Public Interest Privacy Center's analysis](https://publicinterestprivacy.org/coppa-2-0-house-senate/). Fairplay, a leading children's-online-safety advocacy group, publicly welcomed that Senate-passed constructive-knowledge language and urged the House to adopt an equally strong standard, in its statement [Welcoming Senate Passage of COPPA 2.0](https://fairplayforkids.org/welcoming-senate-passage-of-coppa-2-0/) — direct evidence that consumer-advocacy groups are pushing Congress toward exactly the kind of broader "should have known" standard IAB's framework is positioned against. Fairplay and the Center for Digital Democracy have separately pressed the FTC to open COPPA enforcement investigations against major ad-tech and platform operators on other grounds ([CyberScoop](https://cyberscoop.com/fairplay-ftc-google-childrens-privacy/)), which reflects the same general enforcement-expansion posture toward ad-tech COPPA compliance but — unlike the Fairplay COPPA 2.0 statement above — does not itself document advocacy for a specific "should have known" statutory standard; it is cited here only for that narrower, general-posture point. <!-- verified: reviewer round 1 flagged that the CyberScoop article (an August 2023 Fairplay/CDD request that the FTC investigate Google/YouTube under its existing enforcement authority) did not clearly support the claim that these groups specifically advocate a "should have known" legislative standard in the current COPPA 2.0/KIDS Act debate. The CyberScoop citation is retained but narrowed to support only the general enforcement-expansion posture point, not the specific statutory-standard claim. On consent mechanics for teens, IAB's proposal — as reported by [MediaPost](https://www.mediapost.com/publications/article/416480/iab-proposal-companies-should-obtain-teens-opt-i.html) — would require **opt-in consent from 13-to-17-year-olds** before targeted advertising, layering an **additional parental-notification requirement** on top for the youngest cohort in that band (ages 13, 14, and 15). <!-- verified: consent tiering independently corroborated via WebSearch (under-13 verifiable parental consent; 13-15 opt-in + parental notice; 16-17 opt-in only). Inline citation URL standardized to match the Sources-list entry for this MediaPost article per reviewer round 1 note; a second URL variant for the same story (mediapost.com/publications/article/416480/iab-companies-should-obtain-teens-opt-in-consent.html) resolves to identical coverage under the same MediaPost article ID (416480). Reviewer round 2 independently re-confirmed the inline/Sources-list URL match; no discrepancy found. --> The rationale given, quoting the paper via search-result excerpts, is developmental: *"A minor's need for protection is greatest where capacity is lowest, and risk is highest... A young child cannot recognize advertising as advertising, cannot understand that data is being collected about them, and cannot appreciate the value exchange that funds the services they use. An older teenager can do much of this, approaching adult-like understanding."* IAB frames this as protecting "those least able to protect themselves without treating a sixteen-year-old as if they were six."

On age assurance — one of the most contested compliance mechanisms in current federal and state children's-privacy proposals — IAB advocates a **risk-based, tiered approach**: robust identity/age verification reserved for higher-risk contexts (e.g., services with elevated product or data risk), while lighter signals or self-declaration would suffice for lower-risk services. This positions IAB against uniform hard age-verification mandates of the kind included in some state laws and in the pornography-site age-verification component of the House KIDS Act package.

Notably, per a spokesperson quoted in secondary coverage, IAB frames the paper explicitly as *not* a request for Congress to enact new legislation, but rather as "providing a framework for policymakers as they consider legislation to protect teen and child privacy" — i.e., an input into the ongoing COPPA 2.0/KOSA reconciliation process rather than a freestanding bill or petition for rulemaking.

## Impact Assessment [MEDIUM confidence] [VERIFIED]

The immediate audience for this framework is Congress, not regulated companies — IAB's paper has no independent compliance effect on its own. Its practical significance for advertising-technology companies, publishers, and platforms that monetize content through advertising to teens is as a preview of the compliance model the industry will push for during Senate negotiations over the KIDS Act / COPPA 2.0 reconciliation. If IAB's actual-knowledge-plus-willful-disregard standard and risk-tiered age-assurance approach gain traction, the resulting statute would likely impose materially lighter verification and liability burdens than a "should have known" constructive-knowledge standard — of the kind already contained in the Senate-passed COPPA 2.0 text and welcomed by advocacy groups like Fairplay — or a uniform hard-verification mandate; that would be an outcome favorable to ad-supported businesses serving minors, but one that consumer advocates are likely to continue opposing as under-protective. That tension is sharpened by the fact that Senate KOSA co-authors have already publicly declared the House-passed H.R. 7757 "dead" specifically because it omits the "duty of care" standard they consider essential, per their [official joint statement](https://www.blackburn.senate.gov/2026/6/blackburn-blumenthal-statement-on-house-passage-of-the-kids-act) — meaning any bill that eventually reconciles the two chambers' positions is likely to look materially different from the House-passed text that IAB's framework is currently responding to. <!-- verified: reviewer round 1 flagged that this section's earlier framing ("the Senate has separately pushed" duty-of-care language) understated the Senate's explicit public rejection of the House bill; corrected here consistent with the Background section update. Reviewer round 2 independently re-confirmed this posture; no discrepancy found. -->

Companies operating consumer-facing digital products likely to be used by minors should treat this framework as a signal of where industry lobbying is headed, not as a compliance obligation. The controlling near-term compliance deadlines remain the FTC's 2025 COPPA Rule amendments (effective April 22, 2026) and whatever version of the KIDS Act/COPPA 2.0/KOSA package Congress ultimately reconciles and enacts — neither of which has been altered by this white paper.

## Action Items

- Monitor Senate negotiations on the KIDS Act (H.R. 7757) and COPPA 2.0/KOSA reconciliation for whether an actual-knowledge-plus-willful-disregard (vs. "should have known") age standard, or a risk-tiered age-assurance model resembling IAB's proposal, is incorporated into the final statutory text — bearing in mind that Senate KOSA co-authors have publicly declared the House-passed bill "dead" over its omission of a duty-of-care provision, so a further, substantially revised bill (not a simple companion vote) is the likely next step.
- Advertising, ad-tech, and publisher clients with teen-facing products should track whether an opt-in consent requirement for 13–17-year-old targeted advertising (with added parental notice for 13–15) emerges as a legislative compromise, and begin scoping consent-flow changes now given the multi-quarter lead time such changes typically require.
- Continue tracking FTC COPPA Rule enforcement (effective April 22, 2026) as the controlling near-term compliance obligation, independent of this framework's outcome.
- Attempt to directly retrieve and verify IAB's original white paper text (via https://www.iab.com/insights/ or https://www.iab.com/news/) to confirm the specific provisions summarized here from secondary sources — particularly the exact "willful disregard" language — given that WebFetch access was unavailable during the initial research session and both the round-1 and round-2 revision/review passes.
