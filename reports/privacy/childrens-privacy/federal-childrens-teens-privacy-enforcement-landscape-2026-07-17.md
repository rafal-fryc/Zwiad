# The State of Enforcement: Kids' and Teens' Privacy Across US States and Federal Agencies (Mid-2026 Landscape)

**Jurisdiction:** Federal, Texas, New Mexico, California, Multistate | **Category:** Privacy | **Date:** 2026-07-13

## Executive Summary [MEDIUM confidence] [NEEDS REVIEW]

An [IAPP analysis published July 13, 2026](https://iapp.org/news/a/the-state-of-enforcement-part-ii-kids-and-teens-privacy) — the second installment of a three-part enforcement retrospective — surveys the intensifying US children's and teens' privacy enforcement landscape heading into the second half of 2026. Independent research confirms the underlying trend the article describes: the FTC has continued aggressive COPPA enforcement following the January 2025 rule amendments, state attorneys general (led by Texas) have filed or advanced major lawsuits against social platforms under state-specific children's privacy statutes, a multistate social-media-addiction litigation track has produced nine-figure jury verdicts and confidential settlements, and Congress has advanced COPPA 2.0-style teen-privacy protections through both chambers via separate vehicles — the Senate's S.836 and, as of June 29, 2026, the House's H.R. 7757 (KIDS Act) — that must now be reconciled. Companies operating consumer-facing digital products aimed at or reasonably likely to be used by minors face a materially higher enforcement risk profile than in prior years, from both federal regulators and a growing bloc of state AGs. ## Background [MEDIUM confidence] [VERIFIED]

Children's and teens' online privacy enforcement in the United States sits at the intersection of a decades-old federal statute and a fast-growing patchwork of state laws. The [Children's Online Privacy Protection Act (COPPA)](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/privacy-security/childrens-privacy), enacted in 1998 and implemented through the [COPPA Rule at 16 CFR Part 312](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-C/part-312), has long protected only children under 13. In January 2025, the FTC finalized the first substantive amendments to the Rule in over a decade, expanding "personal information" to include biometric identifiers and government-issued IDs, requiring separate opt-in parental consent for third-party data sharing, and mandating written information security programs. Compliance became mandatory April 22, 2026 (see the related report on this site, `reports/privacy/childrens-privacy/federal-coppa-enforcement-begins-2026-05-04.md`). <!-- verified -->

Since that compliance deadline, the FTC has pursued a steady cadence of settlements, including [Disney's $10 million COPPA settlement](https://natlawreview.com/article/court-approves-disneys-10-million-ftc-settlement-resolving-coppa-enforcement-action) (court-approved December 2025) over YouTube kid-directed content, a settlement with [Apitor Technology](https://www.corporatecomplianceinsights.com/what-recent-ftc-enforcement-actions-reveal-coppa-risks/) over geolocation data shared with a third party in China without parental consent, and an enforcement action against anonymous-messaging app [Sendit and its CEO Hunter Rice](https://www.corporatecomplianceinsights.com/what-recent-ftc-enforcement-actions-reveal-coppa-risks/) (announced September 29, 2025) for COPPA and Restore Online Shoppers' Confidence Act violations. Game developer Cognosphere separately paid [$20 million](https://www.corporatecomplianceinsights.com/what-recent-ftc-enforcement-actions-reveal-coppa-risks/) in January 2025 over failure to obtain parental consent even after learning specific users were minors. <!-- verified -->

At the state level, statutes modeled on the "Age-Appropriate Design Code" and parental-consent frameworks — including Texas's Securing Children Online through Parental Empowerment (SCOPE) Act, Maryland's Age-Appropriate Design Code Act, and similar laws in other states — have given state AGs independent civil-penalty authority separate from COPPA. Federal COPPA itself also authorizes state AGs to sue directly under the federal statute: under [15 U.S.C. § 6504](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/15/6504), a state attorney general may bring a civil action in federal court on behalf of state residents for violations of the COPPA Rule, and such suits draw on the FTC's own inflation-adjusted civil penalty schedule -- currently [up to $53,088 per violation](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/complying-coppa-frequently-asked-questions) -- rather than a separate federal state-AG-specific range. <!-- verified: confirmed against Cornell LII statutory text and FTC COPPA FAQ in round 2 review --> (An earlier draft of this report attributed a "$5,000 to $50,000 per violation" range to federal law for state AG COPPA suits; that figure could not be verified against 15 U.S.C. § 6504 or FTC guidance and instead traces to distinct *state* statutory penalties -- e.g., New York's SAFE for Kids Act and Florida's state child-online-safety statute -- which are separate from, and layered on top of, federal COPPA's own penalty cap. That range has been removed to avoid conflating state-specific penalties with federal law.) State-specific statutes, including SCOPE Act and similar design-code laws, carry their own independent penalty schedules discussed below.

## Detailed Analysis [MEDIUM confidence] [VERIFIED]

**State attorney general actions.** Texas has emerged as the most active state enforcer. Attorney General Ken Paxton [sued Snap Inc. in February 2026](https://www.texasattorneygeneral.gov/news/releases/attorney-general-paxton-sues-snapchat-deceiving-parents-endangering-texas-kids-exposing-them), alleging Snapchat misrepresented its safety to parents (including via a "12+" app-store rating) while exposing minors to sexual content, drug-related content, and addictive design features, and further alleging violations of specific SCOPE Act provisions: [Section 509.101](https://www.texasattorneygeneral.gov/sites/default/files/files/press/Petition-4.pdf) (failure to use a commercially reasonable parental-identity-verification method), Section 509.052 (unlawfully sharing, disclosing, or selling known minors' personal identifying information without verified parental consent), and Section 509.054 (failure to provide parental oversight tools for known-minor accounts). Texas seeks civil penalties of up to $10,000 per violation plus injunctive relief. <!-- verified: independently confirmed via Texas Tribune, Courthouse News, and the primary petition PDF --> Paxton separately [opened investigations into Character.AI, Reddit, Instagram, Discord, and other platforms](https://www.texasattorneygeneral.gov/news/releases/attorney-general-ken-paxton-launches-investigations-characterai-reddit-instagram-discord-and-other-companies) over children's privacy and safety practices, underscoring a multi-target enforcement posture rather than a single-defendant strategy.

**Multistate social-media-addiction litigation -- three distinct proceedings.** Running parallel to statutory enforcement, litigation against Meta, Snap, TikTok/ByteDance, and YouTube/Alphabet over alleged addictive design and harm to minors has produced significant financial exposure across three legally separate tracks that are often discussed together but should not be conflated:

1. *State of New Mexico v. Meta* -- a New Mexico attorney-general enforcement action under that state's consumer-protection statutes, tried in two phases before First Judicial District Judge Bryan Biedscheid in Santa Fe. In the first (jury) phase, a Santa Fe jury [found Meta liable for $375 million](https://www.spencer-law.com/post/social-media-addiction-lawsuits-2026-kgm-trial-mdl-3047) based on 75,000 violations of New Mexico's Unfair Practices Act at the statutory $5,000-per-violation cap (verdict reported March 24, 2026). A second, non-jury (bench) phase followed, focused on abatement relief; it concluded in Santa Fe in late May/June 2026. State experts testified that fully remediating the alleged harms statewide over 15 years would cost approximately $3.7 billion, and in a June 2026 filing New Mexico's attorney general asked Judge Biedscheid to order Meta to pay [$953 million into a dedicated fund](https://sourcenm.com/2026/06/17/new-mexico-ag-seeks-nearly-1-billion-payment-from-meta-in-second-phase-of-trial/) as Meta's "equitable share of the cost of abating" the harm, with unspent funds returned to Meta after 15 years. The $3.7 billion figure is thus the state's estimate of the full 15-year societal remediation cost, not a sum sought from Meta directly; the specific abatement payment requested from Meta was $953 million. As of this report's last update, Judge Biedscheid had not yet ruled on the abatement remedy. <!-- verified: round 3 independent re-verification via sourcenm.com, santafenewmexican.com, and topclassactions.com confirms the $3.7B/$953M distinction and the "equitable share" framing are accurately characterized; ruling remained pending as of this report's last_updated date -->
2. *K.G.M. v. Meta et al.* -- a bellwether personal-injury trial in the California Judicial Council Coordination Proceeding (JCCP No. 5255), heard in Los Angeles County Superior Court and legally distinct from both the New Mexico case and the federal MDL. Defendants Snap (settled January 22, 2026) and TikTok (settled January 27, 2026) reached confidential settlements with the plaintiff shortly before jury selection, without admitting liability, [leaving Meta and Google/YouTube as the remaining defendants at trial](https://www.spencer-law.com/post/social-media-addiction-lawsuits-2026-kgm-trial-mdl-3047). The case did not remain pending: on **March 25, 2026 -- one day after the New Mexico jury verdict** -- a Los Angeles jury returned a verdict against Meta and Google/YouTube, finding both companies negligent and liable for failure to warn. The jury awarded **$6 million total** ($3 million compensatory, $3 million punitive), apportioned as [$4.2 million against Meta (70% liability) and $1.8 million against Google/YouTube (30% liability)](https://abcnews.com/GMA/News/jury-returns-verdict-meta-youtube-landmark-social-media/story?id=131121474). Meta has stated it will appeal. <!-- verified: reconfirmed round 2 via CNBC, NBC News, EPIC, Pearl Cohen, Spencer Law -->
3. *In re: Social Media Adolescent Addiction/Personal Injury Products Liability Litigation, MDL No. 3047* -- the coordinated federal multidistrict litigation pending before Judge Yvonne Gonzalez Rogers in the Northern District of California, encompassing thousands of individual and school-district plaintiffs. This federal track remains ongoing and is distinct from both California state-court proceedings above.

Separately from all three tracks, Meta, Snap, Alphabet, and ByteDance reached a [$27 million settlement with the Breathitt County (Kentucky) School District](https://mdlupdate.com/mdl/3047-social-media-adolescent-addiction/), a case within the MDL 3047 coordination. <!-- verified: confirmed round 1 --> These are civil tort/consumer-protection outcomes rather than privacy-statute enforcement per se, but IAPP's survey treats them as part of the same enforcement landscape given their reliance on similar underlying data-collection-from-minors allegations.

**Federal legislative development.** On March 5, 2026, the US Senate [unanimously passed S.836, the Children and Teens' Online Privacy Protection Act ("COPPA 2.0")](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/senate-bill/836), sponsored by Sen. Edward Markey (D-MA) with Sen. Bill Cassidy (R-LA) as lead co-sponsor. The bill would extend COPPA-style protections to teens ages 13-16 (current COPPA covers only under-13), ban targeted advertising to minors, add data-deletion rights, and extend statutory coverage to mobile apps and connected-device services beyond the original website-centric text. This was the bill's second Senate passage in successive sessions without final enactment, a pattern several outlets flagged as a recurring congressional stall. <!-- verified -->

That stall shifted materially on **June 29, 2026**, when the House passed [H.R. 7757, the KIDS Act](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-bill/7757/text), as amended, under suspension of the rules by a **267-117 vote**. <!-- verified: round 3 independent re-verification via GovTrack, Clerk of the House Roll Call 228, Pillsbury, Crowell & Moring, and CyberSecGuru all confirm the 267-117 tally and June 29, 2026 date --> H.R. 7757 is a broader legislative package that folds in COPPA 2.0-style provisions alongside elements of the Kids Online Safety Act, the Safe Messaging for Kids Act, the SPY Kids Act, the Safer GAMING Act, the SAFE Bots Act, and data-broker disclosure and online-safety-education provisions. Its COPPA-2.0 component would bring 13-year-olds into COPPA's "child" category, create separate teen protections through age 17, add a "should have known" knowledge standard, restrict individual-specific advertising to children and teens, and impose new data access, deletion, correction, and retention obligations, per coverage from [Crowell & Moring](https://www.crowell.com/en/insights/client-alerts/house-advances-bipartisan-kids-online-safety-bill-but-senate-showdown-looms) and [Pillsbury Law](https://www.pillsburylaw.com/en/news-and-insights/kids-act-house-ai-chatbot-safety.html). Critically, H.R. 7757's text differs materially from the Senate's S.836 text -- it is a different bill number carrying overlapping but non-identical provisions -- so the two chambers must reconcile the House and Senate approaches before any COPPA 2.0-style teen-privacy protections can be enacted. Reporting on the House vote also notes some Senate sponsors have already criticized the House package over the omission of a duty-of-care provision present in Senate proposals, signaling reconciliation is not a formality. As of this report's last update (July 17, 2026), no conference or reconciled text had been enacted or signed into law. <!-- verified: round 3 independent search confirms reconciliation remains unresolved and contested (Senate Commerce sponsors have signaled the House's omission of a duty-of-care provision is unacceptable), consistent with the report's framing -->

## Impact Assessment [MEDIUM confidence] [VERIFIED]

Companies operating any digital product, app, or online service that collects data from users under 18 — even where the product is not explicitly marketed to children — face converging exposure from three independent channels: (1) FTC COPPA Rule enforcement, including direct FTC actions and state-AG suits brought under the federal statute (civil penalties up to $53,088 per violation under current inflation-adjusted caps), (2) state-specific children's/teens' privacy and design-code statutes carrying their own separate civil-penalty schedules (e.g., Texas SCOPE Act penalties up to $10,000 per violation), and (3) state consumer-protection and product-liability litigation over "addictive design," which has already produced a $375 million New Mexico jury verdict (with a further $953 million abatement payment sought from Meta and a court ruling still pending), a $6 million California jury verdict against Meta and Google/YouTube (with an announced Meta appeal), and multiple eight-figure settlements. Social media, gaming, ed-tech, and messaging platforms are the most exposed sectors given the fact patterns in current cases (Snap, Meta, TikTok, Character.AI, Discord, Reddit all named in active investigations or suits). At the federal legislative level, both chambers of Congress have now separately passed teen-privacy legislation -- the Senate's S.836 and the House's broader H.R. 7757 (KIDS Act) -- but the two bills' differing scope and provisions (including the duty-of-care dispute) mean reconciliation, not simple enactment, is the live open question. Covered entities should begin scenario-planning for either outcome: if a reconciled bill is enacted, companies would need to extend parental/teen consent and deletion mechanisms to the 13-17 age band and eliminate individual-specific advertising to that cohort — a substantial compliance lift for any ad-supported consumer platform with a known teen user base.

## Action Items

- Confirm current COPPA Rule compliance (biometric data handling, third-party disclosure consent, written information security program) ahead of continued FTC enforcement activity; see the related report on the April 22, 2026 compliance deadline.
- If operating in Texas, review SCOPE Act obligations specifically: parental identity-verification method, restrictions on sharing known-minors' PII, and parental oversight tool availability — Texas is currently the most active state enforcer.
- Monitor House/Senate reconciliation of H.R. 7757 (KIDS Act, passed the House June 29, 2026, 267-117) against the Senate's S.836; the two bills' provisions differ and a duty-of-care dispute has already surfaced. If a reconciled bill advances, begin scoping compliance changes for the 13-17 age band (consent mechanisms, ad-targeting restrictions, deletion rights).
- Track all three social-media-addiction litigation tracks separately: New Mexico state-AG enforcement ($375M jury verdict plus a pending abatement ruling on the AG's $953M request), the California JCCP 5255 K.G.M. bellwether (verdict rendered, Meta appeal pending), and federal MDL 3047 (still coordinating additional bellwether trials) -- each carries distinct precedential and financial implications for product design obligations.

## Update 2026-07-17

**Change:** Senate unanimously passed COPPA 2.0 (S.836) for the second consecutive session; FTC enforcing amended COPPA Rule following the April 22, 2026 compliance deadline → House versions of COPPA 2.0 and KOSA stalled over unresolved state-preemption clauses and lack of bipartisan sponsorship; FTC issued a February 25, 2026 enforcement-discretion policy statement on age-verification technology and reaffirmed COPPA enforcement as a 2026 priority
**Source:** https://www.loeb.com/en/insights/publications/2026/06/childrens-online-privacy-in-2026-congress-stalls-again-ftc-signals-priorities
**Summary:** A June 2026 Loeb & Loeb client alert, 'Children's Online Privacy in 2026: Congress Stalls (Again), FTC Signals Priorities', adds two developments beyond what this report previously covered. First, the House versions of both the Kids Online Safety Act (KOSA) and COPPA 2.0 were amended to add broad state-law preemption clauses, but the House COPPA 2.0 bill has no Democratic co-sponsor and the House KOSA preemption language has drawn Democratic pushback, deepening the partisan stalemate beyond the Senate's unanimous S.836 passage already noted in this report. Second, on February 25, 2026 the FTC issued an Enforcement Policy Statement stating it will not bring COPPA Rule enforcement actions against general-audience or mixed-audience operators that collect, use, and disclose personal information solely to determine a user's age via age-verification technology, provided certain conditions are met; the Commission also signaled intent to open a formal COPPA Rule review addressing age-verification mechanisms more broadly. FTC Division of Privacy and Identity Protection Associate Director Ben Wiseman separately confirmed that enforcing the updated COPPA Rule remains a 'key focus' for the agency in 2026.
