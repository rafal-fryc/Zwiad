# Maryland Enacts Age-Appropriate Design Code: UDAP Penalties and MODPA Overlap Create Compounding Compliance Obligations

**Jurisdiction:** Maryland | **Category:** Privacy | **Date:** 2024-05-15

## Executive Summary [HIGH confidence]

On May 9, 2024, Governor Wes Moore signed into law two companion privacy statutes: [House Bill 603 (the Maryland Age-Appropriate Design Code Act, "Maryland Kids Code")](https://mgaleg.maryland.gov/mgawebsite/Legislation/Details/hb0603?ys=2024RS) and [Senate Bill 541 (the Maryland Online Data Privacy Act, "MODPA")](https://mgaleg.maryland.gov/mgawebsite/Legislation/Details/sb0541?ys=2024RS). Maryland thereby became the second U.S. state to enact a children's design code modeled on the United Kingdom's Age-Appropriate Design Code, following California's enjoined attempt. Both laws expressly incorporate Maryland's Unfair, Deceptive, or Abusive Acts or Practices (UDAP) statute, creating a dual penalty structure: design-code-specific per-child fines and UDAP-based civil penalties that can each be triggered by the same violation. The overlap between the two laws — on data minimization, DPIA requirements, and prohibitions on processing minors' data for targeted advertising — creates compounding compliance obligations for businesses that are subject to both. As of April 1, 2026, MODPA enforcement has commenced. The Maryland Kids Code has been effective since October 1, 2024, though its constitutionality remains under challenge in [NetChoice v. Brown](https://www2.mdd.uscourts.gov/Opinions/Opinions/Memorandum%20Opinion_NetChoice%20v.%20Brown%20(RDB-25-0322).pdf), where the U.S. District Court for the District of Maryland denied the State's motion to dismiss in November 2025.

## Background [HIGH confidence]

### The California AADC Precedent

Maryland's Kids Code did not emerge in a vacuum. The United Kingdom's Age-Appropriate Design Code (UK Children's Code) had influenced California's legislature to enact the California AADC in 2022. However, in September 2023, a federal district court [preliminarily enjoined California's AADC on First Amendment grounds](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-information-security-law/new-lawsuit-challenges-marylands-age-appropriate-design-code-act), finding that it likely compelled speech and imposed unconstitutional prior restraints. Maryland's legislature responded by working with constitutional experts to modify key provisions — particularly, adding a statutory definition of "best interests of the child" — aimed at reducing First Amendment exposure.

### Simultaneous Enactment with MODPA

Maryland's General Assembly passed HB 603 and SB 541 on April 6 and April 8, 2024, respectively, and Governor Moore signed both bills on the same date — May 9, 2024. The parallel enactment was deliberate: the General Assembly designed the Kids Code as a complement to MODPA's comprehensive consumer privacy framework rather than as a standalone statute. Together, the two laws constitute what [Troutman Pepper described](https://www.troutman.com/insights/maryland-enacts-age-appropriate-design-code/) as a layered privacy regime covering both general consumer data practices (MODPA) and child-specific design obligations (Kids Code).

### Legislative Threshold Divergence

A structural feature of the two-law scheme is that each law uses a different applicability threshold, which Troutman Pepper flagged as a source of significant compliance complexity. MODPA applies to entities processing personal data of 35,000 or more Maryland consumers (or 10,000+ consumers with 20%+ revenue from data sales). The Kids Code adopts CCPA/CPRA thresholds: $25 million annual gross revenues, buying/selling/sharing personal data of 50,000+ consumers/households/devices, or deriving 50%+ of annual revenue from data sales. Because these thresholds do not align, businesses must independently assess coverage under each law — a company covered by MODPA may not be covered by the Kids Code, and vice versa.

## Detailed Analysis [HIGH confidence]

### Maryland Kids Code (HB 603): Core Provisions

The [enrolled text of HB 603](https://mgaleg.maryland.gov/2024RS/Chapters_noln/CH_461_hb0603t.pdf) (Chapter 461, Acts of 2024) establishes the following framework:

**Covered Entities.** The Kids Code applies to for-profit entities doing business in Maryland that meet at least one of the CCPA/CPRA-aligned thresholds listed above. Non-profits and government entities are excluded.

**Scope: "Reasonably Likely to Be Accessed by Children."** The core trigger is whether the entity offers an "online product" — broadly defined — that is "reasonably likely to be accessed by children" under 18. This standard does not require that the product be formally marketed to children; a general-audience social media platform, gaming service, or e-commerce application with a substantial youth user base can be covered.

**Duty to Act in Children's Best Interests.** The law's centerpiece obligation is a duty to act in the "best interests of children" when designing, developing, and operating covered online products. Maryland's legislature added a statutory definition of this duty — focused on minimizing harm, protecting privacy, and avoiding practices that exploit children's developmental vulnerabilities — specifically to address the constitutional deficiencies that led to California's AADC being enjoined.

**Data Protection Impact Assessments (DPIAs).** Every covered entity must [complete a DPIA by April 1, 2026](https://www.wsgr.com/en/insights/maryland-age-appropriate-design-code-effective-october-1-2024.html) for each online product reasonably likely to be accessed by children. The DPIA must: (1) identify the product's purpose; (2) identify how the product uses children's data; (3) determine whether the product is designed consistently with children's best interests; and (4) describe compliance steps taken and planned. The April 1, 2026 deadline has now passed for covered entities.

**Prohibited Practices.** The Kids Code prohibits covered entities from:
- Selling or sharing children's personal data;
- Collecting sensitive personal information from children (including ethnicity, religion, health, sexual orientation, and precise location) unless strictly necessary to provide the requested product or service;
- Using dark patterns to collect more data than necessary, circumvent privacy protections, or take actions not in children's best interests;
- Profiling children by default, unless appropriate safeguards are in place, profiling is necessary for the requested service, or there is affirmative reason to believe profiling serves children's best interests.

**Privacy by Default and Plain Language.** Covered entities must configure default privacy settings to the highest protective level. Privacy notices, terms of service, and policies must be written in plain, child-accessible language.

**Parental Monitoring Rights.** Parents and guardians may monitor their child's online activity without triggering an obvious signal to the child. No person other than a parent or guardian may monitor a child's online activity without notifying both the child and parent/guardian.

### UDAP Penalty Structure: A Compounding Risk

A critically important feature of both statutes that [Troutman Pepper specifically highlighted](https://www.troutman.com/insights/maryland-enacts-age-appropriate-design-code/) is their express incorporation of Maryland's Unfair, Deceptive, or Abusive Acts or Practices (UDAP) law. This creates a two-tier penalty regime:

**Kids Code-specific penalties:**
- Up to $2,500 per affected child for each negligent violation;
- Up to $7,500 per affected child for each intentional violation.

**UDAP civil penalties (also applicable to Kids Code violations):**
- Up to $10,000 for each violation;
- Up to $25,000 for each repeated violation.

For a platform serving tens of thousands of Maryland children, aggregate Kids Code-specific penalties alone can reach catastrophic levels. The additional UDAP tier means that a single Kids Code violation simultaneously exposes a company to both the per-child and per-violation penalty tracks. The Maryland Attorney General, which has exclusive enforcement authority, has discretion over which penalty framework to pursue in a given case.

MODPA carries the same UDAP penalty structure: $10,000 per initial violation and $25,000 per subsequent violation, enforced by the AG.

### Overlapping Requirements Between MODPA and the Kids Code

[Troutman Pepper's post-enactment analysis](https://www.troutman.com/insights/maryland-enacts-age-appropriate-design-code/) identified four primary areas of overlap that create layered compliance obligations for entities subject to both laws:

1. **DPIA Requirements.** Both statutes require data protection impact assessments, though with distinct scope. MODPA requires DPIAs for each processing activity presenting a heightened risk of harm to consumers generally, including for targeted advertising, data sales, profiling, sensitive data processing, and algorithm use. The Kids Code requires DPIAs specifically covering online products likely to be accessed by children. A product that involves sensitive data processing and is accessible to children requires a DPIA under both laws, and the two DPIAs must address different (though overlapping) statutory elements.

2. **Data Minimization.** MODPA imposes a "reasonably necessary and proportionate" minimization standard for general data (rising to "strictly necessary" for sensitive data). The Kids Code prohibits collecting sensitive data from children unless "strictly necessary" to provide the requested service. Both minimization requirements apply simultaneously to child users' sensitive data, creating redundant but additive compliance obligations.

3. **Minors and Targeted Advertising/Data Sales.** MODPA prohibits processing personal data of consumers "known or should have reasonably known" to be under 18 for targeted advertising or data sales. The Kids Code independently prohibits selling or sharing children's personal data for covered entities. A platform cannot simply satisfy one prohibition and consider itself compliant with the other; both apply and each imposes its own enforcement mechanism and penalty structure.

4. **Sensitive Data.** MODPA restricts sensitive data to what is "strictly necessary" for consumer-requested services and bans sensitive data sales outright. The Kids Code mirrors this with a prohibition on collecting sensitive personal information from children (including ethnicity, religion, health, sexual orientation, and precise location) unless strictly necessary for the requested service. The Children's data categories under both laws largely overlap, meaning a single misstep with a child's health or location data triggers violations of both statutes simultaneously.

### Enforcement Timeline

- **October 1, 2024:** Maryland Kids Code effective date.
- **April 1, 2026:** Kids Code DPIA deadline; MODPA enforcement commencement.
- **April 1, 2027:** Discretionary cure period under MODPA expires. After this date, the AG may proceed directly to civil penalties for MODPA violations without offering a 60-day opportunity to cure.

The Kids Code provides a 90-day opportunity to cure violations upon receiving notice from the Division of Consumer Protection. A covered entity that has completed a DPIA, cures the specified violation within 90 days, and takes adequate preventive steps may receive safe harbor from civil penalties.

### Constitutional Challenge: NetChoice v. Brown [MEDIUM confidence]

On February 3, 2025, NetChoice — an industry association whose members include Meta, X (formerly Twitter), and Google — [filed a constitutional challenge to the Maryland Kids Code](https://marylandmatters.org/2025/02/03/social-media-companies-head-to-court-claim-maryland-kids-code-is-unconstitutional/) in the U.S. District Court for the District of Maryland (Case No. RDB-25-0322).

On November 24, 2025, Judge Richard D. Bennett [denied the State's motion to dismiss](https://www2.mdd.uscourts.gov/Opinions/Opinions/Memorandum%20Opinion_NetChoice%20v.%20Brown%20(RDB-25-0322).pdf). The court found that NetChoice had adequately alleged:

- **First Amendment compelled speech claims:** The DPIA requirement compels platforms to produce opinionated assessments about content suitability for children, which the court characterized as "compelled speech" that "deputize[s] covered entities to act as censors for the State."
- **Burden on editorial functions:** The "best interests" and "reasonably likely to be accessed by children" standards require platforms to evaluate and potentially alter the content they moderate, burdening protected editorial discretion.
- **Unconstitutional vagueness:** Both the "best interests of children" standard and the "reasonably likely to be accessed by children" standard incorporate undefined, subjective criteria that fail to provide fair notice and risk arbitrary enforcement.
- **Federal preemption claims:** NetChoice also adequately alleged that the Kids Code is preempted by federal law, though the court did not rule on the merits of that claim at this stage.

As of the date of this report, the Maryland Kids Code [remains in effect](https://thedailyrecord.com/2025/12/01/maryland-kids-code-lawsuit-netchoice-first-amendment/) as the case proceeds to discovery. No preliminary injunction has been granted, distinguishing the current posture from the California AADC litigation where a preliminary injunction was issued before the law took effect. Perkins Coie [observed](https://perkinscoie.com/insights/blog/netchoice-challenge-maryland-kids-code-moves-forward-motion-to-dismiss-denied) that the court's analysis tracks the evolving First Amendment framework applied to platform regulation, and that the litigation will likely turn on whether the Kids Code survives strict scrutiny.

## Impact Assessment [MEDIUM confidence]

### Industries Most Affected

The combination of MODPA and the Kids Code creates a compounded risk profile for the following sectors:

- **Social media and video-sharing platforms:** General-audience platforms face the "reasonably likely to be accessed by children" standard regardless of their formal age policies. The DPIA requirement applied to these platforms even before MODPA enforcement commenced.
- **Digital advertising and adtech:** MODPA's prohibition on processing minors' data for targeted advertising, combined with the Kids Code's profiling restrictions, effectively eliminates behavioral advertising to identifiable or reasonably identifiable Maryland child users.
- **Gaming and entertainment applications:** Products attractive to minors that also collect sensitive data (location, behavioral profiling) face maximum exposure under both laws' sensitive data provisions.
- **Health and wellness applications:** Given MODPA's outright ban on sensitive data sales (which includes health conditions) and the Kids Code's prohibition on collecting health-related sensitive data from children, health-tech platforms face categorical constraints on data monetization involving Maryland residents, particularly those under 18.
- **Data brokers:** The combination of MODPA's sensitive data sale ban, the constructive-knowledge minors standard under both laws, and the Kids Code's explicit prohibition on selling children's personal data severely restricts brokering of data touching Maryland children.

### Compliance Cost Dimension

The DPIA obligation under the Kids Code is not a one-time exercise. Because MODPA independently requires DPIAs for high-risk processing activities on an ongoing basis, organizations subject to both laws face recurring compliance overhead — separate DPIAs serving different statutory frameworks but frequently requiring overlapping factual analysis. For large platforms operating hundreds of distinct features, this burden is substantial.

### Constitutional Uncertainty

The denial of the motion to dismiss in November 2025 does not resolve the Kids Code's constitutionality. The case proceeds to merits adjudication, and a preliminary injunction motion is possible. Covered entities should evaluate their DPIA completion and ongoing compliance against the backdrop of this uncertainty — completing DPIAs now provides safe harbor protection under the Kids Code if the law ultimately survives constitutional challenge, while failure to complete DPIAs by the April 1, 2026 deadline eliminates access to that safe harbor regardless of litigation outcome.

## Action Items

- **Confirm coverage status under both laws independently.** MODPA and the Kids Code use different applicability thresholds. An entity covered by one is not necessarily covered by the other. Perform separate threshold analyses for each law.
- **Treat DPIA obligations as live regardless of NetChoice litigation.** The April 1, 2026 Kids Code DPIA deadline has passed. Covered entities that have not completed DPIAs are currently exposed; completing them now still provides access to the statutory safe harbor and limits penalty exposure.
- **Map the four overlap areas for dual-exposure risk.** For DPIA requirements, data minimization, minors' data restrictions, and sensitive data handling, identify all products, features, and processing activities where both MODPA and the Kids Code apply simultaneously. Each overlap area presents double enforcement and double penalty exposure.
- **Audit UDAP exposure.** Because both statutes incorporate Maryland's UDAP law, a compliance failure can generate both per-child fines (Kids Code) and per-violation UDAP civil penalties simultaneously. Legal risk quantification must account for both penalty tracks.
- **Cease default profiling of users on platforms accessible to Maryland children.** Under the Kids Code, profiling is prohibited by default unless specific conditions are met (appropriate safeguards, necessity, or affirmative best-interests determination). Review algorithm-driven recommendation and content personalization features.
- **Configure privacy-by-default settings** on all online products reasonably likely to be accessed by children. Review and simplify privacy policies and terms of service for plain-language compliance.
- **Monitor NetChoice v. Brown.** A preliminary injunction ruling — if one is sought — would be the most significant near-term development affecting Kids Code compliance obligations. An injunction would halt enforcement pending final resolution; its absence continues the current posture of a law in force under constitutional cloud.
- **Plan for the MODPA cure period sunset on April 1, 2027.** The AG's discretionary 60-day cure offer under MODPA expires on that date. Build toward full MODPA compliance before then.
