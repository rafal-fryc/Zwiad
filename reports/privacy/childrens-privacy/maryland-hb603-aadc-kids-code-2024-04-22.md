# Maryland Age-Appropriate Design Code (HB 603) and the MODPA Package: Maryland Disrupts the State Privacy Law Patchwork

**Jurisdiction:** Maryland | **Category:** Privacy | **Date:** 2024-04-22

## Executive Summary [HIGH confidence]

In April 2024, Maryland enacted two companion privacy laws in rapid succession — the Maryland Online Data Privacy Act (MODPA, SB 541, signed May 9, 2024) and the Maryland Age-Appropriate Design Code Act (Maryland AADC / "Kids Code," HB 603, signed May 9, 2024) — together creating one of the most comprehensive and stringent state-level privacy regimes in the United States. The dual enactment is significant not only for its individual provisions but because it signals a departure from the predominant Virginia Consumer Data Protection Act (VCDPA) model that has dominated state privacy legislation since 2021. MODPA, which took effect October 1, 2025 with AG enforcement beginning April 1, 2026, is widely regarded as the nation's most restrictive comprehensive consumer privacy law. The Maryland Kids Code, which took effect October 1, 2024 and required DPIAs by April 1, 2026, imposes design-based obligations for online products likely to be accessed by children under 18. Together these laws demand separate compliance workstreams from any business that touches Maryland residents' data or operates digital products accessed by Maryland children.

## Background [HIGH confidence]

By April 2024, approximately 18 states had enacted or were actively advancing comprehensive consumer data privacy laws. The dominant legislative template — drawn from Virginia's 2021 VCDPA — emphasized consumer rights (access, correction, deletion, portability, opt-out) but left data collection practices largely intact, permitting collection for broadly stated "business purposes." Maryland's General Assembly took a different course with both bills.

[Senate Bill 541 (MODPA)](https://mgaleg.maryland.gov/mgawebsite/Legislation/Details/sb0541?ys=2024RS) passed both chambers and was signed by Governor Wes Moore on May 9, 2024. On the same day, Governor Moore signed [House Bill 603 (Maryland AADC)](https://mgaleg.maryland.gov/mgawebsite/Legislation/Details/HB0603?ys=2024rs), making Maryland the second U.S. state to enact an Age-Appropriate Design Code modeled on the United Kingdom's Children's Code — the first being California, whose similar AADC was [preliminarily enjoined on First Amendment grounds in September 2023](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-information-security-law/new-lawsuit-challenges-marylands-age-appropriate-design-code-act) before it took effect. Maryland's legislature worked with constitutional experts to amend HB 603 with a clearer definition of "best interests of the child" and other modifications designed to address those constitutional vulnerabilities.

As [ZwillGen PLLC observed](https://www.zwillgen.com/privacy/maryland-disrupts-state-privacy-law-patchwork/) when the bills passed their respective chambers on April 6 and 8, 2024, Maryland "disrupts the state privacy law patchwork" precisely because neither law tracks the Virginia model. Compliance programs built around VCDPA-derived statutes must be substantially revised or supplemented to address Maryland's requirements.

## Detailed Analysis [HIGH confidence]

### Maryland Online Data Privacy Act (MODPA / SB 541): Key Distinguishing Features

MODPA's architecture represents a structural departure from the majority of U.S. state privacy laws. The following provisions are the most significant points of divergence.

**Applicability Thresholds.** MODPA applies to persons conducting business in Maryland or targeting Maryland residents that, during the preceding calendar year, either (1) controlled or processed personal data of at least 35,000 consumers (excluding data processed solely for payment transactions), or (2) controlled or processed personal data of at least 10,000 consumers and derived more than 20% of gross revenue from personal data sales. The [35,000-consumer floor is proportionally lower](https://www.blankrome.com/publications/maryland-passes-unique-and-operationally-challenging-privacy-law) than thresholds in Colorado (approximately 100,000 consumers) and Oregon (100,000 consumers), meaning MODPA reaches a broader range of mid-size businesses. California's CCPA threshold, by comparison, is 100,000 consumers or $25 million gross revenue, or deriving 50% revenue from data sales.

**Data Minimization.** MODPA's data minimization standard is the most restrictive enacted by any U.S. state. Controllers must limit collection to what is "reasonably necessary and proportionate to provide or maintain a specific product or service requested by the consumer." For sensitive data, the standard rises to ["strictly necessary"](https://mslawgroup.com/marylands-modpa-a-new-standard-for-data-privacy-and-data-minimization/) to provide the requested product or service. Unlike CCPA/CPRA — which permits collection for purposes that are "reasonably necessary and proportionate" to disclosed purposes — MODPA ties permissible collection to a consumer-initiated request, not to the business's own purposes. The [California Lawyers Association](https://calawyers.org/privacy-law/differing-data-minimization-standards-comparing-californias-ccpa-and-marylands-modpa/) has noted that this distinction means MODPA forbids collection practices that CCPA/CPRA expressly permits.

**Sensitive Data: Outright Sale Prohibition.** Most state privacy laws require opt-in consent before processing sensitive data. MODPA goes further: it imposes an [absolute prohibition on the sale of sensitive data](https://www.datagrail.io/blog/data-privacy/why-marylands-new-privacy-law-could-be-the-strictest-in-the-us/) under any circumstances, including where affirmative consent has been obtained. Sensitive data categories include racial or ethnic origin, national origin, religious beliefs, health conditions or diagnoses, sex life information, sexual orientation, status as transgender or nonbinary, citizenship or immigration status, genetic or biometric data, data of known children under 13, and precise geolocation data. CCPA/CPRA, by contrast, permits sensitive data processing with an opt-out right — it does not impose a blanket sale ban.

**Children and Minors.** MODPA prohibits the processing of personal data of consumers known or reasonably believed to be under 18 for targeted advertising or data sales. The "should have reasonably known" standard imposes constructive knowledge requirements that go beyond the actual-knowledge standard in COPPA and most other state privacy laws. Under [MODPA's constructive knowledge framework](https://www.corporatecomplianceinsights.com/what-you-need-know-maryland-data-privacy-law/), the age of the user, contextual signals, and contextual knowledge of the platform's likely user base can all be relevant.

**Universal Opt-Out Mechanisms.** MODPA [requires controllers to honor Universal Opt-Out Mechanisms (UOOMs)](https://www.koleyjessen.com/insights/publications/maryland-online-data-privacy-act), such as Global Privacy Control browser signals. This aligns Maryland with approximately 11 other states that mandate UOOM recognition.

**Exemptions: Narrow Nonprofit Carve-Out.** Unlike most state privacy laws, [MODPA does not broadly exempt nonprofits](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-information-security-law/maryland-legislature-passes-state-privacy-bill-with-robust-requirements-and-broad-threshold-for-application). Only nonprofits that process or share data solely to assist law enforcement investigating insurance-related criminal acts, or first responders at catastrophic events, are exempt.

**Enforcement.** MODPA is enforced exclusively by the Maryland Attorney General. Violations constitute an "unfair, abusive, or deceptive trade practice" under the Maryland Consumer Protection Act (MCPA). Through April 1, 2027, the AG may at its discretion issue a 60-day notice-to-cure before proceeding. After April 1, 2027, this discretionary cure period expires entirely. Penalties: up to $10,000 per initial violation and $25,000 per subsequent violation — among the highest in the country compared to Virginia's $7,500 cap.

**Effective Dates.** MODPA took effect October 1, 2025; compliance obligations and AG enforcement commenced April 1, 2026.

### Maryland Age-Appropriate Design Code Act (HB 603 / Maryland Kids Code)

The Maryland Kids Code is a design-standards law that operates in parallel with — and in some respects goes beyond — MODPA's minors provisions.

**Covered Entities.** HB 603 applies to for-profit entities doing business in Maryland that meet at least one of three thresholds: (1) annual gross revenues of at least $25 million; (2) buying, receiving, selling, or sharing the personal data of 50,000 or more consumers, households, or devices; or (3) deriving at least 50% of annual revenue from the sale of consumer personal data. These [thresholds mirror the CCPA/CPRA applicability framework](https://www.privo.com/blog/what-is-the-maryland-kids-code), which is notably different from MODPA's lower thresholds, meaning some entities subject to MODPA will fall below the Kids Code threshold and vice versa.

**"Reasonably Likely to Be Accessed by Children."** The duty to act in children's best interests applies when the covered entity provides an online product that is "reasonably likely to be accessed by children" under 18. This standard requires an assessment of the actual or likely user base of the product — not merely whether the product is formally directed at children.

**Data Protection Impact Assessments (DPIAs).** Every covered entity offering an online product reasonably likely to be accessed by children must [prepare a DPIA by April 1, 2026](https://www.wsgr.com/en/insights/maryland-age-appropriate-design-code-effective-october-1-2024.html). The DPIA must: (a) identify the purpose of the online product; (b) identify how the product uses children's data; (c) determine whether the product is designed consistently with the best interests of children; and (d) describe the steps taken and to be taken to comply with the best-interests duty. DPIAs must cover both new and existing products, services, and features.

**Best Interests of Children Standard.** The law requires covered entities to act in the "best interests of children" when designing and operating covered products. Maryland amended the bill — specifically in response to the California AADC's First Amendment problems — to include a [clear, statutory definition of "best interests of the child"](https://fpf.org/blog/new-age-appropriate-design-code-framework-takes-hold-in-maryland/), which focuses on minimizing harm, protecting privacy, and avoiding practices that exploit children's psychology or development. The Future of Privacy Forum noted that Maryland's definitional approach is a meaningful constitutional improvement over California's version.

**Prohibited Practices.** Covered entities are prohibited from:
- Selling or sharing children's personal data (separate from MODPA's prohibition, and applicable to the Kids Code's covered entity threshold);
- Collecting sensitive personal information from children — including data on ethnicity, religion, health, sexual orientation, and precise location — unless strictly necessary to provide the requested product or service;
- [Using dark patterns](https://www.mintz.com/insights-center/viewpoints/2826/2024-05-16-maryland-says-dont-mess-kids) to collect more data than necessary, circumvent privacy protections, or take actions the entity knows are not in children's best interests;
- Profiling children by default unless: (a) the entity has appropriate safeguards, (b) profiling is necessary for the requested service, or (c) there is reason to believe profiling is in children's best interests.

**Parental Monitoring.** The law grants parents and guardians the ability to monitor their child's online activity without providing an obvious signal to the child. Entities may not allow a person other than a parent or guardian to monitor a child's online activity without notifying both the child and the parent/guardian.

**Privacy by Default.** Covered entities must configure default privacy settings to provide a high level of privacy. Privacy notices, policies, and terms of service must be written in child-friendly, plain language.

**Enforcement and Penalties.** The Maryland Division of Consumer Protection in the Office of the Attorney General has exclusive enforcement authority. Covered entities receive a 90-day opportunity to cure violations upon receiving a notice from the Division. Companies that have completed a DPIA, cure the specified violation within 90 days, and take adequate preventive steps may receive safe harbor from civil penalties. Penalties: up to $2,500 per child for each negligent violation and $7,500 per child for each intentional violation. Given that covered platforms can serve hundreds of thousands of child users in Maryland, aggregate penalties can reach substantial figures.

**Effective Date and DPIA Deadline.** The Maryland Kids Code took effect October 1, 2024. DPIA completion is required by April 1, 2026.

**Constitutional Challenge.** In February 2025, NetChoice — an industry group whose members include Meta, X, and Google — filed a lawsuit challenging the Maryland Kids Code as unconstitutional. The complaint alleges that the DPIA requirements [compel speech in violation of the First Amendment](https://marylandmatters.org/2025/02/03/social-media-companies-head-to-court-claim-maryland-kids-code-is-unconstitutional/) and that the law's restrictions on data collection used in delivering protected speech burden expression. As of the date of this report, the litigation is pending. The constitutional questions are similar to those that led to the preliminary injunction against California's AADC, though Maryland's legislature made targeted modifications intended to reduce First Amendment exposure.

## Impact Assessment [MEDIUM confidence]

### Disrupting the State Privacy Patchwork

The phrase "disrupts the patchwork" in ZwillGen's analysis captures a specific phenomenon: the majority of post-2021 state privacy laws are variations on the Virginia model, allowing sophisticated compliance programs to manage multi-state obligations with a single set of core controls and minor jurisdictional adjustments. Maryland breaks that pattern at the structural level, not just the marginal provisions level.

Three specific disruptions stand out:

1. **Data Minimization Architecture.** MODPA's consumer-request-anchored minimization standard is incompatible with VCDPA-model "business purpose" collection practices. Businesses that built their programs around the Virginia/Colorado/Connecticut framework must redesign collection justifications from scratch for Maryland.

2. **Absolute Sensitive Data Sale Ban.** No other U.S. state privacy law prohibits the sale of sensitive data regardless of consent. Businesses that rely on consent-based monetization of sensitive data — health tech, adtech, data brokers — face a categorical compliance requirement that cannot be addressed through consent management alone.

3. **Dual-Law Regime with Misaligned Thresholds.** Because MODPA and the Kids Code use different applicability thresholds, businesses must separately assess coverage under each law. A company covered by MODPA may not be covered by the Kids Code, and a company covered by the Kids Code may not be covered by MODPA — but may also be subject to both. This creates four possible compliance states for any given company, each requiring separate analysis.

### Comparison With Other Stringent State Laws

| Feature | Maryland MODPA | California CCPA/CPRA | Oregon OCPA |
|---|---|---|---|
| Consumer threshold | 35,000 | 100,000 or $25M revenue | 100,000 |
| Data minimization | Reasonably necessary to consumer request | Reasonably necessary to disclosed purposes | Reasonably necessary |
| Sensitive data sale | Absolute prohibition | Opt-out right | Opt-out/opt-in depending on category |
| Minors standard | "Should have known" under 18 | "Actual knowledge" under 16 | Actual knowledge |
| Nonprofit exemption | Narrow (insurance/first responder only) | Broad | Broad |
| Design code | Yes (Kids Code, HB 603) | Yes (AADC, enjoined) | No |
| Cure period | Discretionary, sunset April 1, 2027 | AG discretion | 30-day cure period |

### Affected Industries

- **Digital advertising and adtech:** MODPA's data minimization and sensitive data sale ban, combined with the Kids Code's prohibition on profiling children by default, fundamentally restrict behavioral advertising targeting Maryland users.
- **Health, wellness, and reproductive technology platforms:** MODPA's expanded sensitive data categories (including health conditions, sexual orientation, and transgender status) combined with the absolute sale ban require restructuring data monetization.
- **Social media and consumer apps:** The Kids Code's DPIA requirement and "reasonably likely to be accessed by children" standard applies broadly to general-audience platforms with substantial youth user bases — not just products marketed to children.
- **Data brokers:** The combination of the sensitive data sale ban and the constructive-knowledge minor standard severely restricts the brokerage of Maryland residents' sensitive and minors' data.

## Action Items

- **Separately assess MODPA and Kids Code applicability.** The two laws use different thresholds ($25M/50,000 vs. 35,000/10,000 consumers) and each requires independent analysis. Do not assume that coverage under one implies coverage under the other.
- **Conduct a data minimization gap analysis specific to MODPA.** Existing VCDPA-model compliance programs rely on "business purpose" justifications that MODPA does not permit. Audit all data collection practices and retether justifications to specific consumer-initiated product requests.
- **Immediately cease sales of sensitive data involving Maryland residents**, regardless of consent. Audit data broker and third-party sharing arrangements. Note that MODPA's sensitive data categories include national origin and transgender/nonbinary status, which are not always included in other states' definitions.
- **Complete Data Protection Impact Assessments for the Kids Code by April 1, 2026.** For each online product reasonably likely to be accessed by Maryland children, prepare a DPIA addressing the four statutory elements (purpose, data use, best-interests consistency, compliance steps).
- **Audit product design for dark patterns.** Review UX flows, data collection prompts, and default settings for practices that exploit child users or collect more data than the product requires.
- **Set privacy defaults to the highest protective level** for products accessible by children. Review and rewrite privacy policies and terms of service for plain-language compliance.
- **Implement UOOM (Global Privacy Control) recognition** if not already in place to comply with MODPA's universal opt-out mandate.
- **Monitor the NetChoice constitutional litigation** against the Maryland Kids Code. An injunction would relieve the DPIA obligation pending litigation resolution; a loss for NetChoice would confirm the law's enforcement posture. Either outcome affects compliance planning.
- **Plan for MODPA's cure period sunset on April 1, 2027.** Until that date the AG has discretion to offer a 60-day cure opportunity. After April 1, 2027, enforcement can proceed directly to civil penalties without a cure period.
