# House Energy & Commerce Committee Strikes Bipartisan Deal on KIDS Act (H.R. 7757), Consolidating KOSA, COPPA 2.0, and 13+ Child Safety Bills

**Jurisdiction:** Federal | **Category:** Privacy — Children's Privacy | **Date:** June 22–24, 2026

## Summary [HIGH confidence]

On June 22–24, 2026, House Energy & Commerce Committee Chairman Brett Guthrie (R-KY) and Ranking Member Frank Pallone (D-NJ) [announced a bipartisan compromise](https://energycommerce.house.gov/posts/chairman-guthrie-and-ranking-member-pallone-announce-agreement-on-bipartisan-kids-safety-package) on an updated version of H.R. 7757, the Kids Internet and Digital Safety (KIDS) Act. The revised package consolidates the Kids Online Safety Act (KOSA), COPPA 2.0, and more than a dozen additional child online safety bills into a single omnibus measure. The deal's central concession was the elimination of KOSA's signature "duty of care" provision — the legally enforceable obligation that would have required platforms to take reasonable steps to prevent documented harms to minors — along with the addition of explicit statutory text stating the bill cannot be construed to impose such a duty. The legislation advanced through committee but has not been scheduled for a floor vote, and key Senate sponsors have declared the House version unacceptable, clouding its prospects for enactment.

## Key Facts [HIGH confidence]

- **Bill number and introduction:** H.R. 7757 was introduced on March 3, 2026 by Chairman Guthrie, initially referred to the Committees on Energy and Commerce and Judiciary, and passed out of committee on March 5, 2026 on a party-line 28–24 roll-call vote, with all Republicans voting in favor and all Democrats opposed, according to [Congress.gov](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-bill/7757/text) and [Roll Call](https://rollcall.com/2026/03/06/kids-online-safety-bills-move-forward-from-senate-house-panel/).
- **Bipartisan deal date:** Guthrie and Pallone [released revised text on June 22, 2026](https://democrats-energycommerce.house.gov/media/press-releases/chairman-guthrie-and-ranking-member-pallone-announce-agreement-bipartisan-kids), with the committee formally announcing the compromise package on June 24, 2026.
- **Duty of care eliminated:** The revised bill drops KOSA's legally enforceable duty of care and, per [The Hill](https://thehill.com/policy/technology/5934266-bipartisan-deal-kids-online-protection/), adds explicit statutory language providing that the Act shall not be construed to impose one — a direct rebuke to the Senate-passed version that had centered on platform accountability.
- **Replacement accountability mechanism:** In lieu of a duty of care, platforms must submit to annual third-party audits of safety practices and maintain and enforce "reasonable policies" addressing harms such as violence, sexual abuse, and self-harm, per reporting by [TechTimes](https://www.techtimes.com/articles/318896/20260623/house-kids-act-deal-drops-kosa-duty-care-adds-age-verification-all-users.htm) and [Roll Call](https://rollcall.com/2026/06/22/bipartisan-changes-expected-to-kids-internet-safety-bill/).
- **COPPA 2.0 integration:** The package updates the Children's Online Privacy Protection Act's 1998 framework for the modern era, tightening rules on the collection and use of children's personal data, per [Congress.gov text](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-bill/7757/text).
- **Federal data broker registry:** The deal adds a new federal data broker registration requirement, creating transparency obligations for companies that trade in personal information — an expansion well beyond child safety into the broader commercial data ecosystem, per [IAPP reporting](https://iapp.org/news/a/bipartisan-compromise-reached-on-kids-act-containing-kosa-coppa-2-0).
- **Age verification mandate:** Websites hosting primarily adult content must deploy age-verification technology to block minors; the bill prohibits compelling platforms to collect government-issued identification but requires commercially available "effective" age-verification methods, per [TechTimes](https://www.techtimes.com/articles/318896/20260623/house-kids-act-deal-drops-kosa-duty-care-adds-age-verification-all-users.htm).
- **Default privacy settings for minors:** Platforms must enable maximum privacy settings by default for users under 17, restrict unsolicited direct messages, prohibit ephemeral/disappearing messages for all minors, and ban direct messaging entirely for children under 13, per [Congress.gov bill summary](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-bill/7757/text).
- **State preemption:** The revised bill preempts only conflicting state laws while permitting states to regulate beyond the federal floor on topics the bill covers — a concession to Democratic members who opposed broader preemption language that would have wiped out existing state children's privacy statutes, per [Roll Call](https://rollcall.com/2026/06/22/bipartisan-changes-expected-to-kids-internet-safety-bill/).
- **Floor vote status:** The KIDS Act has not been scheduled for a full House floor vote as of June 28, 2026. [The Hill reported](https://thehill.com/policy/technology/5942670-kids-act-opposed-by-safety-groups/) that the bill could move to the House floor under the suspension-of-the-rules process (which requires a two-thirds majority) as early as the week of June 29, 2026.

## Senate Opposition [HIGH confidence]

The deal faces its most significant structural obstacle in the Senate, where the authors of the original KOSA have rejected the House version.

- **Blumenthal and Blackburn:** Senators Richard Blumenthal (D-CT) and Marsha Blackburn (R-TN), the bipartisan authors of KOSA, declared the House version unacceptable. Senator Blumenthal called the package "dead on arrival" and characterized it as "a blank check to Mark Zuckerberg to exploit children," while Senator Blackburn stated that without a duty of care, "tech companies will maintain the status quo of putting profit before the safety of our children," per [The Hill](https://thehill.com/policy/technology/5938935-house-breakthrough-on-kids-online-safety-faces-long-odds-in-senate/).
- **Senate Commerce Committee opposition:** Senators Maria Cantwell and Richard Blumenthal, along with parents' groups, formally raised the alarm about what they characterized as a "weak" House version heading toward a floor vote, per the [Senate Commerce Committee press release](https://www.commerce.senate.gov/press/dem/release/cantwell-blumenthal-parents-raise-the-alarm-about-weak-kids-act-heading-to-house-vote-next-week/).
- **Alternative White House package:** Senator Blackburn is simultaneously negotiating a separate legislative vehicle with the White House that would include a full duty of care standard — suggesting the Senate sponsors are pursuing an alternative path rather than accepting the House compromise, per [The Hill](https://thehill.com/policy/technology/5938935-house-breakthrough-on-kids-online-safety-faces-long-odds-in-senate/).
- **Democratic senators broadly opposed:** Additional Democratic senators joined in opposing the House version as too weak, with [Broadband Breakfast reporting](https://broadbandbreakfast.com/democratic-senators-oppose-weak-house-kids-online-safety-act/) that multiple Senate Democrats characterized the legislation as insufficient to hold platforms accountable.

## Stakeholder Opposition [HIGH confidence]

The KIDS Act faces pressure from both critics who believe it goes too far and advocates who believe it does not go far enough.

- **Attorneys general coalition:** On May 26, 2026, a bipartisan coalition of 44 state attorneys general sent a letter to congressional leadership opposing H.R. 7757, citing concerns that the bill would limit states' ability to address online harms to children by broadly preempting state enforcement authority and eliminating the duty of care, per the [National Association of Attorneys General press release](https://www.naag.org/press-releases/bipartisan-coalition-opposes-kids-act-h-r-7757-calls-for-stronger-online-protections/). States represented in the coalition include New York, Washington, Arizona, and Minnesota, among others.
- **Nearly 100 tech safety groups:** A coalition of approximately 100 child safety and digital rights groups — including the National Center on Sexual Exploitation (NCOSE), Design It for Us, Tech Oversight Project, and Encode — urged House leadership to reject the bill, arguing it "lets Big Tech off the hook" by removing the duty of care, per [The Hill](https://thehill.com/policy/technology/5942670-kids-act-opposed-by-safety-groups/).
- **Civil liberties concerns:** The ACLU and EFF opposed the bill's age verification mandate on First Amendment and privacy grounds, warning that the provision could lead to censorship of LGBTQ+ content and undermine user privacy by incentivizing mass collection of identification data, per [Roll Call](https://rollcall.com/2026/06/22/bipartisan-changes-expected-to-kids-internet-safety-bill/) and [State of Surveillance](https://stateofsurveillance.org/news/kosa-kids-online-safety-act-2026-child-protection-censorship/).

## Background: Legislative History of KOSA [MEDIUM confidence]

The Kids Online Safety Act has been one of the most-debated child safety bills in recent congressional history. The Senate passed KOSA in an earlier Congress with overwhelming bipartisan support, but the bill stalled in the House amid disagreements over the duty of care standard and free speech concerns. The 119th Congress effort — H.R. 7757, introduced March 3, 2026 — represented a House-led attempt to advance child online safety legislation on different terms than the Senate bill. The House Energy & Commerce Committee's March 5, 2026 vote was strictly party-line: all 28 Republicans voted for the bill and all 24 Democrats voted against it, per [Roll Call](https://rollcall.com/2026/03/06/kids-online-safety-bills-move-forward-from-senate-house-panel/) and [The Hill](https://thehill.com/policy/technology/5770101-house-panel-advances-slate-of-kids-online-safety-bills-along-party-lines/). Democratic members specifically objected to the bill's broad state law preemption language — which they argued would undermine existing state children's privacy statutes — and to the absence of a duty of care standard, per [Axios](https://www.axios.com/2026/03/05/house-gop-advances-kids-online-safety-package) and [GovTrack.us](https://www.govtrack.us/congress/bills/119/hr7757). The significance of the June 2026 bipartisan deal is precisely that it converted a party-line committee outcome into a cross-aisle agreement: Ranking Member Pallone's participation in the revised package required concessions on both the state preemption scope (narrowed to only conflicting laws) and the explicit disavowal of a duty of care. The June 22, 2026 revised text was released by the House Energy & Commerce Committee's Democratic staff in a document entitled "[Suspend the Rules and Pass the Bill, H.R. 7757, With an Amendment](https://democrats-energycommerce.house.gov/sites/evo-subsites/democrats-energycommerce.house.gov/files/evo-media-document/updated-kids-act-6.22.26.pdf)," which reflects the procedural vehicle being contemplated for House floor passage.

## Action Items

- **Monitor House floor schedule:** The KIDS Act could be brought to the House floor under the suspension-of-the-rules process (requiring a two-thirds majority) as early as the week of June 29, 2026. Track the House schedule for any formal scheduling of H.R. 7757.
- **Assess federal data broker registry impact:** Companies that buy, sell, or broker personal data should begin assessing their exposure to a federal registration requirement if the data broker registry provisions advance into law. This would represent a new compliance obligation distinct from existing California and Virginia data broker registration regimes.
- **Evaluate age verification obligations:** If the age verification mandate becomes law, any website hosting content primarily intended for adults should assess its current age-gating practices against the "effective, commercially available" standard articulated in the bill, and plan for implementation without collecting government IDs.
- **Track Senate path:** Given Senate KOSA co-authors' opposition, watch for whether a conference process, further House-Senate negotiation, or the White House-backed alternative package emerges. The bill's path to enactment likely requires either Senate acquiescence to the House terms or a revised text that restores some form of duty of care.
- **Assess state law preemption scope:** For organizations operating under state children's privacy laws (e.g., California's CAADCA, Maryland's KOSA equivalent, Colorado's SB 26-051), evaluate how the bill's limited preemption language — preempting only conflicting state laws while allowing states to go further — would interact with your current compliance programs.
- **Assess audit readiness:** If the annual third-party safety audit requirement becomes law, platforms should begin assessing the operational infrastructure needed to conduct and publish safety audits addressing harms enumerated in the bill.
