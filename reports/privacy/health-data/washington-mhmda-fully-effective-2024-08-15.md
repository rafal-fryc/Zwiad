# Washington My Health My Data Act Now Fully Effective: Requirements, Scope, and Litigation Outlook

**Jurisdiction:** Washington | **Category:** Privacy | **Date:** 2024-08-15

## Executive Summary [HIGH confidence]

Washington's My Health My Data Act (MHMDA), codified at [RCW 19.373](https://app.leg.wa.gov/RCW/default.aspx?cite=19.373&full=true), is the first US state law specifically designed to protect consumer health data that falls outside the scope of HIPAA. Signed by Governor Jay Inslee on April 27, 2023, the law applied to large regulated entities beginning March 31, 2024, and reached full effect for small businesses on June 30, 2024. The MHMDA imposes consent, transparency, and data minimization obligations on any entity — regardless of size or nonprofit status — that collects consumer health data from Washington residents or targets products and services at them. Enforcement is channeled through the Washington Consumer Protection Act (CPA), which authorizes both [Washington Attorney General](https://www.atg.wa.gov/protecting-washingtonians-personal-health-data-and-privacy) action and a private right of action with treble damages. The law's broad definitions and significant litigation exposure make it one of the most consequential state health-data privacy statutes enacted to date.

## Background [HIGH confidence]

The MHMDA emerged from legislative concern that HIPAA — the dominant federal health privacy statute — leaves vast quantities of sensitive health-related data unprotected. HIPAA applies only to covered entities (healthcare providers, health plans, clearinghouses) and their business associates. The explosion of consumer health applications, fitness wearables, period-tracking apps, and location-data advertising has created an ecosystem of highly sensitive health information that flows entirely outside HIPAA's reach.

Washington state [signed HB 1155 into law on April 27, 2023](https://www.atg.wa.gov/protecting-washingtonians-personal-health-data-and-privacy), after the legislature passed it on April 17, 2023. The law was designed in part to protect reproductive health data following the Supreme Court's 2022 decision in *Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization*, which overturned *Roe v. Wade* and heightened concerns about law enforcement use of location and health data to investigate abortion-related activities.

The MHMDA is notable for several structural choices that distinguish it from general state privacy laws such as the Virginia Consumer Data Protection Act (VCDPA) model: it focuses exclusively on health-related data, imposes opt-in consent requirements rather than opt-out, extends to nonprofits (which most general privacy laws exempt), and includes a geofencing prohibition that became effective immediately on July 23, 2023. The [Future of Privacy Forum's policy brief](https://fpf.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/04/FPF-Legislation-Policy-Brief_-The-Washington-My-Health-My-Data-Act-Public-Version.pdf) characterized the law as occupying a "critical gap" in health data protection between HIPAA and general consumer privacy statutes.

## Detailed Analysis [HIGH confidence]

### Scope: Who Is Covered

The MHMDA applies to any "regulated entity" — a legal entity that (1) conducts business in Washington or targets products or services to Washington consumers, and (2) determines the purpose and means of collecting, processing, sharing, or selling consumer health data. The law also imposes obligations on "small businesses," a defined subset subject to the same substantive requirements but subject to a later compliance deadline.

Critically, the MHMDA contains **no revenue threshold or minimum data-subject count** for regulated entities. Unlike the general VCDPA model (which applies at 100,000+ consumers), any entity touching consumer health data of Washington residents can be covered. [Small businesses](https://www.stoel.com/insights/publications/faq-washington-states-my-health-my-data-act) are defined more narrowly as entities that process consumer health data of fewer than 100,000 consumers per calendar year, or collect data of fewer than 25,000 consumers while deriving less than 50% of revenues from such data.

Importantly, [nonprofit organizations are not exempt](https://www.bassberry.com/news/washington-my-health-my-data-act/), a departure from most other state privacy laws. Government entities and their vendors are exempt from the Act's requirements.

The law's extraterritorial reach is significant: because it applies to any entity that "targets" products or services at Washington consumers, businesses with no physical presence in Washington but whose apps or websites serve Washington residents may be covered.

### Scope: What Data Is Covered

"Consumer health data" is broadly defined as personal information linked or reasonably linkable to a consumer that identifies the consumer's past, present, or future physical or mental health status. [The statutory definition expressly enumerates 12 categories](https://app.leg.wa.gov/RCW/default.aspx?cite=19.373&full=true), including:

- Individual health conditions, treatments, diseases, or diagnoses
- Social, psychological, behavioral, and medical interventions
- Surgeries or other health-related procedures
- Use or purchase of prescribed medication
- Bodily functions, vital signs, symptoms, or measurements
- Diagnoses or diagnostic testing, treatment, or medication
- Gender-affirming care information
- Reproductive or sexual health information
- Biometric data
- Genetic data
- Precise geolocation data that could reasonably indicate an attempt to acquire or receive health services or supplies
- Any data derived or inferred from nonhealth information (including via algorithms or machine learning) that a regulated entity uses to associate a consumer with the foregoing categories

This last point — that data *derived or inferred* from non-health sources may constitute consumer health data — significantly expands the law's scope. Location data, browsing history, or purchase records that are processed to infer health conditions are covered even though the raw data is not inherently medical.

### Exemptions: Data-Level, Not Entity-Level

The MHMDA's exemptions operate at the **data level**, not the entity level. HIPAA-covered entities are not categorically exempt; rather, data that is already protected health information (PHI) under HIPAA is excluded from the MHMDA's scope. As [Goodwin Law explains](https://www.goodwinlaw.com/en/insights/publications/2024/03/alerts-technology-hltc-my-health-my-data-act-mhmda), a hospital that also operates a consumer wellness app would need to separately assess whether its app-derived data is covered by the MHMDA.

Other data-level exemptions cover information subject to GLBA, FCRA, and FERPA; deidentified data that meets specific standards; and data used in certain peer-reviewed public-interest research.

### Core Obligations

**1. Consumer Health Data Privacy Policy.** Every regulated entity must prominently publish a separate link to its consumer health data privacy policy on its homepage. The policy must be distinct from the entity's general privacy policy and may not contain extraneous information. [The K&L Gates compliance guide](https://www.klgates.com/The-Countdown-to-Complete-Your-Consumer-Health-Data-Privacy-Policy-Under-the-Washington-My-Health-My-Data-Act-3-19-2024) describes the required disclosures in detail, including the categories of data collected, purposes of collection, and a list of third parties with whom data is shared or sold.

**2. Opt-In Consent.** The MHMDA requires affirmative, specific, informed, opt-in consent before collecting or sharing consumer health data for purposes beyond what is necessary to provide the requested product or service. Consent cannot be inferred from:
- Acceptance of general terms of service
- Hovering over, muting, pausing, or closing content
- Agreements obtained through dark patterns or deceptive design

**3. Consumer Rights.** Under [RCW 19.373](https://app.leg.wa.gov/RCW/default.aspx?cite=19.373&full=true), consumers have the right to:
- Confirm whether their consumer health data is being collected, shared, or sold
- Access their consumer health data, including a list of all third parties with whom it has been shared
- Withdraw consent to collection and sharing
- Request deletion of their consumer health data from a regulated entity and its processors

**4. Data Sharing Restrictions.** Selling consumer health data requires a separate, explicit authorization from the consumer, with heightened requirements beyond baseline consent. [Morgan Lewis notes](https://www.morganlewis.com/pubs/2023/07/the-broad-reach-of-washington-states-my-health-my-data-act) that the law's prohibition on selling consumer health data without authorization applies broadly to any exchange of value, not just monetary transactions.

**5. Geofencing Prohibition.** Effective July 23, 2023 — before the rest of the law took effect — the MHMDA prohibits any person from using a geofence (defined as technology using GPS, cell tower, Wi-Fi, or other spatial data to create a virtual boundary of up to 2,000 feet around a health care services location) to:
- Identify or track consumers seeking health services
- Collect consumer health data from consumers
- Send notifications, messages, or advertisements related to consumer health data

This prohibition applies to *all* persons, not just regulated entities, making it one of the broadest technology restrictions in the statute.

### Effective Dates

| Provision | Effective Date | Applies To |
|---|---|---|
| Geofencing prohibition (Section 10) | July 23, 2023 | All persons |
| Sections 4–9 (core requirements) | March 31, 2024 | Regulated entities (non-small businesses) |
| Sections 4–9 (core requirements) | June 30, 2024 | Small businesses |

As of August 15, 2024, all provisions of the MHMDA are fully in effect for all covered entities.

## Impact Assessment [HIGH confidence]

### Who Is Affected

The MHMDA's reach extends well beyond traditional healthcare companies. Because "consumer health data" includes inferred data derived from location, purchase history, or browsing records, the following industries face significant compliance obligations:

- **Consumer apps and platforms** — fitness trackers, period-tracking apps, nutrition and wellness apps, mental health platforms
- **Retailers** — pharmacy chains, grocery chains with loyalty programs (if purchase data reveals medication use), pharmacies operating within general retailers
- **Advertising technology companies** — ad networks and SDKs that collect location or behavioral data that could be characterized as health-related (as alleged in *Maxwell v. Amazon*)
- **Data brokers** — any broker processing inferred health data from Washington residents
- **Nonprofits** — hospitals operating patient portals, disease advocacy organizations, research nonprofits — all explicitly covered

### Compliance Requirements

Affected organizations should have completed the following by June 30, 2024:

1. **Data mapping** — identify all consumer health data (including inferred data) processed by the organization
2. **Privacy policy** — publish a standalone consumer health data privacy policy with required disclosures on the homepage
3. **Consent management** — implement opt-in consent workflows for data collection and separate authorization workflows for data sales
4. **Consumer rights infrastructure** — build mechanisms to respond to access, deletion, and consent-withdrawal requests
5. **Vendor contracts** — update processor agreements to flow down MHMDA obligations and ensure downstream compliance
6. **Geofencing audit** — confirm no geofencing technology operates within 2,000 feet of healthcare facilities for covered purposes (effective July 23, 2023)

The Washington Attorney General published [updated FAQ guidance](https://cdp.cooley.com/washington-attorney-general-publishes-updated-faq-for-my-health-my-data-act/) prior to the March 31, 2024 effective date, which compliance teams should review.

### Enforcement and Litigation Outlook

**Statutory enforcement mechanism.** Any MHMDA violation is a *per se* violation of the [Washington Consumer Protection Act (RCW 19.86)](https://app.leg.wa.gov/RCW/default.aspx?cite=19.86). The CPA authorizes:
- **Attorney General enforcement** with civil penalties of up to $7,500 per violation, plus a $5,000 enhanced penalty for violations targeting protected demographic groups
- **Private right of action** with actual damages, reasonable attorney's fees, costs, and at the court's discretion treble damages up to $25,000 per violation

**No cure period.** Unlike some state privacy laws that provide a cure period before enforcement, the MHMDA does not contain a grace period for violations — once a violation is established, penalties attach.

**First class action: Maxwell v. Amazon.com, Inc.** The first class action under the MHMDA was filed on February 10, 2025, in the [U.S. District Court for the Western District of Washington (Case No. 2:25-cv-00261)](https://dockets.justia.com/docket/washington/wawdce/2:2025cv00261/344509). The plaintiff — a Washington state resident — alleged that Amazon.com and Amazon Advertising collected biometric and precise location data through Amazon's SDKs licensed to third-party mobile applications, including OfferUp and The Weather Channel. The complaint alleges that this location data was sufficient to identify users' attempts to seek health services or supplies. Seven claims are asserted: violations of the Federal Wiretap Act, the Stored Communications Act, the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act, the MHMDA, and the Washington CPA.

Multiple law firm analyses — including from [WilmerHale](https://www.wilmerhale.com/en/insights/blogs/wilmerhale-privacy-and-cybersecurity-law/20250220-first-lawsuit-filed-under-washingtons-my-health-my-data-act), [Orrick](https://www.orrick.com/en/Insights/2025/02/First-Lawsuit-Filed-Under-Washingtons-My-Health-My-Data-Act), and [Paul Hastings](https://www.paulhastings.com/insights/ph-privacy/biometrics-litigation-update-first-class-action-complaint-filed-under-washingtons-my-health-my-data-act-is-your-company-ready) — describe *Maxwell v. Amazon* as a bellwether that will set precedent for the scope of the MHMDA's application to advertising technology and SDK-based data collection. The case also raises novel questions about whether location data alone — without accompanying medical records — constitutes "consumer health data" under the statute.

**Insurance implications.** [Perkins Coie has noted](https://perkinscoie.com/insights/update/washington-states-new-my-health-my-data-act-will-likely-result-insurance-coverage) that the MHMDA's private right of action and CPA linkage will generate significant coverage disputes, as insurers and policyholders disagree about whether MHMDA-based claims fall within standard cyber liability or errors-and-omissions policy language.

## Action Items

- Verify that a standalone consumer health data privacy policy is published and linked prominently on the organization's homepage, separate from the general privacy policy.
- Conduct a data mapping exercise to identify all consumer health data processed, including data inferred or derived from non-health sources (e.g., location data, purchase history processed to infer health conditions).
- Audit all third-party SDK integrations (advertising, analytics, tracking) for potential MHMDA exposure — the *Maxwell v. Amazon* litigation signals that SDK-based data flows are a primary enforcement target.
- Confirm that opt-in consent workflows (not opt-out) are in place for collection and sharing of consumer health data, and that separate explicit authorization is obtained before any consumer health data sales.
- Review geofencing technology and policies to ensure no geofencing operates within 2,000 feet of healthcare facilities for identification, tracking, or advertising purposes.
- Update vendor and processor agreements to include MHMDA-specific data protection terms, flowing down the law's obligations.
- Monitor the *Maxwell v. Amazon* (Case No. 2:25-cv-00261) litigation for rulings on the definition of "consumer health data," the scope of SDK liability, and the standing requirements for private plaintiffs.
- Review cyber liability and E&O insurance policies for coverage of MHMDA-based claims; consult with coverage counsel if policy language is ambiguous.
