# California AB 2426: New Law Mandates Licensing Disclosures for Digital Goods Sales

**Jurisdiction:** California | **Category:** Privacy / Consumer Protection | **Date:** October 21, 2024

## Executive Summary [HIGH confidence]

California Governor Gavin Newsom signed AB 2426 on September 24, 2024, making California the [first state in the nation to require explicit licensing disclosures](https://www.morganlewis.com/pubs/2024/10/california-becomes-first-state-to-pass-law-targeting-advertising-of-digital-media-licenses) when companies sell digital goods under revocable licenses. Effective January 1, 2025, the law adds [Section 17500.6 to the California Business and Professions Code](https://law.justia.com/codes/california/code-bpc/division-7/part-3/chapter-1/article-1/section-17500-6/) and prohibits sellers from using "buy," "purchase," or similar ownership-conveying terms for digital goods unless they deliver a clear pre-transaction disclosure or obtain affirmative consumer acknowledgment. The law covers digital audiovisual works, audio works, books, code, applications, and games. Non-compliant sellers face misdemeanor exposure, civil penalties up to $2,500 per violation, and class action risk under California's Unfair Competition Law. Early enforcement has arrived via [class actions already filed against major retailers including GameStop](https://technologylaw.fkks.com/post/102mdrc/class-actions-under-californias-digital-goods-disclosure-law-reach-video-games), signaling that the private plaintiffs' bar is actively monitoring digital storefronts for non-compliance.

## Background [HIGH confidence]

The transaction-level confusion between "buying" and "licensing" digital goods has been a long-standing consumer protection concern, but it reached a flashpoint in 2023–2024 with two high-profile incidents that drove legislative action.

In December 2023, Sony's PlayStation Store announced that it would remove over 1,300 seasons of previously purchased Discovery Channel content from users' libraries, citing expiring licensing arrangements with Warner Bros. Discovery. Although Sony subsequently updated its licensing terms and reversed the removal, the episode drew widespread consumer attention to the risk of losing access to purchased digital content. Also in December 2023, Ubisoft delisted its online-only racing game "The Crew" from digital storefronts and shut down its servers on March 31, 2024, permanently revoking access for all players who had paid up to $59.99 — a purchase marketed using unambiguous ownership language. These episodes generated widespread media coverage and consumer complaints, [catching the attention of Assemblymember Jacqui Irwin of California's 42nd Assembly District](https://a42.asmdc.org/press-releases/20240925-governor-newsom-signs-ab-2426-law-increases-transparency-surrounding), who introduced AB 2426 in February 2024.

The bill progressed through the California Legislature with remarkable bipartisan consensus. The [Assembly passed AB 2426 on May 22, 2024 (72-0)](https://legiscan.com/CA/bill/AB2426/2023), the [Senate passed it on August 27, 2024 (33-4)](https://legiscan.com/CA/bill/AB2426/2023), and the Assembly concurred in Senate amendments on August 28, 2024 (76-0). Governor Newsom signed the bill on September 24, 2024, as [Chapter 513, Statutes of 2024](https://legiscan.com/CA/text/AB2426/id/3022416). It became effective January 1, 2025.

Prior to AB 2426, California's existing [False Advertising Law (Bus. & Prof. Code §§ 17500 et seq.)](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codesTOCSelected.xhtml?tocCode=BPC&tocTitle=+Business+and+Professions+Code+-+BPC) provided general prohibitions on misleading advertising, but did not specifically address the unique characteristics of digital licensing. No other state had enacted targeted legislation addressing digital goods terminology at point of sale before California acted.

## Detailed Analysis [HIGH confidence]

### Statutory Framework

AB 2426 adds **Section 17500.6** to the California Business and Professions Code, situated within the [False Advertising chapter (Division 7, Part 3, Chapter 1)](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/billNavClient.xhtml?bill_id=202320240AB2426). The provision establishes a targeted prohibition on the unqualified use of ownership-connoting language in the sale of digital goods subject to revocable access.

The key trigger condition is dual: (1) the transaction grants only a license to access rather than an unrestricted ownership interest in the digital good, AND (2) the seller retains the ability to revoke consumer access after payment. If both conditions are met, using terms such as "buy," "purchase," or any other language that a reasonable consumer would understand to confer unrestricted ownership violates the statute unless one of two compliance pathways is satisfied.

### Covered Digital Goods

The statute defines "digital goods" to include:

- **Digital audiovisual works**: motion pictures, musicals, and videos
- **Digital audio works**: songs, music, spoken-word recordings, ringtones, and similar sound recordings
- **Digital books**: any work offered in digital form that would be recognized as a book if sold in print form
- **Digital codes**: redeemable codes that provide access to any of the above categories
- **Digital applications or games**: any application or game accessed on a specialized gaming device, computer, mobile device, tablet, or display-screen device, including downloadable content (DLC) and add-ons

Coverage is broad and technology-neutral, capturing music stores, video platforms, ebook retailers, game storefronts, and mobile app markets equally. [Legal analysts at Linklaters note](https://techinsights.linklaters.com/post/102jpi5/californias-ab-2426-game-changer-for-video-game-and-digital-media-purchases) that the definitions extend to in-game purchases within existing games, including virtual currency, character skins, and expansion packs — potentially affecting the freemium model that dominates mobile gaming.

### Compliance Pathways

Covered sellers must satisfy one of two pathways before executing each transaction:

**Option A — Affirmative Acknowledgment:** The seller obtains, at the time of each transaction, a consumer acknowledgment confirming that:
- The transaction grants only a license to access the digital good;
- The consumer has received a complete list of the license's restrictions and conditions; and
- If applicable, the seller may unilaterally revoke access if the seller no longer holds rights to distribute the digital good.

**Option B — Clear and Conspicuous Pre-Transaction Statement:** The seller provides, before executing each transaction, a statement in plain language that clarifies "buying" or "purchasing" the digital good means acquiring a license only, accompanied by a hyperlink, QR code, or similar mechanism giving direct access to the full license terms.

Both pathways share a critical requirement: [the disclosure must occur distinctly and separately from any other terms the consumer acknowledges](https://www.gtlaw.com/en/insights/2024/12/ab-2426-new-california-law-requires-clear-licensing-disclosures-for-digital-goods) — including privacy policies, general terms of service, or end-user license agreements. Burying the disclosure within a scroll-through EULA does not satisfy the statute.

The phrase "clear and conspicuous" is defined in the statute as meaning the disclosure must clearly call attention to the language — for example, through larger or contrasting type, font, or color relative to surrounding text, or by being set off by symbols or other marks.

### Exemptions

Three categories are exempt from the statute's requirements:

1. **Subscription-based services**: Digital goods offered solely for the duration of a subscription are exempt. The rationale is that consumers who pay recurring subscription fees already understand the access is tied to the ongoing subscription relationship.
2. **No-cost digital goods**: Digital goods offered without monetary consideration (e.g., free app downloads) are exempt.
3. **Permanently downloadable goods**: Digital goods that the seller cannot revoke after the transaction — for example, a file that can be permanently stored on the consumer's device with no ongoing access control — are exempt.

[Sidley Austin notes](https://www.sidley.com/en/insights/newsupdates/2024/11/californias-new-digital-goods-law-ab-2426-what-you-need-to-know) the subscription exemption is narrower than it first appears: hybrid models where a consumer pays a per-unit fee for content that happens to exist within a subscription ecosystem may not qualify if the per-unit purchase is marketed and executed separately from the subscription.

### Open Legal Questions

Several aspects of the statute remain unresolved as of early enforcement:

**NFTs and blockchain-based assets**: The legislative history is silent on non-fungible tokens. [BakerHostetler analysis](https://www.bakerlaw.com/insights/california-ab-2426-the-ephemeral-nature-of-in-game-assets/) notes that while the purchaser of an NFT typically acquires full ownership of the token, they often receive only a limited license to the digital asset associated with the NFT. To the extent the underlying digital asset (e.g., a digital image or video) falls within Section 17500.6's definitions, disclosure requirements may apply.

**Scope of "buy" on multi-step purchase flows**: Early class action pleadings argue that the cumulative impression across an entire purchase flow — not solely the point-of-sale button — must comply with AB 2426. Courts have not yet ruled on whether a disclosure downstream of the initial "buy" click cures the problem.

**In-game purchases at scale**: For high-volume freemium games with microtransactions, obtaining per-transaction acknowledgments introduces significant UX friction. Platforms are grappling with whether platform-level disclosures made once during onboarding satisfy the per-transaction requirement.

## Impact Assessment [HIGH confidence]

### Affected Industries

AB 2426 affects any company with California-resident customers who sells digital goods under revocable licenses. Primary sectors include:

- **Video game publishers and storefronts**: Includes major console manufacturers (Sony PlayStation Store, Microsoft Xbox Marketplace, Nintendo eShop), PC storefronts (Steam, Epic Games Store, GOG), and mobile marketplaces (Apple App Store, Google Play Store) for individual purchases of paid apps and in-app content.
- **Streaming video services with transactional sales**: Services offering individual movie or episode purchases (Amazon Prime Video, Apple TV, Vudu/Fandango At Home, Google Play Movies) must revisit their "Buy" buttons.
- **Digital music storefronts**: iTunes Store and comparable services selling individual tracks or albums.
- **Ebook marketplaces**: Amazon Kindle, Apple Books, Google Play Books, and similar services selling ebooks under license.
- **Game publishers with online-only titles**: Any publisher of games requiring persistent server connectivity must now disclose the risk of access revocation if servers are shut down.

### Compliance Requirements and Timelines

The law has been effective since **January 1, 2025**. There is no grace period. Companies that had not updated purchase flows by that date were immediately exposed to enforcement risk.

Practical compliance steps include:
- Auditing all digital goods listing pages and purchase flows to identify ownership-conveying language;
- Implementing a compliant disclosure mechanism (Option A acknowledgment form or Option B statement with hyperlink) at the transaction layer, separate from general terms;
- Updating all advertising copy — including email campaigns, social media ads, and App Store metadata — that uses "buy" or "purchase" for revocable-license digital goods;
- Reviewing freemium and DLC offerings to assess whether the per-transaction disclosure requirement applies to in-game purchases.

[Wilson Sonsini notes](https://www.wsgr.com/en/insights/ab-2426-californias-new-law-strengthens-protections-against-false-or-misleading-advertisement-claims-regarding-digital-goods.html) that compliance is not merely a checkout-page exercise: advertising materials upstream of purchase also fall within California's False Advertising Law and are therefore implicated if they use misleading ownership language.

### Enforcement Outlook

**Criminal exposure**: Violation of Section 17500.6 is a misdemeanor punishable by up to six months imprisonment and/or a fine up to $2,500. These criminal penalties are rarely invoked for advertising violations, but the statutory misdemeanor classification elevates the regulatory risk profile.

**Civil penalties**: Public prosecutors (including the California Attorney General and local district attorneys) may seek civil penalties up to $2,500 per violation under the UCL. A single digital storefront with thousands of non-compliant transactions per day could face ruinous per-transaction exposure.

**Private litigation**: The most immediate enforcement pressure is coming from the private plaintiffs' bar. A [federal class action was filed against GameStop in January 2026 (Weber v. GameStop, Inc., No. 2:26-at-00047, E.D. Cal.)](https://topclassactions.com/lawsuit-settlements/lawsuit-news/gamestop-class-action-alleges-digital-games-misrepresented-as-purchases/), alleging that GameStop's website listed digital games for sale using "buy" and "purchase" language without required disclosures. The complaint asserts claims under California's Unfair Competition Law, False Advertising Law, and Consumer Legal Remedies Act. Additional class actions targeting video game platforms are pending. [Stradling Law notes](https://www.stradlinglaw.com/news-insights/selling-digital-goods-in-california-the-risks-and-rules-companies-need-to-know.html) that the combination of UCL standing rules and class certification prospects makes AB 2426 a fertile ground for consumer class actions.

**Attorney General enforcement**: Government enforcement has not yet been publicly announced, but the California AG's office and the California Privacy Protection Agency have both demonstrated willingness to pursue consumer protection actions. AB 2426 is enforced through the False Advertising chapter, making government enforcement a realistic prospect as the law matures.

## Action Items

- **Immediate audit (if not completed)**: Conduct a full review of all digital goods purchase flows, product pages, and advertising materials for ownership-conveying language ("buy," "purchase," "own," "yours forever," etc.) applied to revocable-license digital content.
- **Implement compliant disclosure**: For each non-compliant transaction point, deploy either Option A (per-transaction affirmative acknowledgment with license summary) or Option B (clear and conspicuous pre-transaction statement with hyperlink to full license terms), ensuring the disclosure is visually distinct from all other terms and conditions.
- **Review advertising copy**: Update email campaigns, display ads, social media posts, and App Store or Google Play metadata that use ownership language for revocable-license goods. Passive "Buy" buttons in advertising materials may independently violate the False Advertising Law.
- **Address in-game and DLC purchases**: Determine whether per-item in-game purchases require individual disclosures and, if so, design a UX-compliant mechanism. Consider whether a single session-level disclosure at game launch satisfies the per-transaction requirement.
- **Monitor litigation**: Track the GameStop class action (Weber v. GameStop, Inc., No. 2:26-at-00047, E.D. Cal.) and any follow-on cases for judicial guidance on what constitutes a "clear and conspicuous" disclosure, whether upstream advertising triggers liability, and whether platform-level onboarding disclosures satisfy the per-transaction requirement.
- **Assess NFT and blockchain holdings**: If your platform offers digital goods linked to blockchain-based assets, evaluate whether the underlying digital content falls within Section 17500.6's definitions and, if so, what disclosures are required.
- **Flag for other jurisdictions**: California was the first state to enact this type of law; Maryland has since enacted a substantially similar statute ([HB 208, Consumer Protection – False Advertising – Digital Goods](https://mgaleg.maryland.gov/mgawebsite/Legislation/Details/hb0208?ys=2025RS), signed April 22, 2025, effective October 1, 2025). Monitor state legislative activity for additional digital goods disclosure bills — New York has pending legislation — and prepare adaptable compliance frameworks.
