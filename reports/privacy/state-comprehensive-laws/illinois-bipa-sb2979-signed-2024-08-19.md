# BIPA Update: Illinois Limits Liability and Clarifies Electronic Consent for Biometric Data Collection (SB 2979 Signed)

**Jurisdiction:** Illinois | **Category:** Privacy | **Date:** 2024-08-19

## Executive Summary [HIGH confidence]

On August 2, 2024, Illinois Governor J.B. Pritzker signed [Senate Bill 2979](https://www.ilga.gov/legislation/BillStatus.asp?DocNum=2979&GAID=17&DocTypeID=SB&LegId=152094&SessionID=112&GA=103) into law as [Public Act 103-0769](https://www.ilga.gov/Legislation/publicacts/view/103-0769), the first amendment to the [Illinois Biometric Information Privacy Act (BIPA), 740 ILCS 14](https://www.ilga.gov/Legislation/ILCS/Articles?ActID=3004&ChapterID=57&Print=True) since the statute's 2008 enactment. The law took effect immediately upon signing. Two substantive changes define the amendment: (1) it limits BIPA damages to a single recovery per person, regardless of the number of times biometric data was collected or disclosed from that same individual under the same method — directly overriding the Illinois Supreme Court's per-scan damages rule from *Cothron v. White Castle*; and (2) it adds a definition of "electronic signature" to BIPA and expressly recognizes electronic signatures as a valid form of "written release," resolving a contested compliance question for employers and technology platforms. The legislation dramatically reduces the aggregate damages exposure that had made BIPA the most heavily litigated biometric privacy statute in the country, while leaving the underlying liability framework and private right of action intact. In April 2026, the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit confirmed that the damages limitation applies retroactively to cases pending at the time of enactment.

## Background [HIGH confidence]

### BIPA's Statutory Framework

Illinois enacted [BIPA in 2008 (Public Act 095-0994)](https://www.ilga.gov/Legislation/publicacts/view/095-0994) in response to the bankruptcy sale of Pay By Touch, a high-profile fingerprint-based point-of-sale system, whose liquidation raised concerns about the unchecked transfer of biometric data with no consent framework. BIPA was one of the first statutes in the nation to comprehensively regulate private-sector biometric data collection.

[Section 15 of BIPA (740 ILCS 14/15)](https://www.ilga.gov/Legislation/ILCS/Articles?ActID=3004&ChapterID=57&Print=True) imposes substantive obligations on private entities that collect biometric identifiers (fingerprints, retina/iris scans, voiceprints, hand scans, facial geometry) or biometric information (any information based on a biometric identifier). Core requirements include: providing advance written notice of the collection, obtaining a written release from the subject, establishing a publicly available retention and deletion schedule, and prohibiting profit from or sale of biometric data. [Section 20 (740 ILCS 14/20)](https://www.ilga.gov/Documents/legislation/ilcs/documents/074000140K20.htm) creates a private right of action allowing "aggrieved" persons to recover $1,000 per negligent violation or $5,000 per intentional or reckless violation, plus attorneys' fees and injunctive relief.

### The Rosenbach Decision (2019) and Litigation Explosion

In [*Rosenbach v. Six Flags Entertainment Corp.* (2019)](https://law.justia.com/cases/illinois/supreme-court/2019/123186.html), the Illinois Supreme Court held that a technical violation of BIPA — even without concrete injury — constitutes "aggrieved" status sufficient to bring suit. This ruling opened Illinois courts to mass class action litigation. By 2024, more than 1,500 BIPA lawsuits had been filed, spanning industries from manufacturing and retail to healthcare and finance. The statutory damages model, combined with class certification, created settlement pressure even in cases involving minor or procedural non-compliance.

### The Cothron Decision (2023) and Per-Scan Exposure

In [*Cothron v. White Castle System, Inc.* (2023)](https://law.justia.com/cases/illinois/supreme-court/2023/128004.html), the Illinois Supreme Court held that a separate BIPA claim accrues each time a private entity collects or transmits a biometric identifier without consent — not just once per individual. For White Castle, this meant that each employee fingerprint scan to clock in or access pay stubs constituted a distinct violation. A single employer with approximately 9,500 employees faced estimated class-wide damages exceeding $17 billion based on scan frequency. The Illinois Supreme Court acknowledged the potential for "annihilative" damages but declined to limit recovery judicially, stating that cure lay with the legislature rather than the courts.

*Cothron* produced immediate legislative pressure. Within weeks, the Illinois General Assembly began drafting SB 2979.

## Detailed Analysis [HIGH confidence]

### Change 1: Per-Person Damages Cap (Amending Section 20)

The core change in [SB 2979](https://ilga.gov/legislation/fulltext.asp?DocName=&DocNum=2979&DocTypeId=SB&GA=103&GAID=17&LegID=152094&Session=&SessionId=112) amends Section 20 of BIPA to establish a single-violation rule. The amended text provides:

> "A private entity that, in more than one instance, collects, captures, purchases, receives through trade, or otherwise obtains the same biometric identifier or biometric information from the same person using the same method of collection in violation of [Section 15(b)] has committed a single violation of [Section 15(b)] for which the aggrieved person is entitled to, at most, one recovery."

The same single-recovery cap applies to Section 15(d) disclosure violations — a separate BIPA provision prohibiting disclosure or dissemination without consent. The amendment covers same-recipient multiple disclosures: if a company repeatedly transmits the same person's biometric data to the same third party without consent, that entire course of conduct now constitutes one violation.

The practical effect is stark. As [Seyfarth Shaw noted](https://www.seyfarth.com/news-insights/bipa-legislative-update-governor-pritzker-signs-amendment-limiting-damages-to-a-single-recovery-1.html), the maximum statutory exposure shifts from an amount potentially multiplied by thousands of scans to a ceiling of $5,000 per person for intentional violations, or $1,000 per person for negligent violations. Aggregate class exposure is now bounded by headcount, not scan frequency. For a hypothetical employer with 10,000 employees all collecting fingerprint scans without consent, the theoretical maximum damages exposure drops from billions to $50 million (intentional violations) — still significant, but no longer existential.

Importantly, per [Davis Wright Tremaine's analysis](https://www.dwt.com/blogs/privacy--security-law-blog/2024/08/illinois-bipa-biometrics-law-amended-for-damages), the amendment does not change who can be held liable, what conduct violates the Act, or the $1,000/$5,000 per-violation amounts. The underlying liability standards under Sections 15(b) and 15(d) remain unchanged. Companies must still obtain consent before collecting biometric data, maintain a retention policy, and refrain from profit-driven data sharing.

### Change 2: Electronic Signature and Written Release (Amending Section 10)

SB 2979 also amends Section 10 of BIPA — the definitions section — to add a definition of "electronic signature" and to expressly include electronic signatures within the statute's definition of "written release."

BIPA's Section 15(b) requires entities to obtain a "written release" before collecting biometric data. Prior to SB 2979, that term was defined in [Section 10 (740 ILCS 14/10)](https://www.ilga.gov/Legislation/ILCS/Articles?ActID=3004&ChapterID=57&Print=True) as "informed written consent" or a written release executed by the subject. The term "electronic signature" was never mentioned in the original statute, creating uncertainty about whether a digital checkbox, e-signature on an onboarding form, or click-wrap agreement satisfied the "written release" requirement. Several BIPA defendants faced claims that their consent forms — obtained electronically during onboarding or app registration — were legally insufficient.

As [Greenberg Traurig explained](https://www.gtlaw.com/en/insights/2024/8/bipa-update-illinois-limits-liability-and-clarifies-electronic-consent-for-biometric-data-collection), SB 2979 defines "electronic signature" as:

> "an electronic sound, symbol, or process attached to or logically associated with a record and executed or adopted by a person with the intent to sign the record."

This definition tracks the Electronic Signatures in Global and National Commerce Act (E-SIGN Act) and the Illinois Electronic Commerce Security Act. Consent obtained through a checkbox during employee onboarding, a click-wrap acknowledgment in a mobile app, or an e-signature platform now expressly satisfies BIPA's written release requirement. This resolves a significant source of satellite litigation over the adequacy of electronic consent forms.

### Effective Date and Retroactivity

Public Act 103-0769 took effect immediately upon Governor Pritzker's signing on August 2, 2024. The legislation did not contain an express retroactivity clause, which prompted immediate litigation over whether the damages cap applied to pre-August 2, 2024 conduct and to cases already pending in court.

On April 1, 2026, the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit resolved this question in [*Clay v. Union Pacific Railroad Co.*, No. 25-2185](https://datamatters.sidley.com/2026/04/08/seventh-circuit-limits-potential-damages-under-bipa-holds-2024-amendment-applies-retroactively/), holding that the amendment applies retroactively to all cases pending at the time of enactment. Chief Judge Brennan's opinion reasoned that the amendment was "remedial" rather than "substantive" — it modified the damages provision (Section 20) without altering the underlying liability standards (Section 15), and therefore falls within Illinois' presumption in favor of retroactive application of remedial statutes. As [Sidley Austin reported](https://datamatters.sidley.com/2026/04/08/seventh-circuit-limits-potential-damages-under-bipa-holds-2024-amendment-applies-retroactively/), the court reversed three federal district court decisions that had declined to apply the amendment retroactively.

### Legislative History

The Illinois Senate passed SB 2979 on April 11, 2024, by a 46-13 vote. The Illinois House passed it on May 16, 2024, by an 81-30 vote. Governor Pritzker signed the bill on August 2, 2024. The bipartisan, lopsided votes reflected substantial industry lobbying, including from manufacturers, retailers, and healthcare employers who had faced disproportionate class action exposure from high-volume biometric data collection systems such as time-and-attendance fingerprint scanners.

## Impact Assessment [MEDIUM confidence]

### Industries Most Affected

The amendment primarily benefits private employers who use fingerprint or facial recognition-based time-and-attendance systems, which represent the highest volume BIPA litigation category. As [WilmerHale's 2024 year-in-review](https://www.wilmerhale.com/en/insights/blogs/wilmerhale-privacy-and-cybersecurity-law/20250219-year-in-review-2024-bipa-litigation-takeaways) noted, employment-related BIPA cases dominated the docket, with class actions targeting manufacturers, hospitality companies, healthcare staffing firms, and retail employers. The amendment also benefits technology companies (facial recognition and voice biometric vendors), app developers, and any entity that collects biometric data in high-volume consumer contexts.

Plaintiffs and the plaintiffs' bar lose the per-scan damages multiplier, which had been the primary driver of nine-figure and ten-figure class action valuations. Nonetheless, BIPA class actions remain economically viable where class sizes are large: [Alston & Bird noted](https://www.alston.com/en/insights/publications/2024/08/law-limits-damages-information-privacy-act) that $5,000 per person across a 10,000-person class still yields $50 million in potential damages, providing meaningful incentive to litigate.

### Pending Litigation Outlook

Following *Clay v. Union Pacific* (7th Cir. 2026), hundreds of pending federal BIPA cases are subject to re-valuation. As [Fisher Phillips observed](https://www.fisherphillips.com/en/insights/insights/major-biometric-win-for-business-in-illinois), some cases may face jurisdictional dismissal for falling below the $75,000 amount-in-controversy threshold for federal diversity jurisdiction once exposure is recalculated on a per-person basis. Cases will likely be remanded to Illinois state court in significant numbers.

Several major settlements closed in 2024 before the retroactivity question was settled, potentially reflecting inflated damages expectations. Notable 2024 settlements included Incode Technologies ($4 million), ESO Solutions ($4.1 million), and Magid ($5.1 million). Future settlements are expected to reflect the reduced per-person cap.

### Remaining Compliance Obligations

The amendment does not alter the core compliance obligations that trigger BIPA liability in the first place. As [King & Spalding's analysis](https://www.kslaw.com/news-and-insights/illinois-bipa-reform-takes-effect) emphasized, entities collecting biometric data in Illinois must still:

- Provide a written policy establishing a retention schedule and guidelines for permanent destruction of biometric data.
- Make that policy publicly available.
- Obtain a written release (now expressly including electronic signatures) before collection.
- Not sell, lease, trade, or profit from biometric data.
- Not disclose biometric data without consent or certain enumerated exceptions.

Failure to comply with any of these obligations still gives rise to a private right of action. Only the quantum of damages has changed.

## Action Items

- Employers and technology companies using biometric time-and-attendance, access control, or identity verification systems in Illinois should immediately audit their consent documentation for compliance with the electronic signature definition — a checkbox or click-wrap acknowledgment with clear disclosure language now expressly satisfies the written release requirement.
- Entities with pending BIPA litigation should consult counsel regarding recalculation of exposure under the single-recovery rule and, following *Clay v. Union Pacific* (7th Cir. 2026), on whether pending federal cases should be removed or remanded based on recalculated amount-in-controversy.
- Illinois employers with existing biometric consent forms should ensure forms contain the required disclosures (purpose, duration of collection, third-party recipients) even where electronic — consent form adequacy remains litigable even if the electronic format question is now resolved.
- Entities operating in multiple states should map analogous biometric laws in other jurisdictions (Texas CUBI, Washington MY Health MY Data, and emerging state biometric bills) since BIPA's amendment does not affect those frameworks.
- Companies that have collected biometric data without prior consent must still remediate the underlying violation. The amendment reduces damages exposure but does not eliminate prospective liability for ongoing non-compliant practices.
