# Vermont H.121 (VDPA): Legislature Passes Bill with Consumer Right to Sue -- Then Governor Vetoes It

**Jurisdiction:** Vermont | **Category:** Privacy | **Date:** 2024-06-07

## Executive Summary [HIGH confidence]

On May 10, 2024, the Vermont legislature passed H.121 -- "An act relating to enhancing consumer privacy and the age-appropriate design code" -- which would have enacted the Vermont Data Privacy Act (VDPA) as the most stringent comprehensive state privacy law in the United States. The bill's defining feature was an entirely novel private right of action allowing consumers to sue data brokers and large data holders directly for violations involving sensitive personal data, a provision absent from every other enacted state comprehensive privacy law. Governor Phil Scott vetoed H.121 on June 13, 2024, citing the private right of action as creating unacceptable business risk. The House voted 128-17 to override the veto; the Senate voted 14-15 in favor -- falling six votes short of the two-thirds majority required. The veto was sustained on June 17, 2024. H.121 never became law. Vermont continues to lack any enacted comprehensive adult consumer privacy statute, relying solely on its 2018 data broker registration statute.

## Background [HIGH confidence]

Vermont entered the state privacy law conversation early. In 2018, the state enacted [9 V.S.A. § 2430](https://legislature.vermont.gov/statutes/section/09/062/02430), one of the nation's first data broker registration laws, requiring any business that knowingly collects and sells or licenses the brokered personal information of consumers with whom it has no direct relationship to register annually with the Vermont Attorney General. That law imposed no individual consumer rights and no general controller obligations, but it gave Vermont a baseline presence in data broker regulation.

Beginning in the 2022-2023 legislative biennium, Vermont lawmakers pursued comprehensive consumer privacy legislation. H.121 was introduced into the 2023-2024 session and moved through an extended committee process spanning roughly 18 months. The [Electronic Privacy Information Center (EPIC)](https://epic.org/vermont-house-unanimously-passes-vermont-data-privacy-act/) noted that the House passed H.121 unanimously -- an unusual degree of consensus for legislation of this scope. The Senate passed a final version in the early morning hours of May 11, 2024, just before the close of the legislative session.

H.121 emerged at roughly the same moment Maryland's Online Data Privacy Act (MODPA) was signed into law, making it the strictest enacted comprehensive privacy law in the United States. Vermont's bill would have equaled or exceeded MODPA in several respects, particularly the private right of action and outright prohibition on selling sensitive data.

## Detailed Analysis [HIGH confidence]

### Key Provisions of H.121

**Applicability thresholds.** H.121 applied to controllers and processors that, during a calendar year, either: (a) controlled or processed the personal data of at least 100,000 consumers, or (b) controlled or processed the personal data of at least 25,000 consumers and derived more than 25 percent of gross revenue from the sale of personal data. A tiered timeline applied: a lower threshold of 25,000 consumers for certain processing would have applied from July 1, 2025, with a further reduced threshold of 6,250 consumers phasing in on July 1, 2027. Critically, provisions governing consumer health data, minors' data, and data broker obligations were not subject to applicability thresholds.

**Consumer rights.** The bill granted consumers a standard suite of rights modeled on Connecticut's data privacy framework: rights to confirm, access, correct, delete, and port personal data; rights to opt out of targeted advertising, sale of personal data, and profiling for automated decisions with legal or significant effects; and a novel right to obtain a list of third parties to whom their personal data had been disclosed -- a provision that went beyond most state laws. Controllers would have had 45 days to respond to consumer requests, with a potential 45-day extension.

**Data minimization.** Similar to Maryland's MODPA, H.121 imposed a data minimization obligation prohibiting controllers from processing personal data beyond what was reasonably necessary and proportionate to the stated purpose. This represented a stricter approach than Virginia, Connecticut, and Colorado models, which permit processing upon adequate notice.

**Prohibition on sale of sensitive data.** Unlike most state laws that require only consent before selling sensitive data, H.121 would have prohibited the sale of sensitive data entirely, even with consumer consent. Sensitive categories included racial or ethnic origin, religious beliefs, mental or physical health diagnoses, sexual orientation, citizenship or immigration status, genetic or biometric data, precise geolocation, and financial information. According to [Hunton Andrews Kurth's analysis](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-cybersecurity-law-blog/vermont-legislature-passes-state-privacy-bill-with-right-to-sue-for-consumers), this outright prohibition aligned H.121 more closely with Washington's My Health My Data Act.

**Mandatory Global Privacy Control (GPC) recognition.** H.121 required controllers to honor opt-out signals expressed through universal opt-out mechanisms such as the Global Privacy Control -- a browser-based opt-out signal. Mandatory GPC recognition was not universal among state laws as of 2024.

**Consumer health data protections.** A dedicated subchapter addressed consumer health data modeled on Washington's My Health My Data Act, including prohibitions on disclosing health data to third parties and restrictions on geofencing within 1,850 feet of healthcare facilities.

**Age-Appropriate Design Code.** H.121 incorporated Vermont's version of the Age-Appropriate Design Code for minors, requiring controllers to apply default privacy settings at the highest available level, to avoid collecting or sharing a minor's personal data beyond what is necessary, and to meet a "minimum duty of care" preventing design choices that cause reasonably foreseeable emotional distress or compulsive use in minors. This provision was later extracted as a standalone bill and signed into law as [S.69](https://www.zwillgen.com/privacy/vermont-signs-age-appropriate-design-code-into-law/) on June 12, 2025.

### The Private Right of Action: H.121's Defining and Fatal Feature

The private right of action (PRA) was H.121's most novel -- and most controversial -- feature. No enacted comprehensive state privacy law in the United States included a consumer private right of action as of the time H.121 passed the Vermont legislature. Under H.121's final version, the PRA would have applied only to:

1. Data brokers and "large data holders" (entities processing records of at least 100,000 Vermont residents per year)
2. Violations involving **sensitive data** specifically -- including sensitive data processing without consent, COPPA violations with respect to children's sensitive data, and health data confidentiality breaches
3. **Actual damages** only -- no statutory damages per violation of the kind that have generated significant BIPA litigation in Illinois

The PRA would have taken effect January 1, 2027 and was set to sunset on January 1, 2029 -- a deliberate legislative compromise to evaluate its practical effects before making it permanent. A 45-to-90-day cure period would have preceded any viable private suit, giving covered entities an opportunity to remediate violations before being exposed to litigation.

According to [IAPP's analysis](https://iapp.org/news/a/pra-novelty-highlight-vermont-s-comprehensive-privacy-bill), the design of Vermont's PRA attempted to thread a needle: broad enough to provide consumers a direct avenue for redress, narrow enough to limit exposure for small and mid-sized businesses. The limitation to sensitive data violations was intended to confine litigation to high-stakes harms rather than technical process violations.

A comparative analysis by [Freeman Mathis & Gary](https://www.fmglaw.com/appellate-advocacy/the-private-right-of-action-in-privacy-laws-comparing-vermont-to-california/) noted that even California's CCPA -- the most litigated state privacy statute -- does not include a general private right of action. California's PRA is limited to data breaches and security violations. Vermont's proposed PRA for sensitive data processing violations represented a significant extension of direct consumer enforcement beyond any existing state model.

### The Veto

Governor Phil Scott vetoed H.121 on June 13, 2024. The [Governor's official press release](https://governor.vermont.gov/press-release/action-taken-governor-phil-scott-legislation-june-13-2024) stated that the bill "creates an unnecessary and avoidable level of risk." The governor's objections centered on three points:

1. **Private Right of Action.** Scott wrote that allowing consumers to sue directly "would make Vermont a national outlier, and more hostile than any other state to many businesses and non-profits," generating fear and legal uncertainty far exceeding actual litigation.
2. **Cumulative business burden.** The governor noted Vermont businesses were simultaneously absorbing a payroll tax, Clean Heat Standard, and property tax increases, making H.121's compliance costs ill-timed.
3. **Expansive definitions.** The governor cited overly broad definitions that he believed would sweep in businesses and nonprofits beyond the bill's intended targets.

Governor Scott explicitly recommended that Vermont instead adopt Connecticut's data privacy framework -- the same approach New Hampshire adopted -- citing the value of regional consistency.

Vermont Attorney General Charity Clark [issued a formal rebuttal](https://ago.vermont.gov/blog/2024/06/14/statement-attorney-general-clark-governors-veto-h121-vermont-data-privacy-act) calling the veto "extremely disappointing" and arguing that without a private right of action, enforcement would fall entirely on taxpayers through the AG's office rather than empowering aggrieved consumers.

### The Failed Veto Override

At the June 17, 2024 veto session, the House voted 128-17 to override the veto -- decisively exceeding the two-thirds threshold. The Senate voted 14-15 in favor of override, falling six votes short of the 20 votes required under the Vermont Constitution. Per the [Digital Policy Alert](https://digitalpolicyalert.org/event/20700-sustained-governors-veto-regarding-vermont-act-relating-to-enhancing-consumer-privacy-hb-121-including-registration-requirement-for-data-brokers) and [EPIC](https://epic.org/vermont-senate-sustains-governors-veto-of-vermont-privacy-act/), the veto was officially sustained. H.121 is dead as a legislative matter.

The narrow Senate margin -- one vote -- illustrates that H.121 had overwhelming popular and legislative support in the lower chamber but faced a different calculus in the Senate, where business community and Governor's opposition proved sufficient to hold a minority of senators.

## Impact Assessment [MEDIUM confidence]

### Who Was Affected and Why It Matters

Vermont's small population (approximately 650,000 residents) means that most Vermont-specific legislation affects large national actors rather than local small businesses -- the 100,000-consumer applicability threshold would have captured primarily technology companies, large data brokers, and national retailers, not local Vermont stores.

The PRA's proposed scope -- actual damages only, sensitive data violations only, large data holders only, with a cure period -- represented a carefully calibrated attempt to limit litigation exposure while preserving a meaningful enforcement mechanism. The Governor's objection was essentially precautionary: the fear of litigation, not documented evidence that the limited PRA would generate excessive suits.

Had H.121 become law, it would have set a precedent that could have accelerated PRA adoption in other states with comprehensive privacy bills pending. The failure reinforces the pattern that state legislatures face intense business lobbying against any private right of action in comprehensive privacy law.

### National Significance of the PRA Debate

Vermont's experience with H.121 has become a reference point in the ongoing national debate over whether comprehensive privacy laws should include private rights of action. As [WilmerHale observed](https://www.wilmerhale.com/en/insights/blogs/wilmerhale-privacy-and-cybersecurity-law/20240611-minnesota-and-vermont-legislatures-pass-comprehensive-privacy-laws), every enacted comprehensive state privacy law -- Virginia, Colorado, Connecticut, Texas, Montana, Indiana, Tennessee, Iowa, Delaware, Nebraska, New Hampshire, Maryland, Minnesota, and others -- excludes a consumer private right of action. Vermont would have been the sole exception.

The [PIRG analysis](https://pirg.org/updates/gov-scott-may-veto-vermonts-new-privacy-bill-one-of-the-strongest-nationwide/) described H.121 as "one of the strongest privacy bills nationwide" precisely because of the PRA. Its defeat illustrates a structural asymmetry: legislatures can assemble strong majorities to pass protective legislation, but a small minority in the Senate combined with gubernatorial opposition can still block enactment.

### Successor Legislation

Following H.121's defeat, Vermont introduced multiple successor bills in the 2025-2026 session including S.71, H.208, and H.211. As of early 2026, none have passed. The same dispute over a private right of action remains the central obstacle. Governor Scott signed the Age-Appropriate Design Code provisions (S.69) as a standalone bill on June 12, 2025 -- demonstrating that the child-safety components of H.121 commanded sufficient political support to survive independently when separated from the PRA and adult privacy framework.

## Action Items

- Businesses operating in Vermont should monitor H.211, S.71, and H.208 in the 2025-2026 Vermont legislative session; any of these could advance quickly if the PRA dispute is resolved.
- Businesses targeting Vermont minors should prepare for compliance with the Vermont Age-Appropriate Design Code (S.69), which was signed June 12, 2025 and takes effect January 1, 2027.
- Data brokers operating in Vermont must maintain compliance with Vermont's existing data broker registration statute ([9 V.S.A. § 2430](https://legislature.vermont.gov/statutes/section/09/062/02430)), which remains fully in force regardless of H.121's defeat.
- Privacy counsel advising on comprehensive privacy compliance programs should note that Vermont currently has no enacted comprehensive adult consumer privacy law -- programs built around CCPA or Virginia-model compliance do not address Vermont's distinct obligations.
- Policy teams tracking the national PRA debate should monitor whether Vermont's 2025-2026 successor bills attempt a compromise PRA structure (e.g., even narrower scope, longer cure period, or AG-approval prerequisites) that could thread the political needle.
