# Colorado Amends Privacy Act to Add Biometric Data Requirements (HB 24-1130)

**Jurisdiction:** Colorado | **Category:** Privacy | **Date:** June 4, 2024

## Executive Summary [HIGH confidence]

On May 31, 2024, Colorado Governor Jared Polis signed [House Bill 24-1130](https://content.leg.colorado.gov/sites/default/files/2024a_1130_signed.pdf) into law, amending the [Colorado Privacy Act (CPA), C.R.S. § 6-1-1301 et seq.](http://leg.colorado.gov/bills/hb24-1130) to impose comprehensive new obligations on controllers that collect or process biometric data. The law takes effect July 1, 2025, giving businesses just over a year to prepare. HB 24-1130 takes a novel approach among U.S. states by integrating biometric-specific requirements similar to Illinois's Biometric Information Privacy Act (BIPA) directly into a comprehensive state consumer privacy statute, rather than enacting a standalone biometric law. It covers any entity processing biometric data of Colorado residents -- regardless of whether the entity meets the CPA's general data volume thresholds -- and notably limits employers' ability to condition employment on biometric consent. Unlike BIPA, the law does not create a private right of action; enforcement rests with the Colorado Attorney General and district attorneys. Supporting implementing rules were finalized by the Colorado AG on December 5, 2024, and became effective January 30, 2025.

## Background [HIGH confidence]

### The Colorado Privacy Act

Colorado enacted the [CPA via Senate Bill 21-190](https://coag.gov/resources/colorado-privacy-act/), signed July 7, 2021 and effective July 1, 2023. The CPA is a Virginia-model omnibus consumer privacy statute that applies to entities controlling or processing personal data of 100,000 or more Colorado consumers per year, or 25,000 or more consumers per year when the entity derives revenue or receives a discount on the price of goods or services from the sale of personal data (with no minimum revenue percentage required). The CPA grants consumers a suite of rights -- access, correction, deletion, portability, and opt-out of targeted advertising, sale, and profiling -- and requires controllers to conduct data protection assessments for high-risk processing activities.

Prior to HB 24-1130, the CPA already classified biometric data as a category of "sensitive data" requiring opt-in consent, but lacked the detailed, standalone biometric-specific obligations found in dedicated biometric privacy statutes like Illinois BIPA (740 ILCS 14/) or Texas's Capture or Use of Biometric Identifier Act (Tex. Bus. & Com. Code § 503.001). HB 24-1130 fills that gap by layering BIPA-style obligations onto the CPA's existing framework.

### Growing State Biometric Patchwork

HB 24-1130 emerged against a backdrop of increasing state-level biometric privacy legislation. Illinois enacted BIPA in 2008, establishing the model of notice, consent, and retention limits for biometric identifiers. By 2024, [Texas, Washington, and other states](https://www.steptoe.com/en/news-publications/colorado-adds-to-a-growing-biometric-data-privacy-patchwork-and-vermont-may-follow.html) had adopted biometric-specific laws, though most lacked BIPA's private right of action and litigation exposure. Colorado's approach -- embedding biometric obligations within the CPA rather than enacting a standalone statute -- represents a newer legislative model that other states may follow.

In April 2024, Colorado also enacted [HB 24-1058](https://content.leg.colorado.gov/sites/default/files/2024a_1058_signed.pdf) (signed April 17, 2024; effective August 6, 2024), which separately added biological and neural data to the CPA's sensitive data category. HB 24-1130 and HB 24-1058 together represent a coordinated legislative session effort to significantly expand the CPA's scope to cover emerging data types.

## Detailed Analysis [HIGH confidence]

### Scope and Applicability

HB 24-1130's biometric provisions apply to any controller that "controls or processes" biometric identifiers from Colorado residents, irrespective of whether the entity meets the CPA's usual data volume thresholds. This threshold-free applicability is a significant departure from the rest of the CPA and means that small businesses and organizations that would otherwise be exempt from the CPA may be subject to the biometric requirements if they collect any biometric data from Colorado residents.

### Key Definitions

The law establishes a two-tier definitional framework:

- **"Biometric identifier"**: Data generated by technological processing, measurement, or analysis of a consumer's biological, physical, or behavioral characteristics, which "can be processed for the purpose of uniquely identifying an individual." This expressly includes fingerprints, voiceprints, retina scans, and facial geometry. The definition is broader than Illinois BIPA's because it captures measurements that *could* identify an individual, not just those *actually used* to do so. As [Venable LLP notes](https://www.venable.com/insights/publications/2024/06/colorado-amends-state-privacy-law-to-include), the Colorado definition also extends to "other unique biological, physical, or behavioral patterns or characteristics," which is broader than BIPA's enumerated list.

- **"Biometric data"**: One or more biometric identifiers that are "used or intended to be used, singly or in combination with each other or with other personal data, for identification purposes." The distinction matters because certain obligations -- particularly around consent for disclosure -- attach specifically to "biometric data," while the written policy and notice requirements apply to "biometric identifiers" more broadly.

Digital photographs, audio recordings, and voice recordings are not treated as biometric data unless the biometric identifiers derived from them are used for identification purposes.

### Notice Requirements

Before collecting or processing any biometric identifier, a controller must provide clear and accessible notice to the consumer that discloses:
1. That a biometric identifier is being collected;
2. The specific purpose for which the biometric identifier is being collected;
3. The length of time the controller will retain the biometric identifier; and
4. If the biometric identifier will be disclosed to a processor, the identity of the processor and the purpose for disclosure.

The notice may be embedded in the controller's general privacy notice provided it is clearly labeled so that consumers can easily locate the relevant information. According to the [AG's December 2024 implementing rules](https://www.wilmerhale.com/en/insights/blogs/wilmerhale-privacy-and-cybersecurity-law/20241219-colorado-ag-finalizes-new-rules-for-cpa), this notice must be provided "at or before" initial collection.

### Consent for Collection and Disclosure

Consent is required before a controller:
- Collects or processes any biometric identifier; or
- Sells, leases, trades, discloses, rediscloses, or otherwise disseminates biometric identifiers to a third party.

Disclosure without fresh consent is permitted only where: (a) it is necessary to fulfill the purpose for which the biometric identifier was collected; (b) it is required to complete a financial transaction requested by the consumer; or (c) it is required by law.

### Written Biometric Policy and Data Retention

Controllers must adopt a written biometric data policy that:
- Establishes a retention schedule for biometric identifiers;
- Includes a protocol for responding to data security incidents involving biometric data;
- Establishes guidelines requiring deletion of biometric identifiers by the earliest of:
  - The date on which the initial purpose for collecting the identifier has been satisfied;
  - 24 months after the consumer's last interaction with the controller; or
  - The earliest reasonably feasible date -- which must be no more than **45 days** after the controller determines that storage is no longer necessary or relevant to the express processing purpose (this window may be extended by up to an additional 45 days when the complexity or volume of biometric data makes immediate deletion infeasible).

The written policy must be reviewed at least once annually by the controller. As [Bryan Cave Leighton Paisner](https://www.bclplaw.com/en-US/events-insights-news/colorados-new-requirements-for-biometric-data-what-businesses-need-to-know.html) notes, this annual review obligation is more operationally demanding than comparable requirements in other states and will require dedicated internal processes.

### Employer and Employment Context

HB 24-1130 contains significant provisions governing the use of biometric data in employment settings. Employers may require consent to biometric collection as a condition of employment only for the following specific purposes:
- Permitting access to secure physical locations or hardware;
- Recording the commencement and conclusion of an employee's workday;
- Improving or monitoring workplace and public safety.

Critically, employers may **not** condition employment on consent to use biometrics to:
- Track an employee's physical location; or
- Monitor the amount of time an employee spends using a hardware or software application.

The term "employee" is defined broadly to include full-time, part-time, on-call employees as well as contractors, subcontractors, interns, and fellows. As [Littler Mendelson](https://www.littler.com/news-analysis/asap/implications-employers-colorados-new-biometrics-law) observes, this definition captures most workforce configurations and means that staffing agency relationships are not a simple workaround.

### Enforcement and Remedies

Unlike Illinois BIPA -- which has generated thousands of class action lawsuits and hundreds of millions of dollars in settlements -- HB 24-1130 does not provide a private right of action. Enforcement authority rests exclusively with the Colorado Attorney General and district attorneys. The AG's general CPA enforcement toolkit applies: investigators may seek civil penalties, and violators receive a 60-day cure period before penalties are imposed (that cure period was in effect until January 1, 2025; post-2025 violations are subject to direct enforcement without the cure window per the CPA's sunset of the cure provision).

The absence of a private right of action significantly reduces litigation risk compared to BIPA, but does not eliminate compliance urgency -- the AG has demonstrated a willingness to use CPA enforcement authority and the new biometric implementing rules provide a clear compliance benchmark.

### AG Implementing Rules (December 2024)

The Colorado AG finalized implementing rules on December 5, 2024, filed with the Secretary of State on December 17, 2024, and effective January 30, 2025. The rules address:
- Specific formatting and content requirements for the biometric notice;
- Clarification of consent standards for biometric identifiers;
- A "good faith compliance defense" expanded from prior draft versions; and
- Procedures for submitting opinion letter requests to the AG for guidance on specific data practices.

As [Sidley Austin's Data Matters blog](https://datamatters.sidley.com/2025/01/09/colorado-finalizes-privacy-act-rules-key-updates-for-businesses/) reports, the rules offer businesses more flexibility than early drafts but still impose meaningful operational requirements on covered controllers.

## Impact Assessment [HIGH confidence]

### Who Is Affected

Because HB 24-1130 applies without regard to the CPA's volume thresholds, the law's reach extends well beyond the universe of entities already subject to the CPA. Any business -- of any size -- that collects fingerprints, retina scans, voiceprints, facial geometry measurements, or other biometric identifiers from Colorado residents falls within scope. Industries with significant exposure include:

- **Technology and SaaS**: Products using facial recognition, voice authentication, or fingerprint scanning for access control or identity verification;
- **Retail and payments**: Biometric checkout systems and point-of-sale authentication;
- **Physical security and access control**: Building entry systems using fingerprint or iris readers;
- **Employers broadly**: Any employer using biometric time-and-attendance systems or secure facility access;
- **Healthcare adjacent**: Technologies outside HIPAA-regulated contexts that process physical biometric characteristics.

### Compliance Requirements and Timeline

With the July 1, 2025 effective date, organizations had approximately 13 months from the Governor's signing to come into compliance. The AG's implementing rules (effective January 30, 2025) provided critical additional specificity. Key compliance obligations include:

1. **Inventory**: Audit all products, systems, and processes that collect, process, or disclose biometric data from Colorado residents;
2. **Written policy**: Draft and adopt a compliant written biometric data policy covering retention schedules, security incident response, and deletion guidelines;
3. **Notice update**: Update privacy notices to include the four required biometric-specific disclosures;
4. **Consent workflows**: Implement consent collection mechanisms before initial biometric collection and before any third-party disclosure;
5. **Employer protocols**: Review and update employment agreements and HR data practices to ensure biometric consent is obtained only for permissible conditioning purposes;
6. **Annual review cycle**: Establish processes for annual review of the written biometric policy.

### Comparison to Illinois BIPA and Enforcement Outlook

The [Security Industry Association](https://www.securityindustry.org/2024/06/03/colorado-adds-new-requirements-for-biometric-data-to-its-privacy-law-marginal-adjustments-compared-to-illinois-bipa/) has described HB 24-1130 as imposing obligations comparable to BIPA but with "marginal adjustments." The absence of a private right of action removes the class action litigation exposure that has made BIPA so costly for defendants. However, the law's broader definitional framework for "biometric identifier" -- capturing any data that *could* identify an individual -- may create compliance uncertainty in borderline cases where a company's biometric processing does not cross into actual identification.

The AG's expanding CPA enforcement activity, including its adoption of detailed biometric implementing rules, signals that biometric compliance will be a genuine enforcement priority in Colorado. Organizations should treat the July 1, 2025 effective date as a hard deadline rather than a soft target.

## Action Items

- **Immediately**: Conduct a biometric data inventory to identify all products, systems, employment practices, and vendor relationships involving biometric identifiers from Colorado residents.
- **Before July 1, 2025**: Draft and adopt a compliant written biometric policy addressing retention schedules, security incident protocols, and deletion timelines. The policy must be reviewed annually.
- **Before July 1, 2025**: Update privacy notices to include the four HB 24-1130 required disclosures (collection notice, purpose, retention period, processor disclosure if applicable).
- **Before July 1, 2025**: Implement or update consent workflows for biometric collection and any third-party disclosure of biometric identifiers.
- **HR and employment teams**: Audit all uses of employee biometric data; revise employment consent documents to reflect that biometric consent may only be conditioned on employment for access control, timekeeping, and emergency safety purposes -- not location tracking or application usage monitoring.
- **Review AG implementing rules** (effective January 30, 2025) for specific notice format and content guidance; consider submitting opinion letter requests to the AG for any uncertain use cases.
- **Monitor AG enforcement activity** under the CPA for early enforcement actions that signal compliance interpretation.
