# Alabama Becomes 21st State to Enact Comprehensive Privacy Law (HB 351 — APDPA)

## Executive Summary [HIGH confidence]

On April 7, 2026, the Alabama Legislature unanimously passed [House Bill 351](https://alison.legislature.state.al.us/files/pdf/SearchableInstruments/2026RS/HB351-enr.pdf), the Alabama Personal Data Protection Act ("APDPA"), making Alabama the [21st U.S. state](https://privacymatters.dlapiper.com/2026/04/u-s-alabama-becomes-21st-state-to-enact-comprehensive-privacy-law/) to adopt a comprehensive consumer privacy statute. The bill cleared the House 104-0 and the Senate 34-0 on April 7, 2026, and was signed by Governor Kay Ivey on April 16, 2026. The law takes effect on **May 1, 2027**.

While the APDPA broadly tracks the Virginia/Connecticut template, it contains several notable departures — including one of the lowest applicability thresholds in the country, a narrower definition of "sale," a non-sunsetting cure period, expanded entity-level exemptions, and no requirement to recognize universal opt-out preference signals such as Global Privacy Control — that will require tailored compliance mapping.

## Applicability [HIGH confidence]

The APDPA applies to persons who conduct business in Alabama or produce products or services targeted to Alabama residents and who, during a calendar year, either:

- Control or process the personal data of **more than 25,000 consumers** (excluding data processed solely to complete a payment transaction); or
- Derive **more than 25% of gross revenue from the sale of personal data**.

As [IAPP notes](https://iapp.org/news/a/alabama-set-to-add-variation-to-us-state-privacy-patchwork), Alabama's 25,000-consumer threshold is the lowest numerical floor among comprehensive state privacy laws enacted to date — lower than the 100,000-consumer threshold used in Colorado, Connecticut, Minnesota, Oregon, and New Jersey, and lower than Delaware and Maryland's 35,000-consumer thresholds. This relatively low bar means the statute will sweep in a broader set of mid-market companies than peer state laws.

## Consumer Rights [HIGH confidence]

Alabama consumers (defined as Alabama residents acting in an individual or household context, not in an employment or B2B capacity) receive the now-standard suite of rights:

- **Access / confirmation** — confirm whether a controller processes the consumer's personal data and access that data.
- **Correction** — correct inaccuracies in personal data.
- **Deletion** — delete personal data provided by or obtained about the consumer.
- **Portability** — obtain a portable copy of personal data in a usable format.
- **Opt-out** — opt out of (i) targeted advertising, (ii) the sale of personal data, and (iii) profiling in furtherance of solely automated decisions producing legal or similarly significant effects.

Controllers must respond within 45 days, with a permitted 45-day extension.

## Definition of "Sale" [HIGH confidence]

The APDPA defines "sale" as an exchange of personal data for monetary or other valuable consideration where the controller receives a material benefit **and** the third party is not restricted in subsequent uses of the data. This narrower formulation — requiring the receiving party to be *unrestricted* in downstream use — is a meaningful departure from the CCPA-style definition, per [IAPP](https://iapp.org/news/a/alabama-set-to-add-variation-to-us-state-privacy-patchwork).

The statute also contains sale-exclusion carve-outs not found in other states, including disclosures for the purposes of "providing analytics services" or "providing marketing services solely to the controller." This means activities classified as "sales" under California law may fall outside Alabama's scope — but only where contractual restrictions on downstream use are in place.

## Controller and Processor Obligations [HIGH confidence]

Controllers must:

- Limit collection to what is adequate, relevant, and reasonably necessary for specified purposes.
- Establish and maintain reasonable administrative, technical, and physical data security practices.
- Provide a clear and accessible privacy notice.
- Post a clear, conspicuous link to opt out of sales and targeted advertising.
- Obtain opt-in consent before processing sensitive data.

Processors must adhere to controller instructions and execute data processing agreements. Written contracts must address confidentiality, subcontractor flow-downs, deletion or return of data at contract termination, and audit rights — mirroring the VCDPA-style controller/processor contracting framework.

## Exemptions [HIGH confidence]

### Entity-Level Exemptions

The APDPA's exemption structure is broader than many peer laws. Exempt entities include:

- State agencies and political subdivisions of Alabama.
- Institutions of higher education.
- Financial institutions subject to the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act (entity-level, not merely data-level — broader than California and Oregon, which use data-level GLBA carve-outs).
- HIPAA-covered entities and their business associates.
- Businesses with fewer than **500 employees** that do not sell personal data — an unusually broad headcount-based threshold; most state laws rely on revenue or consumer-record thresholds alone.
- Nonprofits with fewer than 100 employees that do not sell personal data.
- Political action committees, political parties, principal campaign committees, and political organizations defined under [26 U.S.C. § 527](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/527), as well as entities that sell data primarily to such organizations. This exemption was added by Senate amendment to address potential First Amendment concerns.
- Electric providers subject to NERC reliability standards.

### Data-Level Exemptions

HR/employee and B2B data; PHI governed by HIPAA; FCRA consumer-report data; DPPA-regulated data; FERPA educational records; Farm Credit Act data; and data processed under the Airline Deregulation Act.

## Departures from the Virginia Model [HIGH confidence]

According to [DLA Piper's Privacy Matters](https://privacymatters.dlapiper.com/2026/04/u-s-alabama-becomes-21st-state-to-enact-comprehensive-privacy-law/), [Maynard Nexsen's analysis](https://www.maynardnexsen.com/publication-alabama-legislature-passes-comprehensive-data-privacy-bill), and [IAPP](https://iapp.org/news/a/alabama-set-to-add-variation-to-us-state-privacy-patchwork), the APDPA departs from the Virginia template in several significant respects:

1. **No Data Protection Impact Assessments (DPIAs).** Unlike Virginia, Colorado, Connecticut, and most subsequent state privacy laws, the APDPA does not require controllers to conduct or document DPIAs for higher-risk processing activities (including targeted advertising, sale of personal data, certain profiling, and processing of sensitive data).

2. **Non-sunsetting 45-day cure period.** The AG must provide 45-day written notice and an opportunity to cure before initiating an enforcement action, and this cure right **does not sunset**. If the controller cures and provides an express written statement that no further violations will occur, no action may be brought. Most Virginia-model states originally included cure periods that expired on a set date; Alabama's is permanent — among the most business-friendly enforcement postures in the country.

3. **No universal opt-out / preference signal requirement.** A Senate amendment removed a House-passed provision that would have required controllers to recognize browser-based universal opt-out signals such as Global Privacy Control (GPC). Alabama therefore does not mandate UOOM recognition, unlike [Connecticut (Conn. Gen. Stat. § 42-520)](https://www.cga.ct.gov/current/pub/chap_743jj.htm), [Colorado (C.R.S. § 6-1-1306)](https://law.justia.com/codes/colorado/title-6/article-1/part-13/section-6-1-1306/), California (CCPA/CPRA), Oregon, Texas, Montana, New Jersey, and Delaware.

4. **Narrower definition of "sale"** with analytics and marketing-to-controller carve-outs (see above).

5. **No private right of action.** Enforcement is exclusive to the Alabama Attorney General.

6. **Broader entity-level exemptions**, including the political-organization carve-out and 500-employee small-business threshold not typically found in other state laws.

## Enforcement and Penalties [HIGH confidence]

The [Alabama Attorney General](https://www.billtrack50.com/billdetail/1957511) has **exclusive enforcement authority**. Before bringing an action, the AG must provide the alleged violator with written notice and a 45-day cure opportunity. Civil penalties are capped at **$15,000 per violation**. There is no private right of action.

## AI and Automated Decision-Making [MEDIUM confidence]

As [Privacy Daily reported](https://privacy-daily.com/article/2026/02/25/alabama-house-passes-comprehensive-privacy-bill-with-no-mention-of-ai-2602250016?BC=bc_679b511912c1f), the bill contains no express references to artificial intelligence. Coverage of automated decisions is limited to the standard Virginia-model right to opt out of profiling in furtherance of solely automated decisions producing legal or similarly significant effects. The APDPA does not include the more developed ADMT/AI rulemaking frameworks emerging in California, Colorado, or New York — a conspicuous absence given the 2025–2026 wave of state AI laws.

## Compliance Action Items

- **Re-scope applicability.** The 25,000-consumer threshold will pull in companies that were previously out-of-scope under VCDPA-style 100,000-consumer floors. Mid-sized marketers, e-commerce firms, and ad-tech intermediaries should run fresh Alabama-resident counts. Also confirm whether 25% of gross revenue is derived from the sale of personal data.
- **Revisit "sale" categorization.** The narrower Alabama definition, combined with the analytics/marketing carve-outs, may permit activities classified as "sales" under California law to fall outside Alabama's scope — but only where contractual restrictions on downstream use are in place. Audit vendor contracts now.
- **Update privacy notices** before May 1, 2027 to add Alabama-specific rights language, opt-out mechanisms, and the AG complaint mechanism.
- **Extend consumer rights infrastructure** to cover Alabama residents (access, correction, deletion, portability, opt-outs) within the 45-day response window (extendable by 45 days).
- **Confirm opt-out mechanisms** for sale, targeted advertising, and profiling are accessible from Alabama-facing properties. Note that GPC/UOOM recognition is not required in Alabama; existing multi-state GPC handling can remain but need not be extended specifically for Alabama compliance.
- **Review data processing agreements** with processors to confirm Alabama-compliant terms (confidentiality, subcontractor flow-downs, deletion/return, audit rights).
- **Evaluate entity-level exemption eligibility** if the organization has fewer than 500 employees and does not sell personal data; is a GLBA-covered financial institution; is a HIPAA-covered entity; or is a nonprofit, political organization, or electric provider.
- **Confirm sensitive data consent.** Processing of sensitive data requires opt-in consent under the Virginia model that Alabama adopts.
- **Track gubernatorial signing** via the [Alabama Legislature's bill status page](https://alison.legislature.state.al.us/files/pdf/SearchableInstruments/2026RS/HB351-enr.pdf) to confirm the effective-date trigger.

For organizations already compliant with the VCDPA and peer state laws, the incremental build for Alabama should be relatively modest. Companies that rely on a "VCDPA-compliant = APDPA-compliant" shortcut can do so with low risk, since the APDPA is functionally a subset of Virginia obligations. The inverse is not true: organizations that built to Alabama alone would fall short of Colorado, California, or Connecticut.
