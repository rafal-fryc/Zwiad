# Vermont Enacts Act 138 (H.211), Overhauling Its Data Broker Registration Law

**Jurisdiction:** Vermont | **Category:** Privacy | **Date:** 2026-06-16

## Executive Summary [MEDIUM confidence] [NEEDS REVIEW]

On June 16, 2026, Vermont Governor Phil Scott signed [H.211](https://legislature.vermont.gov/Documents/2026/Docs/BILLS/H-0211/H-0211%20As%20Passed%20by%20Both%20House%20and%20Senate%20Unofficial.pdf) into law as [Act 138](https://legislature.vermont.gov/Documents/2026/Docs/ACTS/ACT138/ACT138%20Act%20Summary.pdf), substantially amending Vermont's existing data broker registration statute at [9 V.S.A. chapter 62](https://legislature.vermont.gov/statutes/chapter/09/062) ("Protection of Personal Information"). This is a distinct statutory scheme from Vermont's newly enacted comprehensive consumer privacy law, [S.71 (the Vermont Data Privacy and Surveillance Act)](reports/privacy/state-comprehensive-laws/vermont-sb71-data-privacy-surveillance-act-2026-06-15.md); the two bills passed in the same legislative session but amend different chapters and serve different regulatory purposes. Act 138 raises the annual data broker registration fee nearly ninefold (from $100 to $900), adds a new $20,000 surety bond requirement, broadens the definition of "brokered personal information" to align more closely with modern comprehensive privacy statutes, creates a broker-by-broker consumer right to request deletion of brokered personal information (while directing the Secretary of State to separately study the feasibility of a *centralized* deletion mechanism, after the House-passed universal deletion portal was removed by the Senate), imposes affirmative due-diligence obligations on brokers before they disclose data to third parties, and adds a new, data-broker-specific breach notification requirement (45 days to consumers, 14 business days to the Attorney General).<!-- needs-human-review: deletion-right and AG-notice-deadline claims are corroborated by multiple independent secondary sources (Hunton, Fox Rothschild, Privacy Daily, Campaign for Vermont) after two review rounds, but WebFetch of the official Act 138 enacted PDF was unavailable in the researcher's session across both rounds; verify exact statutory section numbers against the primary source before treating as final. --> Most substantive obligations take effect January 1, 2027.<!-- verified -->

## Background [MEDIUM confidence] [VERIFIED]

Vermont was one of the first states to regulate data brokers directly, having enacted its original data broker registration law in 2018 under 9 V.S.A. chapter 62. That statute required entities meeting the definition of "data broker" to register annually with the Vermont Secretary of State, pay a modest $100 fee, and make certain disclosures about their data practices, but it predated the wave of comprehensive state privacy statutes (Virginia, Colorado, Connecticut, California's amendments, etc.) that followed beginning in 2021-2023 and left the broker-specific regime comparatively thin on consumer rights and enforcement teeth, according to [Hunton Andrews Kurth's analysis](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-cybersecurity-law-blog/vermont-enacts-significant-amendments-to-data-broker-legislation).

H.211 was introduced in the 2025-2026 biennium as "An act relating to data brokers and personal information" and moved through several drafts -- as introduced, as passed by the House, and as amended by the Senate -- before final passage by both chambers ([Vermont Legislature bill documents](https://legislature.vermont.gov/Documents/2026/Docs/BILLS/H-0211/H-0211%20As%20Passed%20by%20Both%20House%20and%20Senate%20Unofficial.pdf)). Notably, the House-passed version of H.211 would have created a state-run universal deletion portal allowing consumers to submit a single deletion request against all registered brokers at once; the Senate Committee on Economic Development, Housing and General Affairs removed that universal-portal mechanism before the bill's final passage on approximately May 28-29, 2026, replacing it with a directive for the Secretary of State to study the portal's feasibility ([Privacy Daily, "Vt. Data Broker Bill Goes to the Governor After Losing Right to Delete," 2026-05-29](https://privacy-daily.com/article/2026/05/29/vt-data-broker-bill-goes-to-the-governor-after-losing-right-to-delete-2605290056?BC=bc_6a1a6531e2be3); [Campaign for Vermont, "REVIEW: Protections for Personal Information (Act 138)"](https://www.campaignforvermont.org/act_138_2026)). The bill was delivered to the Governor on June 10, 2026, and signed June 16, 2026, becoming Act 138 ([Vermont Legislature bill status page](https://legislature.vermont.gov/bill/status/2026/H.211); [Recording Law summary](https://www.recordinglaw.com/news/vermont-data-broker-law-h211/)). It advanced in parallel with, but separately from, S.71, Vermont's comprehensive consumer data privacy and surveillance statute enacted around the same time -- the two should not be treated as the same legal instrument even though both were part of Vermont's 2026 privacy legislative package.

## Detailed Analysis [MEDIUM confidence] [NEEDS REVIEW]

**Fee and bonding increases.** Act 138 raises the annual data broker registration fee from $100 to $900 and, for the first time, requires registered data brokers to maintain a $20,000 surety bond ([Fox Rothschild client alert](https://dataprivacy.foxrothschild.com/2026/06/articles/general-privacy-data-security-news-developments/vermont-amends-its-data-broker-law-what-do-you-need-to-know/); [Hunton](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-cybersecurity-law-blog/vermont-enacts-significant-amendments-to-data-broker-legislation)).<!-- verified -->

**Steeper penalties.** Failure to register carries an administrative fine that increases from $50 per day (capped at $10,000 annually under the prior law) to $200 per day with no annual cap. The Act also creates new penalties for incomplete or inaccurate registration filings -- $1,000 per day for failing to correct omitted information, and a flat $25,000 penalty for submitting materially incorrect information -- which did not exist in the pre-amendment statute ([search-corroborated by Hunton](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-cybersecurity-law-blog/vermont-enacts-significant-amendments-to-data-broker-legislation) and [Sheppard Mullin](https://www.sheppard.com/insights/blogs/vermont-amends-data-broker-law)).<!-- verified: independently re-confirmed via WebSearch in round 2, no discrepancy found -->

**Broadened definition of "brokered personal information."** The Act replaces the prior statute's enumerated list of covered data elements with a broader, technology-neutral definition -- "any information, including derived data and unique identifiers, that is linked or reasonably linkable, alone or in combination with other information, to an identified or identifiable individual or to a device ... reasonably linkable to one or more identified or identifiable individuals in a household" -- bringing the data broker statute's scope closer to the "personal data" definitions used in comprehensive state privacy laws ([FKKS/Andrew Folks analysis](https://technologylaw.fkks.com/post/102n4qn/vermont-strengthens-data-broker-law)).

**New consumer deletion right -- corrected and expanded per reviewer verification.** The legislative history here matters and was the subject of conflicting secondary reporting. The House-passed version of H.211 would have created a state-run, *centralized* universal deletion portal letting a consumer submit one request that reached every registered broker. The Senate removed that centralized-portal mechanism before final passage (~May 28-29, 2026) and replaced it with a directive for the Secretary of State to study the feasibility of building such a centralized "accessible deletion mechanism," with an interim report due December 1, 2027, a final report (including any proposed legislation) due December 1, 2028, and a $50,000 FY2027 General Fund appropriation to hire a consultant to support the study ([Privacy Daily](https://privacy-daily.com/article/2026/05/29/vt-data-broker-bill-goes-to-the-governor-after-losing-right-to-delete-2605290056?BC=bc_6a1a6531e2be3); [Campaign for Vermont](https://www.campaignforvermont.org/act_138_2026)).

Independent of the centralized-portal question, multiple corroborating sources confirm that the enacted Act 138 *does* retain a **broker-by-broker** consumer deletion right: each registered data broker must maintain a dedicated webpage through which consumers can submit deletion requests directly to that broker, must generally process valid requests within 30 days, and must provide an appeals process for denied requests ([PrivacyLawMap](https://privacylawmap.com/blog/vermont-data-broker-delete-act-h211-2026); [CyberAdviser](https://www.cyberadviserblog.com/2026/06/vermont-just-changed-the-rules-for-data-brokers-is-your-business-ready/)). In other words, consumers gained a per-broker deletion right and must contact each broker individually (as originally reported), but did *not* gain the House-proposed single universal opt-out portal -- that mechanism is now merely under study, with no guarantee it will be enacted. This nuance was not clearly distinguished in the original version of this section, and the official enacted text (`Act 138 As Enacted`, cited below) should be checked directly to confirm the exact statutory placement of both provisions, since WebFetch access to the primary PDF remained unavailable in both the round 1 and round 2 research sessions.<!-- needs-human-review: round 2 independent WebSearch re-verification corroborates this reconciled account (per-broker deletion right retained; only the centralized/universal portal was removed and replaced by the feasibility study) across Hunton, Campaign for Vermont, and Privacy Daily -- consistent with the round 2 reviewer's own independent re-check, which reached the same conclusion but noted that some AI-generated search summaries remain internally inconsistent on this point. Because WebFetch of the official Act 138 enacted PDF remained unavailable in this reviewer session, this claim is held at MEDIUM confidence and still requires human verification against the official PDF before being treated as final. -->

**Due diligence before disclosure.** Act 138 imposes new affirmative obligations on data brokers before they disclose brokered personal information to a third party: the broker must identify the recipient and the recipient's purpose for the data and obtain certification that the data will not be used for any other purpose; if the broker has reasonable grounds to believe the data will be misused or used unlawfully, it must decline the transaction ([Hunton](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-cybersecurity-law-blog/vermont-enacts-significant-amendments-to-data-broker-legislation)). Separately, brokers must disclose annually whether they shared or sold consumer data with foreign actors, the federal government, other state/local governments, law enforcement (outside of subpoenas or court orders), or developers of generative AI systems ([search summary of bill provisions](https://legislature.vermont.gov/Documents/2026/Docs/BILLS/H-0211/H-0211%20As%20Passed%20by%20Both%20House%20and%20Senate%20Unofficial.pdf)).

**Breach notification -- citation corrected per round 2 reviewer feedback.** Act 138 creates a new, standalone **Data Broker Security Breach Notice Act** within 9 V.S.A. chapter 62, which is separate and distinct from Vermont's pre-existing general Security Breach Notice Act at [9 V.S.A. Section 2435](https://legislature.vermont.gov/statutes/section/09/062/02435). Under this new data-broker-specific regime, following a breach of brokered personal information, data brokers must notify affected consumers within 45 days of discovery and must notify the Vermont Attorney General within 14 business days, subject to a law-enforcement-requested delay and an exception where misuse is determined not to be reasonably possible ([Mondaq/Vermont Strengthens Data Broker Law](https://www.mondaq.com/unitedstates/data-protection/1809204/vermont-strengthens-data-broker-law); independent WebSearch corroboration in round 2 confirms Act 138 "adds the Data Broker Security Breach Notice Act," distinct from the technical amendments the same act makes to the pre-existing Security Breach Notice Act). The round 1 version of this report cited Section 2435 as the source of these figures; that citation has been removed because Section 2435 is the *general* breach law (its own 14-business-day notice runs to the Attorney General **or** the Department of Financial Regulation, "as applicable," for DFR-regulated entities) and is not confirmed to be the specific codification of the data-broker deletion/breach deadlines. The exact new section number for the Data Broker Security Breach Notice Act within chapter 62 could not be confirmed because the official Act 138 PDF remained inaccessible via WebFetch in both research rounds.<!-- needs-human-review: the 45-day consumer / 14-business-day AG figures are independently corroborated by WebSearch in round 2 across multiple sources, and those sources now confirm Act 138 creates a *separate* Data Broker Security Breach Notice Act rather than amending Section 2435 to cover data brokers -- correcting the round 1 citation, which imprecisely pointed to Section 2435. ## Impact Assessment [MEDIUM confidence] [NEEDS REVIEW]

Entities that meet Vermont's definition of "data broker" -- generally, businesses that knowingly collect and sell or license personal information of consumers with whom they do not have a direct relationship -- face materially higher compliance costs starting January 1, 2027: a 9x fee increase, a new $20,000 bond, new per-broker deletion-request infrastructure (dedicated webpage, 30-day processing, appeals process), new due-diligence documentation before any data disclosure, and new breach-notification obligations under the new Data Broker Security Breach Notice Act (45 days to consumers, 14 business days to the Attorney General), in addition to Vermont's pre-existing general Security Breach Notice Act at Section 2435. The steep new per-day and per-violation penalties for registration errors raise the stakes of getting registration filings wrong. Because the amended definition of "brokered personal information" is now broader and more closely tracks comprehensive privacy law definitions of personal data, some entities that were not previously in scope (or assumed they were not) should re-evaluate their registration obligations. Separately, the Secretary of State's forthcoming feasibility study on a centralized deletion portal (interim report due December 1, 2027; final report and any proposed legislation due December 1, 2028) could eventually add a universal opt-out obligation on top of the per-broker right -- entities should monitor that process even though it creates no near-term compliance obligation. The Vermont Attorney General is the presumptive enforcement authority, consistent with the pre-amendment statute's enforcement structure, though the Act's own text should be checked to confirm no new dedicated enforcement body was created.

## Action Items

- Confirm whether your organization meets Vermont's amended, broadened definition of "data broker" and "brokered personal information" -- entities previously outside scope under the narrower legacy definition may now be captured.
- Budget for the increased $900 annual registration fee and new $20,000 surety bond ahead of the January 1, 2027 effective date.
- Build a dedicated consumer-facing webpage for brokered-personal-information deletion requests, with a process to respond within 30 days and an appeals path for denied requests, consistent with the Act's broker-by-broker deletion right (note: this is distinct from the House-proposed universal portal, which was not enacted and is instead the subject of a Secretary of State feasibility study).
- Implement due-diligence procedures (recipient identification, purpose verification, certification) before disclosing brokered personal information to any third party.
- Update breach-response playbooks to include the new 45-day consumer notification and 14-business-day Attorney General notice requirements under the new Data Broker Security Breach Notice Act, keeping this distinct from your existing Section 2435 general breach-notice procedures.
- Verify current registration filings for accuracy given the new $1,000/day and $25,000 penalties for incomplete or inaccurate submissions.
- Distinguish this data-broker-specific compliance track from Vermont's separate S.71 comprehensive privacy law obligations; the two statutes have different scopes, definitions, and compliance dates.
- Monitor the Vermont Secretary of State's data broker registry page for updated filing instructions ahead of the 2027 effective date, and separately track the Secretary of State's centralized-deletion-mechanism feasibility study (interim report due December 1, 2027; final report due December 1, 2028) for any future universal opt-out obligation.
