# Colorado Amends Privacy Act with H.B. 1058, Adding New Protections for Biological and Neural Data

**Jurisdiction:** Colorado | **Category:** Privacy | **Date:** May 14, 2024

## Executive Summary [HIGH confidence]

On April 17, 2024, Colorado Governor Jared Polis signed [H.B. 24-1058](https://content.leg.colorado.gov/sites/default/files/2024a_1058_signed.pdf), making Colorado the first U.S. state to explicitly extend the protections of a comprehensive consumer privacy statute to biological and neural data. The law amends the [Colorado Privacy Act (CPA), C.R.S. § 6-1-1301 et seq.](http://leg.colorado.gov/bills/hb24-1058), by adding both "biological data" and "neural data" to the definition of "sensitive data" — categories that require affirmative opt-in consent before a controller may process them. The new provisions took effect August 6, 2024. The law targets consumer-facing neurotechnology products (such as sleep trackers, neurofeedback wearables, and other EEG-adjacent devices) while carving out data already regulated under HIPAA in clinical settings. Compliance officers and technology companies operating in Colorado — particularly those in health tech, wearables, and any industry deploying biometric or brain-monitoring products — should treat this as an active compliance obligation as of August 6, 2024.

## Background [HIGH confidence]

### The Colorado Privacy Act (CPA)

Colorado enacted the [CPA via Senate Bill 21-190](https://content.leg.colorado.gov/sites/default/files/2021a_190_signed.pdf), signed into law on July 7, 2021, with an effective date of July 1, 2023. The CPA is a Virginia-model omnibus consumer privacy statute that applies to entities conducting business in Colorado — or producing or delivering commercial products or services intentionally targeted to Colorado residents — that meet one of two thresholds:

- During a calendar year, controls or processes personal data of **100,000 or more** Colorado residents; or
- Both derives revenue (or receives discounts) from selling personal data **and** controls or processes the personal data of **25,000 or more** Colorado residents.

Notably, unlike the California Consumer Privacy Act (CCPA), the CPA contains no revenue-floor threshold. A company that meets either processing threshold is covered regardless of size. Enforcement authority rests with the [Colorado Attorney General and district attorneys](https://coag.gov/resources/colorado-privacy-act/); the statute contains no private right of action.

Under the original CPA, "sensitive data" — the highest-protection tier — already included racial or ethnic origin, religious beliefs, mental or physical health diagnoses, sexual orientation, citizenship/immigration status, genetic data, biometric data used to uniquely identify a natural person, personal data of a known child, and precise geolocation data. Controllers must obtain affirmative opt-in consent before processing sensitive data and must complete a documented data protection assessment for each processing activity.

### Rise of Consumer Neurotechnology

The legislative impetus for H.B. 1058 flows directly from the growing consumer market for neurotechnology devices. Brain-computer interfaces (BCIs), EEG headbands, consumer sleep trackers, and neurofeedback wearables — devices that measure electrical activity in the brain or peripheral nervous system — have moved out of clinical and research settings into everyday commerce. [Colorado itself recorded one of the first consumer-grade BCI surgeries in the country](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1123508), underscoring the jurisdiction's particular relevance to this industry.

Data generated by these devices is qualitatively distinct from conventional biometric identifiers: it can reveal neurological conditions, mood states, cognitive patterns, emotional reactions, and behavioral tendencies that are among the most intimate possible categories of personal information. As the [Colorado House Democrats noted when advancing the bill](https://www.cohousedems.com/news/first-in-the-nation-neural-data-protections-bill-advances), existing law left a gap between HIPAA (which covers clinically generated neural data) and the consumer device market (which it does not).

## Detailed Analysis [HIGH confidence]

### New Definitions Added to the CPA

H.B. 1058 inserts two new definitions into the CPA's definitional section, [C.R.S. § 6-1-1303](https://content.leg.colorado.gov/sites/default/files/2024a_1058_signed.pdf):

**"Biological data"** — Data that is generated by the technological processing, measurement, or analysis of an individual's biological, genetic, biochemical, physiological, or neural properties, compositions, or activities, or of an individual's body or bodily functions, which data is used or intended to be used, singly or in combination with other personal data, **for identification purposes**. Biological data explicitly includes neural data.

**"Neural data"** — Information that is generated by the measurement of the activity of an individual's central or peripheral nervous systems and that can be processed by or with the assistance of a device.

A critical structural distinction between the two definitions: the biological data definition requires that the data be "used or intended to be used for identification purposes." Neural data carries **no such qualifier**. The legislature expressly declared that because neural data contains distinctive information about the structure and functioning of individual brains and nervous systems, it **always** contains sensitive information that may link data to an identified or identifiable individual. This means neural data receives absolute sensitive-data treatment regardless of how it is used or whether the controller actually deploys it for identification.

Both categories are added to the CPA's enumerated list of "sensitive data," triggering the full sensitive-data compliance regime.

### Core Obligations for Controllers

Controllers that process biological or neural data of Colorado residents must now satisfy three principal obligations:

1. **Opt-in consent**: Before processing biological or neural data, a controller must obtain affirmative, freely given, specific, informed, and unambiguous consent from the consumer. Pre-checked boxes or inferred consent are insufficient. This is the same opt-in standard that already applies to all CPA sensitive data categories.

2. **Data protection assessment**: Controllers must conduct and document a data protection assessment for each activity involving the processing of biological or neural data. Under [C.R.S. § 6-1-1309](https://content.leg.colorado.gov/sites/default/files/2024a_1058_signed.pdf), these assessments must weigh the benefits of processing against risks to consumers and are subject to review by the Colorado AG upon request.

3. **Privacy notice update**: Controllers' publicly posted privacy notices must disclose whether the controller processes biological or neural data. Controllers that have not previously disclosed such processing are out of compliance until their notices are updated.

### Scope: What Is Covered and What Is Not

The [Hunton Andrews Kurth analysis of H.B. 1058](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-information-security-law/colorado-amends-privacy-act-with-h-b-1058-adding-new-protections-for-biological-and-neural-data) and [National Law Review coverage](https://natlawreview.com/article/colorado-amends-privacy-act-hb-1058-adding-new-protections-biological-and-neural) identify the following scope boundaries:

- **Covered**: Consumer wearables, neurofeedback apps, sleep trackers, EEG-based consumer devices, any technology that processes brain or nervous-system data outside of a clinical or HIPAA-regulated context.
- **Excluded**: Data that qualifies as protected health information (PHI) under HIPAA, because PHI is already excluded from the CPA's coverage at the entity level. Clinically generated EEG and EMG data processed by covered entities and their business associates is thus unaffected.
- **Threshold applicability**: All CPA-level thresholds remain unchanged. Entities that do not meet the 100,000-consumer processing threshold (or the 25,000/revenue threshold) are not subject to the CPA, and by extension, the H.B. 1058 amendments.

### Legislative Process

The bill passed both chambers with near-unanimous margins. In the House, only three members voted against the bill on third reading. In the Senate, the bill passed with **zero no votes** on third reading. Governor Polis signed it on April 17, 2024 — the same day as final passage. The bill was co-sponsored by Representatives Kipp and Soper, with a bipartisan list of additional sponsors including Representatives Amabile, Bacon, and Bird, reflecting broad cross-partisan consensus on the underlying issue.

### Enforcement Mechanism

The CPA's enforcement regime is unchanged. The [Colorado Attorney General and district attorneys](https://coag.gov/resources/colorado-privacy-act/) hold exclusive enforcement authority. There is no private right of action. Under the CPA, the AG may issue a notice of violation; controllers have a 60-day cure period (which is not a permanent safe harbor; cure provisions may sunset). Civil penalties can reach $20,000 per violation, with violations aggregated per consumer per incident.

## Impact Assessment [MEDIUM confidence]

### Industries Affected

The [Law Week Colorado coverage](https://www.lawweekcolorado.com/article/new-colorado-law-protects-consumer-biological-and-neural-data/) and [legal commentary from Alston & Bird](https://www.alston.com/en/insights/publications/2024/04/key-issues-raised-by-colorado) identify the broadest impact falling on:

- **Consumer health and wellness technology**: Makers of EEG-based sleep trackers, neurofeedback bands, stress-monitoring wearables, and similar products that capture physiological signals.
- **Neurotechnology startups**: Companies developing early-stage BCIs, cognitive enhancement devices, and disability-assistive neural interfaces for the consumer market.
- **Fitness and biometric platforms**: Platforms that aggregate biological signals (heart rate variability, skin conductance, electromyography) alongside neural data may find that the combination crosses into "biological data" territory.
- **Healthcare-adjacent technology**: Digital therapeutics and mental health apps that use sensor data to infer mental states may qualify as collecting biological data even without explicit EEG hardware.

The [Sum and Substance legal commentary](https://tsscolorado.com/new-colorado-privacy-laws-to-impact-broad-swath-of-companies/) notes that the law will "impact a broad swath of companies" in Colorado, including firms not primarily in the neurotechnology space whose products incidentally collect biological signals.

### Nationwide Ripple Effects

Colorado's H.B. 1058 has directly catalyzed legislative activity in other states. According to [Morrison Foerster's March 2025 survey of state neural data legislation](https://www.mofo.com/resources/insights/250317-more-states-propose-privacy-laws-safeguarding-neural-data), at least six other states — Connecticut, Illinois, Massachusetts, Minnesota, Montana, and Vermont — had pending neural data privacy legislation as of early 2025. Illinois's existing [BIPA](https://natlawreview.com/article/colorado-amends-privacy-act-hb-1058-adding-new-protections-biological-and-neural) framework has been proposed for amendment to explicitly cover brainwave patterns. California enacted its own neural data provisions in 2024. At the federal level, U.S. senators introduced the [MIND Act](https://www.dwt.com/blogs/privacy--security-law-blog/2025/10/senate-mind-act-neural-data-ftc-regulation) in late 2025 to study and recommend national standards for neural data protection.

### Definitional Challenges

The [FPF "Goldilocks Problem" analysis](https://fpf.org/blog/the-neural-data-goldilocks-problem-defining-neural-data-in-u-s-state-privacy-laws/) identifies an emerging tension in how states define neural data: a definition too narrow risks excluding inference-based data derived from neural signals; a definition too broad risks sweeping in ordinary heart rate or galvanic skin response data. Colorado's approach — anchoring neural data to measurement of central and peripheral nervous system activity processed by a device — is considered by many analysts the broadest definitional net cast so far. Companies selling multi-modal wellness devices should assess whether any sensor combination crosses into this definition.

## Action Items

- Audit all data collection pipelines to identify whether any sensor, API, or data feed captures information about neural activity, biological compositions, or physiological properties that could constitute "biological data" or "neural data" under the amended CPA definitions.
- If biological or neural data is collected from Colorado residents: (a) implement affirmative opt-in consent flows before data collection begins; (b) update privacy notices to disclose the category of data processed; (c) conduct and document a formal data protection assessment for each such processing activity.
- Ensure data protection assessments are complete and retrievable — the Colorado AG may request them as part of any enforcement inquiry. Assessments should document the purposes of processing, the risks to consumers, and the countervailing business benefits.
- Companies relying on a HIPAA exemption should verify whether the specific data at issue is actually processed as PHI by a HIPAA-covered entity or business associate; consumer-facing applications operated separately from a covered entity do not inherit the HIPAA carve-out.
- Monitor sister-state and federal legislative developments: given the pace of state neural data bills in 2025, additional patchwork obligations are likely. Build consent and assessment infrastructure to a standard that can accommodate multiple state regimes.
- If the company does not yet meet the CPA's applicability thresholds, track growth in Colorado resident data processing — crossing the 100,000-consumer threshold or the 25,000/revenue threshold triggers the full CPA regime inclusive of H.B. 1058's amendments.
