# Southeastern Privacy Laws Taking Shape: Alabama, Georgia, Florida, and Tennessee

**Jurisdiction:** Alabama, Georgia, Florida, Tennessee | **Category:** Privacy | **Date:** 2026-06-02

## Executive Summary [MEDIUM confidence]

Four southeastern states — Alabama, Georgia, Florida, and Tennessee — are shaping a distinct regional privacy compliance landscape that diverges meaningfully from both the California model and the broader Virginia-template majority. Alabama signed the [Alabama Personal Data Protection Act (APDPA)](https://alison.legislature.state.al.us/files/pdf/SearchableInstruments/2026RS/HB351-enr.pdf) on April 16, 2026 (effective May 1, 2027), becoming the 21st state with comprehensive consumer data privacy legislation. Georgia followed with the [Georgia Consumer Privacy Protection Act (SB 111)](https://legiscan.com/GA/bill/SB111/2025), signed May 11, 2026 and effective July 1, 2026. Florida's [Digital Bill of Rights (FDBR)](https://www.flsenate.gov/Session/Bill/2023/262) has been in force since July 1, 2024, but applies only to a narrow set of very large technology companies meeting multiple criteria. Tennessee's [Information Protection Act (TIPA)](https://www.tn.gov/attorneygeneral/news/2025/4/30/pr25-25.html) took effect July 1, 2025. Together, these four states create overlapping but non-uniform compliance obligations that businesses operating across the Southeast must navigate individually.

## Background [HIGH confidence]

Prior to 2024, none of the four southeastern states under review had enacted a comprehensive consumer data privacy statute. The region lagged behind Virginia (enacted 2021), Colorado and Connecticut (enacted 2022), and California's CCPA/CPRA framework.

Florida moved first in the region. Governor Ron DeSantis signed [SB 262](https://www.flsenate.gov/Session/Bill/2023/262), the Florida Digital Bill of Rights, in June 2023, effective July 1, 2024. The FDBR was immediately distinctive: unlike the Virginia-model statutes that had proliferated elsewhere, Florida's general consumer privacy provisions apply only to large online platforms that exceed $1 billion in annual global revenue AND satisfy at least one of three additional criteria — deriving 50% or more of global revenue from digital advertising, operating an app store with at least 250,000 apps, or operating a consumer smart speaker with an integrated virtual assistant. This multi-factor test effectively targets a handful of the largest technology companies rather than the broad mid-market. The FDBR also includes separate and more broadly applicable protections for children, prohibiting targeted advertising to any person under 18 and mandating parental controls on companion chatbot platforms.

Tennessee enacted [TIPA](https://publications.tnsosfiles.com/acts/113/pub/pc0051.pdf) in May 2023, which took effect July 1, 2025. TIPA more closely resembles the Virginia-template model, but with applicability requirements that include both a high consumer threshold (175,000 consumers, or 25,000 consumers where more than 50% of revenue comes from data sales) AND a $25 million annual gross revenue floor — making it one of the least expansive laws by coverage. TIPA does not require recognition of universal opt-out signals such as Global Privacy Control (GPC), aligning it with Virginia's cautious approach to automated opt-out mechanisms.

Alabama's HB 351, the APDPA, was passed unanimously by both chambers of the Alabama Legislature on April 7, 2026, and signed by Governor Kay Ivey on April 16, 2026. [Hunton Andrews Kurth](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-cybersecurity-law-blog/alabama-becomes-21st-state-with-comprehensive-consumer-privacy-law) and [WilmerHale](https://www.wilmerhale.com/en/insights/blogs/wilmerhale-privacy-and-cybersecurity-law/20260422-alabama-enacts-nations-twenty-first-state-comprehensive-privacy-law) both confirmed Alabama as the 21st state to join the comprehensive privacy patchwork.

Georgia's SB 111 was the most recent enactment: signed by Governor Brian Kemp on May 11, 2026, effective July 1, 2026, making it the first southeastern law to go live in the current calendar year alongside still-pending compliance cycles for Alabama's newer enactment.

## Detailed Analysis [MEDIUM confidence]

### Alabama Personal Data Protection Act (APDPA) — Effective May 1, 2027

The APDPA is structured on the Virginia Consumer Data Protection Act (VCDPA) controller-processor model, but with several meaningful variations.

**Applicability:** The APDPA applies to entities that conduct business in Alabama or produce products/services targeted to Alabama residents and that, during a calendar year, either (a) process personal data of more than **25,000 Alabama consumers** (excluding data processed solely to complete payment transactions), or (b) derive more than **25% of gross revenue from the sale of personal data**. As [IAPP](https://iapp.org/news/a/alabama-set-to-add-variation-to-us-state-privacy-patchwork) notes, the 25,000-consumer threshold is the lowest numeric floor among comprehensive state privacy laws — lower than Colorado (100,000), Connecticut (100,000), Virginia (100,000), Delaware (35,000), and Maryland (35,000). This subjects a broader cross-section of mid-market businesses to the statute than peer state laws.

**Consumer Rights:** Alabama consumers obtain standard VCDPA-model rights: access, correction, deletion, portability, and opt-out rights for targeted advertising, data sales, and profiling in furtherance of legal or similarly significant decisions. Affirmative consent is required for processing sensitive data categories (biometric data, precise geolocation, and personal data of known children). Consent must be revoked within 45 days.

**Notable Differences from Peers:**
- No requirement to recognize **Global Privacy Control (GPC)** or universal opt-out signals, in contrast to Colorado, Connecticut, and California.
- A **permanent 45-day cure period** for every violation — controllers can cure violations indefinitely without losing cure protection, unlike states where the cure period sunsets or is discretionary.
- Broad entity-level exemptions including for businesses with fewer than **500 employees**, qualifying this as one of the most business-friendly state laws.
- No private right of action; exclusive enforcement authority rests with the Alabama Attorney General.
- Civil penalties up to **$15,000 per violation** per the [Osano analysis](https://www.osano.com/articles/alabama-personal-data-protection-act-apdpa).

**Official text:** [Alabama HB 351 enrolled](https://alison.legislature.state.al.us/files/pdf/SearchableInstruments/2026RS/HB351-enr.pdf) (Alabama Legislature).

### Georgia Consumer Privacy Protection Act (SB 111) — Effective July 1, 2026

Georgia's SB 111, signed by Governor Kemp on May 11, 2026, makes Georgia the newest entrant to the state privacy landscape, with a law that already took effect as of July 1, 2026. The statute follows the Virginia model and was evaluated by [EPIC](https://epic.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/GA-one-pager-privacy-law-1.pdf) as comparatively weak among state privacy laws, scoring 6/10.

**Applicability:** SB 111 applies to entities in Georgia or targeting Georgia consumers that: (a) process personal data of at least **175,000 consumers per calendar year**, or (b) process personal data of at least **25,000 consumers per calendar year AND derive more than 50% of gross revenue from the sale of personal data**, AND in either case exceed **$25 million in annual gross revenue**. The revenue floor narrows the law's coverage relative to Alabama's thresholds.

**Consumer Rights:** Georgia consumers receive rights to access, correct, delete, and opt out of data sales and targeted advertising, consistent with the Virginia template.

**Enforcement:** The Georgia Attorney General has exclusive enforcement authority. A mandatory **60-day cure period** precedes any enforcement action, and there is no private right of action. The [ACLU of Georgia](https://www.acluga.org/press-releases/report-georgias-consumer-privacy-bill-gets-failing-grade-0/) and [EPIC](https://epic.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/GA-one-pager-privacy-law-1.pdf) criticized the 60-day cure period, high thresholds, and lack of a private right of action as undermining the law's protective value for consumers.

**Official text:** [Georgia SB 111 enrolled](https://legiscan.com/GA/text/SB111/id/3413377) (LegiScan).

### Florida Digital Bill of Rights (FDBR) — In Effect Since July 1, 2024

Florida's FDBR is structurally unlike any other comprehensive state privacy law in the Southeast. Its general consumer privacy chapter applies exclusively to entities meeting a two-part test: they must (1) exceed **$1 billion in global annual revenue**, AND (2) satisfy at least one of three additional criteria: derive 50% or more of global gross annual revenue from the sale of online advertisements; operate an app store or digital distribution platform offering at least **250,000 different software applications**; or operate a consumer smart speaker and voice command service with an integrated virtual assistant connected to a cloud computing service. Per [Davis Wright Tremaine](https://www.dwt.com/blogs/privacy--security-law-blog/2023/06/florida-digital-bill-of-rights-data-privacy) and [White & Case](https://www.whitecase.com/insight-alert/florida-enacts-digital-bill-rights-joining-growing-privacy-landscape), this multi-factor applicability test means only a handful of the largest technology companies worldwide fall within scope of the comprehensive provisions.

Where the FDBR applies broadly is to **children's privacy and social media**: it prohibits targeted advertising to any person under 18, requires treble damages for violations involving minors ($50,000 per violation multiplied by three), and imposes parental control requirements on companion chatbot and LLM platforms. The [Perkins Coie](https://perkinscoie.com/insights/blog/florida-enacts-digital-bill-rights-combining-narrowly-applicable-comprehensive) analysis notes this dual-tier structure is intentional — Florida prioritized children's protections over broad consumer coverage.

The FDBR does not include a GPC or universal opt-out requirement. Enforcement rests with the Florida Attorney General, with a discretionary 45-day cure period and civil penalties up to $50,000 per violation. Florida's 2026 session also saw introduction of [SB 482](https://www.troutmanprivacy.com/2026/02/key-takeaways-on-proposed-state-ai-and-privacy-laws-january-2026/), the Florida Artificial Intelligence Bill of Rights, which passed the Senate Commerce Committee 10-0 but had not been enacted as of this writing.

### Tennessee Information Protection Act (TIPA) — In Effect Since July 1, 2025

TIPA's applicability requires meeting both a consumer threshold AND a revenue floor. A business is covered if it does business in Tennessee or targets Tennessee residents, earns more than **$25 million in annual gross revenue**, AND either controls or processes personal data of at least **175,000 Tennessee consumers** during a calendar year, or controls or processes personal data of at least **25,000 Tennessee consumers** while deriving more than 50% of gross annual revenue from the sale of personal data. The combined revenue-and-consumer-count requirement makes TIPA one of the least expansive state privacy laws by coverage among the Virginia-template states.

TIPA provides standard access, correction, deletion, portability, and opt-out rights for targeted advertising and data sales. Like Florida and Alabama, TIPA does not require recognition of GPC signals. As [Osano](https://www.osano.com/articles/tennessee-information-protection-act-tipa) and [Usercentrics](https://usercentrics.com/knowledge-hub/tennessee-information-protection-act-tipa/) confirm, this aligns TIPA with the Virginia-template majority that treats opt-out as a right requiring affirmative exercise, not an automated signal that triggers automatic compliance obligations.

As of June 2026, no formal TIPA enforcement actions have been publicly announced; the Tennessee AG's office has focused on guidance. The [Tennessee Attorney General issued compliance guidance](https://www.tn.gov/attorneygeneral/news/2025/4/30/pr25-25.html) in April 2025 covering definitions, exemptions, and core controller obligations.

## Impact Assessment [MEDIUM confidence]

### Affected Entities and Industries

The southeastern patchwork creates four distinct compliance zones with overlapping but non-identical obligations. Businesses need to assess each state's thresholds independently:

| State | Primary Threshold | Revenue Floor | GPC Required | Cure Period | Effective |
|-------|-----------------|---------------|-------------|------------|-----------|
| Alabama | 25,000 consumers | None | No | 45 days (permanent) | May 1, 2027 |
| Georgia | 175,000 or 25,000 + 50% data revenue | $25M | No | 60 days | July 1, 2026 |
| Florida (broad) | $1B revenue + one of three criteria (digital ads, app store, smart speaker) | $1B | No | 45 days (discretionary) | July 1, 2024 |
| Florida (children) | Any platform likely to be accessed by children | N/A | No | 45 days (discretionary) | July 1, 2024 |
| Tennessee | 175,000 consumers (or 25,000 + 50% data revenue) | $25M | No | 60 days | July 1, 2025 |

Mid-market companies operating regionally — particularly those in retail, healthcare, financial services, and hospitality with southeastern footprints — face the widest exposure. Alabama's low 25,000-consumer threshold means companies not covered by Georgia's or Tennessee's higher thresholds may nonetheless be subject to Alabama's statute. Florida's FDBR broadly covers only very large technology platforms for general consumer provisions (and requires meeting one of three additional operational criteria beyond the $1B revenue floor), but its children's privacy rules apply across a broader set of businesses with online services likely accessed by minors.

### Compliance Requirements and Timelines

For businesses not already compliant with peer VCDPA-model laws (Colorado, Connecticut, Virginia), each southeastern law requires:

1. **Data mapping and inventory** to identify personal data flows touching each state's consumers.
2. **Privacy notice updates** disclosing categories of data collected and sold, consumer rights, and contact information.
3. **Opt-out mechanisms** for targeted advertising and data sales (opt-out rights in all four states; no universal signal recognition required).
4. **Sensitive data consent workflows** for Alabama (affirmative consent required before processing biometric data, precise geolocation, children's data).
5. **Data processing agreements** with processors consistent with each state's controller-processor framework.
6. **Children's data protocols** for any services potentially accessed by users under 18 — particularly critical in Florida where treble damages apply.

The most pressing deadline is Georgia's July 1, 2026 effective date, which has already passed. Tennessee has been in effect since July 2025. Florida has been in effect since July 2024. Alabama's May 1, 2027 date provides companies approximately 11 months of additional lead time.

### Enforcement Outlook

None of the four states has yet filed a major public enforcement action under their respective comprehensive statutes. All four laws vest exclusive enforcement with the state attorney general and include no private right of action, distinguishing the southeast from California (CPPA enforcement) and Illinois (BIPA private litigation). The absence of a private right of action reduces class action litigation risk but does not eliminate regulatory risk — particularly in Alabama where the low consumer threshold means more businesses are covered than anticipated.

The [Seyfarth Shaw](https://www.jdsupra.com/legalnews/southeastern-privacy-laws-taking-shape-6041158/) analysis published on JDSupra in May 2026 notes that businesses with existing Virginia, Colorado, or Connecticut compliance programs will be largely able to extend those programs to cover the Southeast, but should conduct gap analyses focused specifically on: (a) Alabama's lower threshold; (b) Georgia's higher revenue floor and 60-day cure period; (c) Florida's children's protections and narrow platform-specific scope requiring $1B revenue plus one of three operational criteria; and (d) Tennessee's $25M revenue floor and enforcement posture.

## Action Items

- **Immediate (Georgia, July 2026):** Assess whether your business meets Georgia's thresholds ($25M revenue + 175,000 consumers, or 25,000 consumers with 50%+ data sales revenue). If so, the statute is already in effect and compliance is required now.
- **Tennessee audit:** Confirm whether Tennessee's dual applicability requirements are met — both the $25M annual gross revenue floor AND the 175,000-consumer threshold (or 25,000 with 50%+ data-sale revenue). Existing Virginia-model compliance programs must be verified against TIPA specifically on sensitive data consent and data processing agreements.
- **Florida children's review:** For any service likely to be accessed by persons under 18, audit targeted advertising practices and consent mechanisms — Florida's treble damages provision makes children's data violations a high-risk exposure category regardless of whether your company meets the FDBR's general consumer provisions criteria.
- **Alabama preparation (by March 2027):** Begin APDPA gap analysis by Q3 2026, focusing on the low 25,000-consumer threshold, the absence of GPC recognition requirements, and the broad employee-headcount exemption (fewer than 500 employees).
- **Privacy notice harmonization:** Update privacy notices to satisfy each state's disclosure requirements simultaneously; Alabama and Georgia both require disclosure of data categories, processing purposes, and consumer rights contact information.
- **Monitor Georgia enforcement:** The Georgia AG's office will likely issue compliance guidance before initiating enforcement actions under SB 111; monitor for guidance documents analogous to Tennessee's April 2025 AG press release.
- **Florida AI Bill Watch:** Track Florida SB 482 (AI Bill of Rights) through the next legislative session; if enacted, it would add consumer-facing AI obligations specifically relevant to chatbot and LLM deployments.
