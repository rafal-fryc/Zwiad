# Minnesota Enacts the Consumer Data Privacy Act (MCDPA / HF 4757): Comprehensive Analysis

**Jurisdiction:** Minnesota | **Category:** Privacy | **Date:** 2024-05-30

## Executive Summary [HIGH confidence]

On May 24, 2024, Minnesota Governor Tim Walz signed [HF 4757](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/laws/2024/0/Session+Law/Chapter/121/), enacting the Minnesota Consumer Data Privacy Act (MCDPA) and codified as [Minnesota Statutes, chapter 325M](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/325M). Minnesota became the 18th state to enact comprehensive data privacy legislation, with the law taking effect on July 31, 2025. The MCDPA broadly follows the Virginia-model framework adopted by many states but introduces several distinctive provisions not found in peer statutes — most notably a mandatory data inventory requirement (the first of its kind in any US state privacy law), a consumer right to question profiling decisions with detailed explanatory information, and a right to obtain a list of specific third parties to whom personal data was disclosed or sold. Enforcement rests exclusively with the Minnesota Attorney General, who may impose civil penalties of up to $7,500 per violation. Businesses processing personal data of 100,000 or more Minnesota consumers, or deriving more than 25% of gross revenue from personal data sales while processing data of 25,000 or more consumers, are covered by the Act.

## Background [HIGH confidence]

Minnesota's path to enacting comprehensive data privacy legislation spanned multiple legislative sessions. The 93rd Legislature considered several privacy bills, including [SF 2915](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/bills/93/2023/0/SF/2915/versions/latest/) introduced in 2023, before settling on the framework ultimately enacted as HF 4757 in 2024.

By the time Minnesota acted, a substantial majority of states had already adopted or were actively advancing comprehensive consumer privacy statutes. The Virginia Consumer Data Protection Act (effective January 2023) and the Connecticut Data Privacy Act (effective July 2023) served as the primary templates for the wave of state legislation in 2023 and 2024. Minnesota's legislature took this established framework and deliberately added provisions that address perceived gaps — particularly around profiling transparency and data supply chain visibility through third-party disclosure lists.

The Minnesota legislature passed the bill in its final session in May 2024. Governor Walz signed HF 4757 on May 24, 2024. The Minnesota Attorney General's office was appropriated $941,000 in fiscal year 2026 and $701,000 in fiscal year 2027 to support MCDPA enforcement, per [Minnesota Laws 2024, Chapter 121, appropriations section](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/laws/2024/0/121/laws.2.37.0) — a signal that the office intends active enforcement from day one of the law's effectiveness.

The bill used proposed section numbers in chapter 325O during the legislative process but was ultimately codified as [chapter 325M (§§ 325M.10–325M.21)](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/325M) upon enactment.

## Applicability and Thresholds [HIGH confidence]

The MCDPA applies to any person or legal entity that conducts business in Minnesota or produces products or services targeted to Minnesota residents and, during a calendar year, either:

- Controls or processes personal data of **100,000 or more** consumers; or
- Derives more than **25% of gross revenue from selling personal data** and controls or processes personal data of **25,000 or more** consumers.

The dual-threshold structure mirrors the Virginia model and is notably broader than some peer laws. Unlike some statutes, there is no explicit revenue-floor threshold independent of data-sale revenue.

### Entity-Level Exemptions

The following categories of entities are wholly exempt from the MCDPA:

- **Government entities** (state and local)
- **Small businesses** as defined by the U.S. Small Business Administration — the MCDPA is among the Virginia-model states that incorporate the SBA's small business definition as a full entity-level exemption (following Texas in 2023 and Nebraska in early 2024), though small businesses retain a specific prohibition against selling sensitive data without prior consent
- **Financial institutions** subject to the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act (banks, credit unions)
- **Insurance companies** regulated under state law
- **Air carriers** subject to the federal Airline Deregulation Act
- **Federally recognized Native American tribes**
- **Nonprofits** established to detect and prevent insurance fraud (a narrower nonprofit carve-out than some states)

### Data-Level Exemptions

Specific categories of data remain outside the MCDPA's reach regardless of the entity processing them:

- Health information regulated under **HIPAA**
- Consumer report data under the **Fair Credit Reporting Act**
- Financial data under **Gramm-Leach-Bliley**
- Driver data under the **Driver's Privacy Protection Act**
- Student data under **FERPA**
- Farm credit data under the **Farm Credit Act**
- Data under the **Minnesota Insurance Fair Information Reporting Act**

## Consumer Rights [HIGH confidence]

The MCDPA establishes a comprehensive set of consumer rights largely modeled on the Virginia framework, with significant enhancements:

### Standard Rights (shared with most Virginia-model states)

- **Right to access**: Confirm whether a controller processes personal data about the consumer and obtain a copy of that data
- **Right to correct**: Rectify inaccuracies in personal data
- **Right to delete**: Require deletion of personal data provided by or obtained about the consumer
- **Right to data portability**: Obtain personal data in a portable, readily usable format
- **Right to opt out**: Opt out of processing for (1) targeted advertising, (2) sale of personal data, and (3) profiling in furtherance of decisions with legal or similarly significant effects

### Distinctive Minnesota Rights

**Right to a list of specific third parties**: Consumers may request a list of the specific third parties to whom the controller has disclosed the consumer's personal data. If the controller does not maintain data organized by individual consumer, it may instead provide a general list of all third parties to whom it has disclosed any consumer personal data. This right is shared only with Oregon among state comprehensive privacy laws and represents a meaningful advance in supply-chain transparency.

**Right to question profiling decisions**: Where personal data is processed for profiling that produces legal or similarly significant effects, consumers have the right to:
1. Be informed of the reason the profiling resulted in the decision rendered
2. Be informed, where feasible, of what actions the consumer could have taken to secure a different decision and what actions they might take to do so in the future
3. Review the personal data used in the profiling
4. Have inaccurate data corrected and the profiling decision reevaluated

This three-part profiling challenge right goes substantially beyond the simple opt-out rights in most peer statutes and creates operational demands — controllers must be able to explain not just what data was used, but the causal role of that data and potential remediation paths.

### Response Timelines

Controllers must respond to authenticated consumer requests within **45 days**, with a single 45-day extension available when reasonably necessary. Responses must be provided free of charge, up to twice per year per consumer. Requests that are manifestly unfounded, excessive, or repetitive may be declined with a written explanation.

## Controller and Processor Obligations [HIGH confidence]

### Data Inventory (First-of-Kind Requirement)

The MCDPA is the first US state privacy law to explicitly require controllers to maintain a **data inventory**. Specifically, a controller must establish, implement, and maintain reasonable administrative, technical, and physical data security practices and must maintain an inventory of the data subject to those practices. This transforms data mapping from a compliance best practice into a legal obligation under Minnesota law.

### Privacy Notice

Controllers must provide a reasonably accessible, clear, and meaningful privacy notice that includes:
- Categories of personal data processed
- Purposes for processing
- How and where consumers may exercise their rights, including how to appeal a controller's decision
- Categories of personal data shared with third parties
- Categories of third parties with whom personal data is shared

### Data Protection Impact Assessments (DPIAs)

Controllers must conduct, document, and maintain DPIAs for processing activities that present a heightened risk, including:
- Processing sensitive data
- Processing for targeted advertising
- Selling personal data
- Profiling that presents a reasonably foreseeable risk of unfair or deceptive treatment, unlawful disparate impact, intrusion upon solitude, financial, physical, or reputational harm, or other substantial harms

### Sensitive Data

The MCDPA defines sensitive data as personal data revealing:
- Racial or ethnic origin
- Religious beliefs
- Mental or physical health diagnosis
- Sexual orientation
- Citizenship or immigration status
- Genetic data
- Biometric data processed for the purpose of unique identification
- Personal data collected from a known child
- Precise geolocation data

Processing sensitive data requires obtaining **prior consent** from the consumer. Sensitive data cannot be sold by any covered entity — including otherwise-exempt small businesses — without prior consumer consent.

### Controller-Processor Contracts

Controllers and processors must execute a binding contract governing data processing that specifies:
- Types of personal data subject to processing
- Duration of processing
- Rights and obligations of both parties

The processor contract must require that the processor: (1) maintain confidentiality; (2) delete or return all personal data upon controller's request; (3) allow the controller to object to any subcontractor; and (4) impose equivalent obligations on any subcontractor.

### No Rulemaking Authority

Unlike California's CPPA, the Minnesota Attorney General was not granted rulemaking authority under the MCDPA. This means the statute's text — as enacted — governs compliance without the prospect of supplemental agency regulations expanding its scope.

## Enforcement [HIGH confidence]

The MCDPA is enforced exclusively by the **Minnesota Attorney General**. There is no private right of action for consumers.

**Penalties**: Up to **$7,500 per violation**, plus injunctive relief and the AG's litigation expenses.

**Cure period**: Until **January 31, 2026**, the AG must issue a written warning letter identifying the specific provisions allegedly violated and provide the controller or processor 30 days to cure. If the AG determines violations have not been cured within 30 days, enforcement may proceed. After January 31, 2026, the cure period requirement expires and the AG may bring enforcement actions without prior warning.

The AG received a dedicated enforcement appropriation of $941,000 in FY2026 and $701,000 in FY2027, per [Minnesota Laws 2024, Chapter 121, appropriations section](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/laws/2024/0/121/laws.2.37.0), indicating legislative intent for active enforcement from the law's first day of effect.

## Comparative Analysis [MEDIUM confidence]

### Position Among State Privacy Laws

As the 18th state to enact comprehensive privacy legislation (per multiple law firm analyses at signing; the finding's original description of "19th" may reflect a slightly different count depending on the counting methodology), Minnesota's MCDPA occupies a mid-field position in the national landscape:

| Feature | MCDPA (MN) | VCDPA (VA) | CTDPA (CT) | MODPA (MD) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Applicability threshold | 100K consumers or 25K + 25% revenue from data sales | 100K or 25K + 50% revenue | 100K or 25K + 25% revenue | 35K consumers |
| Small business exemption | Full (SBA definition) | Full | Full | No SBA exemption |
| Sensitive data consent | Required | Required | Required | Required (opt-in) |
| Data inventory mandate | **Yes (unique)** | No | No | No |
| Right to question profiling | **Yes (detailed)** | Opt-out only | Opt-out only | Opt-out only |
| Right to list third parties | **Yes (shared with OR)** | No | No | No |
| Private right of action | No | No | No | No |
| AG cure period | Until Jan. 31, 2026 | Permanent (60 days) | Expired | 60 days |
| Rulemaking authority | No | No | No | No |

### Notable Distinctions

Minnesota's data inventory mandate creates a concrete, auditable obligation that no other Virginia-model statute contains. Combined with the right to question profiling decisions, the MCDPA places Minnesota among the more demanding comprehensive privacy regimes — less stringent than Maryland's MODPA (which imposes data minimization obligations and shorter timelines) but substantially more demanding than Virginia or Connecticut in these specific areas.

The absence of rulemaking authority means compliance obligations are fixed at enactment, providing relative certainty compared to states like California where CPPA rulemaking continues to expand requirements.

## Impact Assessment [HIGH confidence]

### Affected Entities

The MCDPA's dual threshold — 100,000 consumers or 25,000 consumers with 25%+ data-sale revenue — places a wide range of businesses within scope. Minnesota's population of approximately 5.7 million means that businesses with statewide or regional consumer reach in the Midwest may readily cross the 100,000-consumer threshold.

Key sectors facing significant compliance obligations include:
- **Technology platforms and data brokers**: Full scope, including mandatory data inventory, DPIA requirements, and third-party disclosure lists
- **Retailers and e-commerce**: Targeted advertising and profiling obligations, cookie consent requirements
- **Healthcare-adjacent entities**: HIPAA-exempt covered entities are carved out, but business associates and non-covered health tech companies face sensitive data obligations
- **Financial services**: GLBA-covered entities are exempt at the entity level; others (fintech, neobanks) face full obligations
- **Postsecondary educational institutions regulated by Minnesota OHE**: Delayed compliance deadline of **July 31, 2029**

### Compliance Requirements and Timelines

- **July 31, 2025**: Law in effect; all covered controllers and processors must be compliant
- **July 31, 2029**: Postsecondary educational institutions (MN OHE-regulated) compliance deadline
- **January 31, 2026**: AG cure period expires; enforcement without prior notice becomes available

### Key Compliance Steps

1. Conduct applicability analysis under the dual threshold
2. Build and maintain a data inventory (mandatory under § 325M.17)
3. Update privacy notices to include third-party disclosure categories and profiling disclosures
4. Implement consumer request intake and response workflows with 45-day response obligations
5. Build profiling explanation workflows capable of satisfying the right-to-question-profiling requirements
6. Execute DPIA assessments for all high-risk processing activities
7. Negotiate and update processor agreements to include MCDPA-required provisions
8. Implement consent mechanisms for sensitive data processing
9. Ensure small business vendors do not sell sensitive data without prior consent

## Action Items

- Determine whether your organization meets either MCDPA applicability threshold (100,000+ MN consumers processed, or 25,000+ with 25%+ revenue from data sales) — the law applies to out-of-state businesses targeting Minnesota residents
- Build a data inventory now; this is a statutory obligation under Minn. Stat. ch. 325M, not merely a best practice
- Update consumer-facing privacy notices to include the categories of third parties receiving data and explain the profiling opt-out and challenge rights
- Design a consumer request intake process that can handle the novel right to question profiling decisions, including the ability to explain the basis for automated decisions and advise on potential remediation
- Update processor data processing agreements to include all MCDPA-required provisions before July 31, 2025
- Conduct DPIAs for targeted advertising, sensitive data processing, data sales, and high-risk profiling workflows
- Monitor the Attorney General's enforcement posture; the cure period expires January 31, 2026, after which the AG may bring actions without advance notice
- Track companion developments in other states with similar third-party-list rights (Oregon) to understand how that right is being operationalized in practice
