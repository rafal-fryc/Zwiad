# Connecticut Data Privacy Act: New Opt-Out Rights Effective January 1, 2025

**Jurisdiction:** Connecticut | **Category:** Privacy | **Date:** December 30, 2024

## Executive Summary [HIGH confidence]

On December 30, 2024, Connecticut Attorney General William Tong issued a [formal advisory](https://portal.ct.gov/ag/press-releases/2024-press-releases/tong-advises-connecticut-consumers-and-businesses-of-opt-out-rights-and-requirements) to consumers and businesses announcing that new opt-out rights under the Connecticut Data Privacy Act (CTDPA) took effect on January 1, 2025. The centerpiece of the advisory is a mandatory requirement for all covered controllers to honor universal opt-out preference signals (OOPS) -- browser-based signals such as the Global Privacy Control (GPC) -- communicating a Connecticut resident's intent to opt out of targeted advertising and the sale of personal data. Effective the same date, the CTDPA's mandatory 60-day cure period for violations expired; the AG now has full discretion over whether to offer businesses an opportunity to cure before initiating enforcement. These changes represent the most operationally significant CTDPA milestone since the law's initial effective date of July 1, 2023, and the AG has signaled active technical testing of compliance.

## Background [HIGH confidence]

Connecticut enacted the Connecticut Data Privacy Act (Conn. Gen. Stat. § 42-515 et seq.) on May 10, 2022, when Governor Ned Lamont signed [Senate Bill 6: An Act Concerning Personal Data Privacy and Online Monitoring (Public Act No. 22-15)](https://www.cga.ct.gov/2022/ACT/PA/PDF/2022PA-00015-R00SB-00006-PA.PDF). Connecticut became the fifth U.S. state to enact a comprehensive consumer privacy law. The CTDPA's core consumer-rights provisions took effect July 1, 2023, covering businesses that process the personal data of at least 100,000 Connecticut residents per year, or of 25,000 or more residents where data sales generate at least 25% of gross revenue.

The CTDPA has since been amended twice. In June 2023, the legislature passed [Senate Bill 3 (Public Act No. 23-56)](https://www.cga.ct.gov/2023/act/Pa/pdf/2023PA-00056-R00SB-00003-PA.PDF), amending the CTDPA to add consumer health data protections and children's online safety requirements. Those amendments took effect in stages: consumer health data obligations became effective July 1, 2023, while children's online protection provisions took effect October 1, 2024.

Additional amendments became operative on January 1, 2025. These included: (1) the OOPS mandate requiring controllers to recognize and honor universal opt-out signals; and (2) the expiration of the mandatory 60-day cure period (in effect from the law's original effective date through December 31, 2024). From January 1, 2025 forward, the AG may exercise discretion in deciding whether to grant a cure opportunity.

The AG's [December 30, 2024 advisory](https://portal.ct.gov/ag/press-releases/2024-press-releases/tong-advises-connecticut-consumers-and-businesses-of-opt-out-rights-and-requirements) was issued the day before these requirements became effective, providing both a consumer education resource and a clear signal to businesses of enforcement intent.

## Detailed Analysis [HIGH confidence]

### Opt-Out Preference Signal (OOPS) Mandate

Effective January 1, 2025, the CTDPA requires all covered controllers to recognize and honor universal opt-out preference signals sent by Connecticut residents. The [CT AG's advisory](https://portal.ct.gov/ag/press-releases/2024-press-releases/tong-advises-connecticut-consumers-and-businesses-of-opt-out-rights-and-requirements) describes these signals as being sent "from a platform or mechanism that enables a controller to accurately determine whether the consumer is a Connecticut resident." Consumers activating GPC through a compatible browser or browser extension are the primary use case.

When a controller receives an OOPS, it must treat the signal as the consumer's request to opt out of both targeted advertising and the sale of personal data. This is a broader automatic effect than the OOPS requirement in some other states: the signal triggers opt-out across all personal data the controller processes, not just data collected through tracking technologies. Controllers may not require consumers to affirmatively opt out again via a separate in-product mechanism if an OOPS is already present.

The advisory explicitly addresses a potential business-side complication: if a consumer's OOPS conflicts with their previously stated privacy preference or their voluntary participation in a loyalty rewards or discount program, the controller must still comply with the OOPS. However, the controller may notify the consumer of the conflict and ask them to confirm their choice -- with a clear explanation that confirmation would affect their prior privacy setting or loyalty program participation.

### Pre-Existing Opt-Out Rights (Unchanged)

The OOPS mandate supplements, but does not replace, prior CTDPA opt-out rights. Connecticut consumers have held the following individual opt-out rights since the law's July 1, 2023 effective date:

- **Targeted advertising:** The right to opt out of the processing of personal data for the purpose of targeted advertising.
- **Sale of personal data:** The right to opt out of the sale of personal data to third parties.
- **Profiling:** The right to opt out of profiling in furtherance of solely automated decisions that produce legal or similarly significant effects concerning the consumer.

Controllers were already required to offer an easily accessible opt-out link on their website for targeted advertising and data sales. The January 2025 OOPS mandate adds the obligation to also honor browser-based signals automatically, in addition to any controller-hosted opt-out mechanism.

### Cure Period Expiration and Enforcement Posture

From July 1, 2023 through December 31, 2024, the CTDPA required the AG to provide businesses with a mandatory 60-day cure period before initiating enforcement action. Effective January 1, 2025, that mandatory right to cure expired. The AG now has discretion on whether to extend a cure opportunity, weighing up to seven statutory factors:

1. The number of violations
2. The size and complexity of the business
3. The nature and extent of the processing activities
4. The likelihood and severity of potential public injury
5. The safety of persons or property
6. Whether the violation was caused by human or technical error
7. The sensitivity of the data involved

Violations of the CTDPA are enforceable as violations of the [Connecticut Unfair Trade Practices Act (CUTPA)](https://portal.ct.gov/ag/sections/privacy/the-connecticut-data-privacy-act), carrying civil penalties of up to $5,000 per willful violation. Only the AG may bring enforcement actions; there is no private right of action under the CTDPA.

### AG Technical Testing

The AG's office has stated it is actively working with technologists to test whether businesses are properly detecting and effectuating opt-out preference signals, specifically GPC. The [2025 CTDPA enforcement report](https://portal.ct.gov/-/media/ag/press_releases/2025/updated-enforcement-report-pursuant-to-connecticut-data-privacy-act-conn-gen-stat--42515-et-seq.pdf) confirmed that by the end of 2025, the office had issued dozens of notices of violation and warning letters, finalized multiple data breach settlements, and concluded its first formal enforcement action under the CTDPA. The report specifically called out cookie banners that make opting out more difficult than opting in as a compliance concern.

### Joint Multi-State Enforcement Sweep

In 2025, Connecticut joined California and Colorado in a [joint investigative privacy sweep](https://portal.ct.gov/ag/press-releases/2025-press-releases/connecticut-california-and-colorado-announce-joint-investigative-privacy-sweep), signaling coordination among state attorneys general on privacy enforcement. Connecticut's OOPS enforcement priorities align with California's Global Privacy Control enforcement under the CCPA and Colorado's similar signal recognition requirement.

## Impact Assessment [MEDIUM confidence]

### Affected Entities

The OOPS mandate and cure-period expiration affect all controllers subject to the CTDPA -- businesses that, in a calendar year, control or process the personal data of at least 100,000 Connecticut consumers, or of at least 25,000 consumers where personal data sales constitute more than 25% of gross revenue. This encompasses a broad range of online businesses, advertising technology companies, data brokers, and retailers with Connecticut customer bases.

Businesses in advertising-heavy industries (retail, media, adtech) face the highest operational exposure because OOPS signals directly curtail their ability to process consumer data for behavioral targeting. Data brokers whose revenue model depends on selling personal data must update their systems to detect and honor incoming signals.

### Compliance Requirements

To comply with the OOPS requirement, controllers should:

- Implement technical capability to detect GPC signals on their websites and apps (typically via server-side HTTP header inspection or JavaScript-based detection).
- Route GPC signals from Connecticut residents to opt-out processing pipelines covering both targeted advertising and personal data sales.
- Audit existing cookie consent platforms to confirm GPC detection is active and correctly categorized as a dual opt-out from both targeted advertising and data sale.
- Review any conflict-resolution logic for consumers enrolled in loyalty programs who also send OOPS signals, to ensure the signal overrides prior preferences and that consumers are notified of the conflict.

With the cure period now discretionary rather than mandatory, businesses can no longer rely on a guaranteed 60-day correction window. The practical effect is that a business violating the CTDPA after January 1, 2025 could face immediate enforcement notice without a right to fix the violation first.

### Enforcement Outlook [MEDIUM confidence]

The AG's stated testing of GPC compliance and the 2025 multi-state sweep indicate that OOPS enforcement is a live priority, not a theoretical risk. The [2025 CTDPA enforcement report](https://portal.ct.gov/-/media/ag/press_releases/2025/updated-enforcement-report-pursuant-to-connecticut-data-privacy-act-conn-gen-stat--42515-et-seq.pdf) identified dark-pattern cookie banners and failure to honor opt-out requests as top complaint categories. Businesses that have not yet implemented GPC detection should treat this as high-priority given the documented enforcement activity.

## Action Items

- **Immediate (if not yet completed):** Verify that websites and apps detect GPC signals from Connecticut-resident visitors and route them to opt-out processing for both targeted advertising and personal data sales.
- **Audit cookie consent platform:** Confirm that any CMP (Consent Management Platform) in use properly interprets GPC as an opt-out signal rather than requiring a separate in-platform action.
- **Review loyalty program conflict handling:** Implement consumer notification workflows for cases where a GPC signal conflicts with a consumer's loyalty program enrollment, per the AG advisory guidance.
- **Update privacy notice:** Ensure the privacy notice accurately discloses the categories of personal data sold, categories of third-party recipients, and how consumers can exercise opt-out rights including via OOPS.
- **Prepare for discretionary cure:** Update internal incident response protocols to account for the fact that the AG may (but is not required to) grant a cure period; do not assume a 60-day window.
- **Monitor AG enforcement activity:** Track further enforcement actions and any guidance issued by the Connecticut OAG regarding OOPS technical compliance standards.
- **Watch SB 1295 (2025):** A further round of CTDPA amendments signed by Governor Lamont on June 24, 2025 (with most provisions effective July 1, 2026) broadens the profiling opt-out right and lowers the coverage threshold; businesses should begin gap assessment.
