# Vermont Data Privacy Act (H.121): Passed Legislature, Vetoed by Governor -- A Near-Miss for the Nation's Strongest State Privacy Law

**Jurisdiction:** Vermont | **Category:** Privacy | **Date:** 2024-05-22

## Executive Summary [HIGH confidence]

Vermont's legislature passed H.121, "An act relating to enhancing consumer privacy and the age-appropriate design code," on May 10, 2024, making Vermont what would have been the 18th state to enact comprehensive data privacy legislation. The bill included several provisions that went beyond any existing state law, including a private right of action for consumers, a general prohibition on selling sensitive data even with consent, mandatory recognition of the Global Privacy Control (GPC) opt-out signal, and an Age-Appropriate Design Code for minors. Governor Phil Scott vetoed the bill on June 13, 2024, citing the private right of action as an undue burden on Vermont businesses. The House voted 128-17 to override the veto, but the Senate voted 14-15 to sustain it -- falling six votes short of the two-thirds supermajority required. H.121 is dead. As of 2025-2026, the Vermont legislature continues to pursue comprehensive privacy legislation in successor bills, but the core dispute over a private right of action remains unresolved.

## Background [HIGH confidence]

Vermont has a long history in data broker regulation. The state enacted one of the nation's first data broker registration statutes in 2018 (9 V.S.A. § 2430 et seq.), requiring data brokers to register annually with the Vermont Attorney General. This made Vermont an early outlier in the state privacy landscape, though the 2018 law did not grant consumers individual rights or impose broad controller obligations.

Starting in 2022, the Vermont legislature began work on comprehensive consumer privacy legislation modeled loosely on the Virginia Consumer Data Protection Act (VCDPA) but incorporating stronger protections from Connecticut's model and novel provisions without precedent in other state laws. H.121 was introduced in the 2023-2024 legislative biennium and underwent extensive committee hearings, testimony from industry groups, privacy advocates, and the Attorney General's office before passing both chambers in May 2024.

Vermont's bill emerged at the same moment that Maryland's Online Data Privacy Act (MODPA), widely considered the nation's most stringent enacted comprehensive privacy law, was signed by Maryland's governor. H.121 would have rivaled or exceeded MODPA in several respects.

## Detailed Analysis [HIGH confidence]

### Applicability Thresholds

H.121 applied to controllers and processors that, during a calendar year, either (a) control or process the personal data of at least 100,000 consumers, or (b) control or process the personal data of at least 25,000 consumers and derive more than 25 percent of gross revenue from the sale of personal data. The bill included a tiered timeline: the lower threshold of 25,000 consumers for certain larger processors would apply from July 1, 2025, with a further reduced threshold of 6,250 consumers phasing in on July 1, 2027. Notably, several provisions -- including those governing consumer health data, minors' data, and data broker obligations -- were not subject to these applicability thresholds, meaning they would apply to any covered person conducting business in Vermont or targeting Vermont residents regardless of data processing volume.

### Consumer Rights

The bill granted consumers the following rights, consistent with the Connecticut and Virginia models:
- **Right to confirm and access** personal data being processed
- **Right to correct** inaccuracies in personal data
- **Right to delete** personal data (subject to exceptions)
- **Right to data portability** in a portable and readily usable format
- **Right to opt out** of targeted advertising, the sale of personal data, and profiling for automated decisions with legal or similarly significant effects
- **Right to obtain a list of third parties** to which the controller has disclosed their personal data -- a provision that went beyond most state laws

Controllers would have had 45 days to respond to consumer requests, with a potential 45-day extension when reasonably necessary, and were required to provide a means to appeal denied requests.

### Novel and Stronger-Than-Average Provisions

Several provisions of H.121 set it apart from the majority of state comprehensive privacy laws:

**1. Prohibition on Sale of Sensitive Data**
Unlike most state laws that require only consent before selling sensitive data, H.121 would have prohibited the sale of sensitive data entirely -- even with consumer consent. Sensitive data categories included racial or ethnic origin, religious beliefs, mental or physical health diagnosis, sexual orientation or gender identity, citizenship or immigration status, genetic or biometric data, precise geolocation, and financial information beyond general salary or income. This outright prohibition aligned H.121 more closely with the Washington My Health My Data Act's approach to health data.

**2. Mandatory Global Privacy Control (GPC) Recognition**
H.121 required controllers to honor opt-out preferences expressed through universal opt-out mechanisms such as the [Global Privacy Control](https://globalprivacycontrol.org/), a browser-based signal for automated opt-out. The California Privacy Protection Agency has taken similar positions under the CCPA regulations, but mandatory GPC recognition was not universal in state law as of 2024.

**3. Data Minimization**
Similar to Maryland's MODPA, H.121 would have imposed a general data minimization obligation, prohibiting controllers from processing personal data beyond what was reasonably necessary and proportionate to the stated purpose. This stands in contrast to most state laws (Virginia, Connecticut, Colorado) that permit processing if adequate notice is given, regardless of proportionality.

**4. Consumer Health Data Protections**
A dedicated subchapter addressed consumer health data, mirroring Washington's My Health My Data Act (MHMDA). The provisions included prohibitions on disclosing health data to third parties, restrictions on geofencing within 1,850 feet of health care facilities to target consumers for health-related purposes, and confidentiality obligations for entities that receive consumer health data.

**5. Private Right of Action**
The most controversial feature of H.121 was a limited but real [private right of action](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-cybersecurity-law-blog/vermont-legislature-passes-state-privacy-bill-with-right-to-sue-for-consumers) allowing consumers to sue data brokers and "large data holders" (entities processing records of at least 100,000 Vermont residents per year) for violations of: (a) prohibitions on processing sensitive data without consent; (b) violations of COPPA with respect to children's sensitive data; and (c) confidentiality obligations for consumer health data. The private right of action would have taken effect January 1, 2027 and would have sunset on January 1, 2029 -- a deliberate compromise to allow evaluation of its practical effects. Only Washington's My Health My Data Act, at the state level, had enacted a comparable private right of action in the privacy space as of 2024.

**6. Age-Appropriate Design Code**
H.121 incorporated Vermont's version of the [Age-Appropriate Design Code](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-information-security-law/vermont-enacts-age-appropriate-design-code), commonly called the Kids Code, which was added during final legislative negotiations. Controllers offering online services, products, or features to minors would have been required to: (a) apply default privacy settings at the highest available level; (b) not collect or share a minor's personal data beyond what is necessary to provide the requested service; and (c) meet a "minimum duty of care" preventing their design from causing reasonably foreseeable emotional distress, compulsive use, or identity-based discrimination in minors.

### Data Broker Provisions

Vermont's existing data broker registration statute (9 V.S.A. § 2430) defined "data broker" as any business that knowingly collects and sells or licenses the brokered personal information of consumers with whom the business has no direct relationship. H.121 would have expanded on existing data broker obligations, requiring annual security assessments, limiting data collection to specific purposes, and -- critically -- applying the private right of action to data broker violations involving sensitive data.

### Enforcement

Primary enforcement authority rested with the Vermont Attorney General, who would have had: (a) authority to issue civil investigative demands; (b) power to seek civil penalties; (c) a 60-day cure period before initiating most enforcement actions; and (d) rulemaking authority for certain provisions. Unlike many state attorney general offices, Vermont's AG publicly and vocally supported the bill and urged the Senate to override the governor's veto.

## The Veto and its Aftermath [HIGH confidence]

Governor Phil Scott vetoed H.121 on June 13, 2024, in [a formal veto letter](https://governor.vermont.gov/sites/scott/files/documents/H.121%20-%20Veto%20Letter.pdf) that focused on three main objections:

1. **Private Right of Action**: Scott wrote that allowing consumers to sue companies directly "would make Vermont a national outlier, and more hostile than any other state to many businesses and non-profits." He acknowledged the concern was primarily about fear and uncertainty rather than a large volume of anticipated suits.

2. **Accumulated Business Burden**: The governor argued that Vermont businesses were already absorbing new costs from a payroll tax, a Clean Heat Standard, and property tax increases, making the additional compliance burden of H.121 ill-timed.

3. **Expansive Definitions**: Scott cited "expansive definitions" that he believed would sweep in businesses and nonprofits beyond the bill's intended targets.

The governor explicitly recommended that Vermont instead adopt Connecticut's data privacy framework, which New Hampshire had also adopted, citing the value of regional consistency.

Attorney General Charity Clark [issued a sharp rebuke](https://ago.vermont.gov/blog/2024/06/14/statement-attorney-general-clark-governors-veto-h121-vermont-data-privacy-act) of the veto, calling it "extremely disappointing" and arguing that without a private right of action, enforcement costs would fall entirely on taxpayers through the AG's office rather than allowing aggrieved consumers to seek their own remedies.

At the June 17, 2024 veto session, the House voted 128-17 to override -- far exceeding the two-thirds threshold. The Senate voted 14-15 in favor of override, falling six votes short of the 20 needed. The veto was [officially sustained](https://epic.org/vermont-senate-sustains-governors-veto-of-vermont-privacy-act/), and H.121 did not become law.

### 2025-2026 Legislative Successor Efforts

Despite H.121's failure, the Vermont legislature has continued to pursue comprehensive privacy legislation in the 2025-2026 biennium. Bills including S.71, H.208, and H.211 have been introduced, but as of early 2026, the core dispute over a private right of action remains unresolved and no successor bill has passed. The [Vermont Age-Appropriate Design Code](https://www.zwillgen.com/privacy/vermont-signs-age-appropriate-design-code-into-law/) (S.69), a standalone kids code bill, was separately signed into law by Governor Scott on June 12, 2025, with an effective date of January 1, 2027 -- the governor having been willing to sign the kids code provisions when separated from the private right of action and broader data privacy framework.

## Impact Assessment [MEDIUM confidence]

### Who Would Have Been Affected

Had H.121 become law, the primary compliance burden would have fallen on:
- Large technology companies operating nationally, given the 100,000-consumer threshold
- Data brokers, who faced both existing registration obligations and new restrictions
- Healthcare-adjacent businesses, which would have faced consumer health data obligations regardless of processing volume
- Online platforms and services targeting Vermont minors

Vermont's small population (approximately 650,000 residents) means the 100,000-consumer threshold would have captured primarily large national actors rather than local small businesses. However, Governor Scott's concern centered on the potential chilling effect of private litigation regardless of actual threshold applicability.

### Significance for the National Privacy Landscape

H.121's passage and subsequent veto illustrates the central tension in state-level comprehensive privacy legislation: whether to include a private right of action. As of 2024, no comprehensive state privacy law enacted in the Virginia-model tradition included a private right of action -- even Illinois's BIPA (a sector-specific biometric law, not a comprehensive privacy statute) has faced significant litigation pressure from its private right of action. Vermont came closest to breaking that pattern.

The governor's suggestion that Vermont adopt Connecticut's law reflects a broader trend toward interstate convergence around a middle-ground model: comprehensive consumer rights and controller obligations, but enforcement exclusively through state attorneys general. The private right of action debate remains one of the most consequential unresolved questions in US privacy law.

### Partial Victory: Kids Code

Vermont did achieve a partial win when the Age-Appropriate Design Code (S.69) was enacted separately in June 2025. This demonstrates that the substantive child safety provisions of H.121 commanded enough support -- including from the governor -- to survive as standalone legislation, even as the comprehensive adult privacy framework remained stalled.

## Action Items

- Vermont-headquartered businesses and national businesses with Vermont customers should continue to monitor the 2025-2026 legislative session; H.211 and related bills remain active.
- Businesses in industries with significant Vermont minors users should prepare for compliance with the Vermont Age-Appropriate Design Code (S.69), effective January 1, 2027.
- Data brokers operating in Vermont must maintain compliance with Vermont's existing data broker registration statute (9 V.S.A. § 2430), which remains in full force regardless of H.121's fate.
- Compliance teams building state-by-state privacy programs should note that Vermont currently has no enacted comprehensive adult consumer privacy law; CCPA-modeled programs do not apply here.
- Organizations tracking the private right of action debate should use Vermont's experience as a case study: strong legislative support (128-17 House override) was insufficient to overcome organized business opposition in the Senate.
