# Five States Pass Comprehensive Privacy Laws in Q2 2024: Kentucky, Nebraska, Rhode Island, Maryland, and Minnesota Comparative Analysis

**Jurisdiction:** Kentucky | Nebraska | Rhode Island | Maryland | Minnesota | **Category:** Privacy | **Date:** 2024-07-26

## Executive Summary [HIGH confidence]

Between April 1 and July 1, 2024, five US states — Kentucky, Nebraska, Rhode Island, Maryland, and Minnesota — enacted comprehensive consumer data privacy statutes, bringing the total number of states with omnibus privacy laws to approximately nineteen. Each law grants consumers core rights to access, correct, delete, and port personal data, and to opt out of targeted advertising and personal data sales. All five assign exclusive enforcement authority to the state Attorney General with no private right of action. However, the laws diverge significantly in their applicability thresholds, data minimization standards, sensitive data treatment, cure periods, and the extent to which they depart from the "Virginia model" that has dominated state privacy lawmaking since 2021. Maryland's [Online Data Privacy Act (MODPA)](https://mgaleg.maryland.gov/2024RS/bills/sb/sb0541e.pdf) stands apart as the most consumer-protective of the five and arguably the strictest state privacy law in the country, drawing from the proposed federal American Data Privacy and Protection Act (ADPPA) rather than the VCDPA template. Kentucky and Nebraska closely follow the Virginia model, while Minnesota and Rhode Island fall at different points on the spectrum between these poles.

## Background [HIGH confidence]

### The Virginia-Model Wave

Virginia's [Consumer Data Protection Act (VCDPA)](https://law.lis.virginia.gov/vacode/title59.1/chapter53/), enacted in March 2021, established the dominant template for state privacy legislation: threshold-based applicability (100,000 consumers processed, or 25,000 consumers with 50% revenue from data sales), a standard set of consumer rights (access, correction, deletion, portability, opt-out), opt-in consent for sensitive data processing, data protection assessment requirements, a 30-day cure period, and exclusive AG enforcement. By early 2024, states including Colorado, Connecticut, Utah, Iowa, Indiana, Tennessee, Texas, Montana, Oregon, Delaware, New Hampshire, and New Jersey had enacted variations of this framework.

The five Q2 2024 laws reflect both the maturation of the Virginia model and growing experimentation. Kentucky adopted the VCDPA framework nearly verbatim. Nebraska borrowed from the Texas model on applicability. Minnesota layered several novel consumer-protective provisions onto the Virginia baseline. Maryland broke sharply from the template, producing a law that legal commentators have described as a potential "third model" for state privacy legislation alongside the California and Virginia frameworks.

## Detailed Analysis [HIGH confidence]

### Applicability Thresholds

One of the most consequential differences among the five laws is the threshold for coverage.

**Virginia-model standard (Kentucky, Minnesota):** Both [KCDPA](https://www.akingump.com/en/insights/blogs/ag-data-dive/kentucky-data-protection-act-what-businesses-need-to-know) and [MCDPA](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/325M) apply to entities that control or process personal data of at least 100,000 consumers annually, or at least 25,000 consumers while deriving more than 50% (Kentucky) or 25% (Minnesota) of gross revenue from personal data sales. Kentucky's revenue trigger is the same 50% figure used in Virginia; Minnesota lowered it to 25%, modestly broadening coverage.

**Texas/Nebraska model (Nebraska):** The [NDPA](https://nebraskalegislature.gov/bills/view_bill.php?DocumentID=54904) jettisons numeric thresholds entirely. It applies to any entity conducting business in Nebraska or targeting Nebraska residents that processes personal data and is not a "small business" as defined by the federal Small Business Act. This broad-net approach — first used in the Texas Data Privacy and Security Act — means that even relatively modest processors (under 100,000 consumers) must comply if they are not federally classified as small businesses. However, small businesses that sell sensitive data must still obtain opt-in consumer consent.

**Lower numeric threshold (Maryland, Rhode Island):** [MODPA](https://mgaleg.maryland.gov/2024RS/bills/sb/sb0541e.pdf) covers entities that process the personal data of at least 35,000 consumers annually (excluding payment transaction data) or derive 20% or more of gross revenue from personal data sales while processing data of at least 10,000 consumers. [RIDTPPA](https://webserver.rilegislature.gov/BillText/BillText24/HouseText24/H7787Aaa.pdf) uses the same 35,000-consumer / 10,000-consumer-with-20%-revenue structure. Maryland's lower threshold compared to the VCDPA's 100,000-consumer threshold significantly widens the pool of covered businesses, making both Maryland and Rhode Island more expansive in reach than the Virginia model.

### Data Minimization

Data minimization is the area of greatest divergence across the five laws.

**Purpose-limited minimization (Kentucky, Minnesota, Rhode Island, Nebraska):** Four of the five laws follow the Virginia-model standard requiring that personal data collection be "reasonably necessary and proportionate to the purposes disclosed to consumers." While this requires purpose disclosure, it gives controllers significant latitude: once a purpose is declared, collection consistent with that purpose is generally permissible. [KCDPA](https://apps.legislature.ky.gov/recorddocuments/bill/24RS/hb15/bill.pdf), [MCDPA](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/325M), [RIDTPPA](https://webserver.rilegislature.gov/BillText/BillText24/HouseText24/H7787Aaa.pdf), and [NDPA](https://nebraskalegislature.gov/FloorDocs/108/PDF/Slip/LB1074.pdf) all operate within this framework.

**Product/service-tethered minimization (Maryland):** [MODPA](https://mgaleg.maryland.gov/2024RS/bills/sb/sb0541e.pdf) imposes a fundamentally stricter standard. Collection must be "reasonably necessary and proportionate to provide or maintain a specific product or service requested by the consumer." As [Davis Wright Tremaine observed](https://www.dwt.com/blogs/privacy--security-law-blog/2024/05/maryland-online-data-privacy-act-signed), this tethers permissible collection directly to what the consumer requests — not what the business declares as a general purpose. Controllers cannot process personal data beyond what is directly required for the specific product or service in use. For sensitive data, the standard is stricter still: processing is permitted only when "strictly necessary" to provide or maintain the requested service. Maryland's [cyberlawmonitor.com](https://www.cyberlawmonitor.com/2024/08/26/marylands-new-approach-to-data-minimization-creates-unique-compliance-issues/) noted this "new paradigm" creates unique compliance challenges for ad-supported business models, cross-context behavioral advertising, and data analytics operations that exceed the strict service-delivery purpose.

### Sensitive Data and the Blanket Sales Prohibition

All five laws require opt-in consent before processing consumers' sensitive personal data (including racial or ethnic origin, health data, biometric data, precise geolocation, sexual orientation, immigration status, and mental health data). However, Maryland goes further in two respects.

First, [MODPA](https://mgaleg.maryland.gov/2024RS/bills/sb/sb0541e.pdf) prohibits the **sale** of sensitive data outright — no form of consent can authorize a controller to sell sensitive personal data. This is the first such blanket sensitive-data sales ban in any US state privacy law. Second, MODPA's definition of "sensitive data" is broader than the VCDPA template, encompassing additional categories such as transgender and nonbinary status and citizenship/immigration status.

Nebraska's [NDPA](https://nebraskalegislature.gov/FloorDocs/108/PDF/Slip/LB1074.pdf) similarly contains a prohibition: small businesses that would otherwise be exempt from the law's general requirements must still obtain opt-in consent before selling sensitive data. This creates a floor of protection that extends to a class of businesses otherwise entirely outside the law.

### Universal Opt-Out Mechanisms

An increasingly contested issue in state privacy law is whether controllers must honor browser-based or device-level universal opt-out signals such as the Global Privacy Control (GPC).

**No requirement (Kentucky):** The [KCDPA](https://apps.legislature.ky.gov/recorddocuments/bill/24RS/hb15/bill.pdf) is one of the few recently enacted comprehensive privacy laws that does not require controllers to honor universal opt-out mechanisms. Consumers must exercise opt-out rights through controller-provided mechanisms, distinguishing Kentucky from California, Colorado, and Connecticut, which mandate GPC recognition.

**Required (Maryland, Minnesota):** Both [MODPA](https://mgaleg.maryland.gov/2024RS/bills/sb/sb0541e.pdf) and [MCDPA](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/325M) require controllers to honor universal opt-out mechanisms for opt-out of personal data sales and targeted advertising, consistent with the direction of most post-2023 state laws.

**Not required (Nebraska, Rhode Island):** [NDPA](https://nebraskalegislature.gov/FloorDocs/108/PDF/Slip/LB1074.pdf) and [RIDTPPA](https://webserver.rilegislature.gov/BillText/BillText24/HouseText24/H7787Aaa.pdf) do not mandate universal opt-out recognition under the Virginia-model framework each generally follows.

### Consumer Rights: Notable Innovations

**Minnesota's profiling transparency right:** The [MCDPA](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/325M.14) provides consumers with a unique right to question the results of profiling that produces "legal effects or similarly significant effects" concerning a consumer — such as decisions affecting access to employment, housing, education, insurance, or essential services. Consumers may demand the reason for a profiling decision, learn what corrective actions could yield a different decision, review the personal data used in profiling, and require reevaluation if the data was inaccurate. This right is broader than the opt-out-of-profiling rights found in the Virginia model, and extends to AI-assisted profiling regardless of whether artificial intelligence is the decision mechanism.

**Minnesota's mandatory data inventory:** Unlike any other US state privacy law at the time of enactment, the [MCDPA](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/325M) requires controllers to maintain a data inventory — a documented record of all personal data they process. This GDPR-adjacent requirement imposes baseline records management obligations beyond what most Virginia-model states require.

**Minnesota's third-party disclosure list:** The MCDPA grants consumers the right to obtain a list of specific third parties to whom their personal data was disclosed or sold, rather than merely the categories of third parties disclosed in a privacy notice.

**Rhode Island's universal notice requirement:** The [RIDTPPA](https://webserver.rilegislature.gov/BillText/BillText24/HouseText24/H7787Aaa.pdf) extends a privacy notice requirement to all commercial websites operating in Rhode Island regardless of whether they meet the general consumer threshold. Any commercial website collecting personal data from Rhode Island residents must post a privacy notice identifying data categories collected, third-party sharing practices, and how consumers can exercise rights. This universal notice obligation applies even to businesses that do not reach the 35,000-consumer threshold for the law's full requirements.

### Cure Periods and Enforcement

Cure periods — the window before which the AG may seek penalties — vary substantially among the five laws.

| State | Cure Period | Notes |
|-------|-------------|-------|
| Kentucky | 30 days (permanent) | [KCDPA](https://www.dwt.com/blogs/privacy--security-law-blog/2024/06/kentucky-consumer-data-protection-act-signed) cure period does not sunset; most business-friendly of the five |
| Nebraska | 30 days (permanent) | [NDPA](https://nebraskalegislature.gov/FloorDocs/108/PDF/Slip/LB1074.pdf) cure period does not sunset |
| Maryland | Discretionary (sunset April 1, 2027) | [MODPA](https://mgaleg.maryland.gov/2024RS/bills/sb/sb0541e.pdf): AG has discretion to provide a cure period before April 1, 2027, but no mandatory cure after that date |
| Minnesota | 30 days (until January 31, 2026) | [MCDPA](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/325M): mandatory cure period sunsets; after January 31, 2026, cure is at AG's discretion |
| Rhode Island | None | [RIDTPPA](https://webserver.rilegislature.gov/BillText/BillText24/HouseText24/H7787Aaa.pdf) provides no cure period; violations immediately subject to penalties |

### Civil Penalties

All five laws cap civil penalties at $7,500 per violation for willful violations, consistent with the Virginia model. Rhode Island's penalty structure has a notable wrinkle: general violations carry up to $10,000 per violation, but intentional disclosures of personal data specifically carry penalties of $100–$500 per disclosure — potentially lighter at the high end of targeted enforcement.

**Kentucky, Nebraska, Minnesota:** Up to $7,500 per violation (consistent across the Virginia-model tier).

**Maryland:** Up to $10,000 per violation, with each affected consumer potentially constituting a separate violation.

**Rhode Island:** Up to $10,000 per violation for general violations; $100–$500 per intentional disclosure.

## Impact Assessment [MEDIUM confidence]

### Compliance Timeline Priority

Nebraska's NDPA, which took effect January 1, 2025, is the most immediately pressing compliance obligation of the five for organizations that have not already assessed exposure. Businesses operating in Nebraska or offering products/services to Nebraska residents that are not SBA-classified small businesses should have completed initial assessments by this date.

Minnesota's MCDPA (effective July 31, 2025) and Maryland's MODPA (effective October 1, 2025, with enforcement from April 1, 2026) represent the next priority tier. Both require more novel compliance work — Minnesota's data inventory and profiling transparency requirements and Maryland's strict data minimization regime require substantive program changes, not just policy updates.

Kentucky and Rhode Island both take effect January 1, 2026, allowing additional lead time. However, Rhode Island's absence of a cure period and Kentucky's permanent cure period mean that the risk tolerance for delayed compliance differs markedly between the two.

### Industries Most Affected

**Ad-tech and programmatic advertising:** Maryland's MODPA imposes the most challenging requirements on ad-supported business models. The product/service tethered data minimization standard, universal opt-out requirements, and outright prohibition on selling sensitive data are incompatible with standard behavioral advertising data flows unless substantially constrained.

**Data brokers and analytics vendors:** Nebraska's broad applicability without revenue or volume thresholds means that data brokers operating in Nebraska or targeting Nebraska residents — regardless of size — must comply with the NDPA, including for sensitive data sales.

**Employers and HR technology:** Minnesota's profiling transparency rights specifically address employment decisions (access to jobs), creating new disclosure and correction obligations for employers using automated screening tools, hiring algorithms, or background check products.

**Platforms and consumer-facing technology:** All five laws require privacy notices, consumer rights request mechanisms, data protection assessments for high-risk processing, and processor contracts. Organizations operating nationally that have built VCDPA-compliant programs will need gap analyses primarily for the Maryland and Minnesota deviations.

### State Enforcement Capacity

The [Minnesota Attorney General](https://ag.state.mn.us/Data-Privacy/) has published guidance and signaled active enforcement intent for the MCDPA. The [Maryland Attorney General](https://oag.state.md.us/) has the Division of Consumer Protection staffed for MODPA enforcement. Kentucky's AG has published [consumer rights information](https://www.ag.ky.gov/about/Office-Divisions/ODP/KCDPA/Pages/default.aspx). Nebraska and Rhode Island have not yet published prominent enforcement guidance.

## Action Items

- **Immediate (Nebraska — already in effect):** Confirm whether your organization is covered by the NDPA's broad applicability. If not SBA-classified as a small business and you operate in or target Nebraska, ensure privacy notice, consumer rights mechanisms, processor contracts, and sensitive data opt-in requirements are in place.
- **By July 31, 2025 (Minnesota):** Implement MCDPA compliance including: (a) data inventory documentation, (b) consumer rights request mechanisms including the profiling transparency right, (c) third-party disclosure list capability, (d) universal opt-out mechanism recognition, and (e) data protection assessments for high-risk processing.
- **By October 1, 2025 / enforcement April 1, 2026 (Maryland):** Audit data collection practices against MODPA's product/service-tethered minimization standard. This is the highest-effort task: many data flows permissible under Virginia-model laws may not satisfy Maryland's stricter "strictly necessary" standard for sensitive data. Implement the prohibition on selling sensitive data and configure universal opt-out mechanism recognition. Complete data protection assessments.
- **By January 1, 2026 (Kentucky, Rhode Island):** Complete KCDPA and RIDTPPA gap analyses. Rhode Island requires particular attention: no cure period means violations carry immediate penalty risk. The universal privacy notice requirement for commercial websites applies regardless of size threshold.
- **Cross-state program review:** Organizations with national data practices should conduct a single multi-state mapping exercise covering all five laws simultaneously, with particular focus on identifying data flows that satisfy the VCDPA standard but not Maryland's stricter minimization requirements. This is the most common failure mode for organizations relying on previously certified Virginia compliance.
- **Monitor enforcement signals:** Track enforcement actions under the Nebraska NDPA (first to go live) as a leading indicator for how AGs in other states will interpret analogous provisions.
