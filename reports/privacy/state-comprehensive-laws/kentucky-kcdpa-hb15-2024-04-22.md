# Kentucky Enacts the Consumer Data Protection Act (HB 15/KCDPA): Virginia-Model Law Takes Effect January 2026

**Jurisdiction:** Kentucky | **Category:** Privacy | **Date:** 2024-04-22

## Executive Summary [HIGH confidence]

On April 4, 2024, Governor Andy Beshear signed [House Bill 15](https://apps.legislature.ky.gov/recorddocuments/bill/24RS/hb15/bill.pdf) into law, making Kentucky the fifteenth US state to enact a comprehensive consumer data privacy statute. The Kentucky Consumer Data Protection Act (KCDPA) takes effect on January 1, 2026 — with data protection assessments deferred until June 1, 2026 — and applies to businesses that process personal data of at least 100,000 Kentucky consumers, or at least 25,000 consumers while deriving more than 50% of gross revenue from data sales. Modeled closely on Virginia's Consumer Data Protection Act, the KCDPA is a notably business-friendly law: it defines "sale" as an exchange for monetary consideration only, does not require recognition of universal opt-out mechanisms, and maintains a permanent 30-day cure period with no sunset. Enforcement rests exclusively with the Kentucky Attorney General, with civil penalties up to $7,500 per violation and no private right of action.

## Background [HIGH confidence]

Kentucky's path to comprehensive privacy legislation followed the well-worn Virginia-model template that spread through Republican-leaning states in 2022–2024. Virginia enacted its Consumer Data Protection Act (VCDPA) in March 2021, and within three years, states including Connecticut, Colorado, Utah, Iowa, Indiana, Tennessee, Montana, Texas, and Oregon had passed similar statutes. Kentucky HB 15 was introduced in the 2024 Regular Session of the Kentucky General Assembly and passed both chambers with overwhelming bipartisan support before Governor Beshear signed it on April 4, 2024.

The law is codified as Chapter 72 of the 2024 Regular Session Acts. The [official enrolled act](https://apps.legislature.ky.gov/law/acts/24RS/documents/0072.pdf) is available from the Kentucky Legislature. Kentucky became the fifteenth state to join the growing patchwork of state privacy laws, and the KCDPA was designed to bring business compliance costs in line with the Virginia-modeled states rather than the more demanding California or Maryland regimes.

Kentucky's Office of the Attorney General subsequently [established a dedicated KCDPA page](https://www.ag.ky.gov/about/Office-Divisions/ODP/KCDPA/Pages/default.aspx) to guide both businesses and consumers through the law's requirements, signaling an intent to actively use the new enforcement authority.

## Detailed Analysis [HIGH confidence]

### Applicability Thresholds

The [KCDPA](https://apps.legislature.ky.gov/recorddocuments/bill/24RS/hb15/bill.pdf) applies to persons that conduct business in Kentucky or produce products or services targeted to Kentucky residents and, during a calendar year, either:

1. Control or process personal data of **at least 100,000** Kentucky consumers; or
2. Control or process personal data of **at least 25,000** Kentucky consumers and derive **more than 50% of gross revenue** from the sale of personal data.

There is no small-business carve-out beyond the thresholds themselves, but the relatively high floor means that smaller regional businesses are unlikely to be covered. Notably, the KCDPA does not include a revenue-based size threshold such as the $25 million gross revenue floor in the California CCPA, meaning that a high-volume data processor that is otherwise small could still be covered.

### Entity and Data Exemptions

The KCDPA provides several categorical exemptions at the entity level. [Exempt entities](https://www.koleyjessen.com/insights/publications/kentucky-becomes-fifteenth-state-to-enact-privacy-law) include:

- State agencies and political subdivisions of the state
- Nonprofit organizations
- Institutions of higher education
- Financial institutions subject to the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act (GLBA)
- HIPAA-covered entities and business associates (for data governed by HIPAA)
- Entities governed by the Fair Credit Reporting Act (FCRA) or Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act (FERPA)
- Small telephone utilities, Tier III CMRS providers, and municipal utilities that do not sell or share personal data

Certain data types are also excluded at the data level, including employment data, publicly available information, de-identified data, pseudonymous data, and data maintained for research or journalistic purposes.

### Consumer Rights

The KCDPA grants Kentucky residents five principal rights with respect to their personal data held by covered controllers:

1. **Right to access** — Confirm whether a controller is processing personal data and obtain a copy of that data.
2. **Right to correction** — Correct inaccuracies in personal data.
3. **Right to deletion** — Delete personal data provided by or obtained about the consumer.
4. **Right to data portability** — Obtain a portable copy of personal data in a readily usable format, at least once per 12-month period.
5. **Right to opt out** — Opt out of processing for targeted advertising, sale of personal data, or profiling in furtherance of decisions that produce legal or similarly significant effects.

Controllers must respond to consumer rights requests within **45 days**, with a single permissible extension of an additional 45 days when reasonably necessary. If a controller declines a request, the consumer has the **right to appeal** the decision within a reasonable period. The controller must respond to appeals within 60 days and must provide a method for the consumer to submit a complaint to the Attorney General if the appeal is denied.

### Controller Obligations

Controllers — entities that determine the purpose and means of processing personal data — bear the primary compliance obligations under the KCDPA. These include:

- **Data minimization**: Limit collection to what is adequate, relevant, and reasonably necessary for the disclosed processing purposes.
- **Purpose limitation**: Do not process personal data in a manner incompatible with the purposes disclosed in the privacy notice.
- **Security safeguards**: Implement reasonable administrative, technical, and physical data security practices appropriate to the volume and nature of the data.
- **Nondiscrimination**: Do not discriminate against consumers for exercising their KCDPA rights.
- **Privacy notice**: Provide a reasonably accessible, clear, and meaningful privacy notice disclosing the categories of data processed, the purposes of processing, categories of third parties receiving data, consumer rights and how to exercise them, appeal procedures, and whether personal data is sold or used for targeted advertising.
- **Processor contracts**: Enter into binding contracts with processors specifying the nature and purpose of processing, the types of data involved, and the duration of processing, along with standard data protection obligations.
- **Data protection assessments**: Conduct and document data protection assessments for higher-risk processing activities.

### Sensitive Data

The KCDPA requires **opt-in consent** (affirmative express consent) before a controller processes sensitive data — this is the most stringent approach, shared with Virginia and most other Virginia-model states (as contrasted with Utah and Iowa, which use an opt-out model for sensitive data). Sensitive data under the KCDPA includes:

- Data revealing racial or ethnic origin
- Religious beliefs
- Mental or physical health diagnosis
- Sexual orientation
- Citizenship or immigration status
- Genetic data
- Biometric data processed for the purpose of uniquely identifying an individual
- Precise geolocation data
- Personal data collected from a known child under 13 years of age

For children's data specifically, controllers must comply with the Children's Online Privacy Protection Act (COPPA).

### Data Protection Assessments

Controllers must conduct data protection assessments for processing activities that present heightened risk, including:

- Processing for targeted advertising
- Selling personal data
- Processing for profiling that presents certain reasonably foreseeable risks of harm
- Processing sensitive data
- Any other processing presenting heightened risk of harm to consumers

Importantly, [the obligation to maintain data protection assessments applies only to processing activities created on or after January 1, 2026, but the assessments themselves are not required to be completed until **June 1, 2026**](https://www.datagrail.io/blog/regulations/kcdpa/), giving organizations additional runway.

### Processor Obligations

Processors — entities that process personal data on behalf of controllers — must adhere to the controller's instructions and assist controllers in meeting their obligations. [Contracts between controllers and processors](https://www.ketch.com/regulatory-compliance/kentucky-consumer-data-protection-act-kcdpa) must specify: the nature and purpose of processing; the type of personal data subject to processing; the duration of processing; and the rights and obligations of both parties. Processors must implement appropriate security measures, notify controllers of security incidents, delete or return data upon contract termination, and cooperate with audits.

### Definition of "Sale" — Business-Friendly Scope

A key business-friendly feature of the KCDPA is its narrow definition of "sale of personal data." Like Virginia and Utah (but unlike California), the KCDPA defines a sale as a disclosure of personal data to a third party **for monetary consideration only**. Non-monetary exchanges of data — such as data sharing arrangements where the controller receives services in return — do not constitute a "sale" triggering the opt-out right. This contrasts with the broader California CCPA definition, which encompasses exchanges for "valuable consideration."

### No Universal Opt-Out Mechanism Requirement

Unlike Colorado, Connecticut, and several other states, the KCDPA does **not** require controllers to recognize universal opt-out mechanisms (such as the Global Privacy Control browser signal) as a valid means for consumers to exercise their opt-out rights. Controllers may offer opt-out through their own mechanisms, giving businesses flexibility in how they implement the opt-out right.

### Enforcement and Penalties

The KCDPA provides **exclusive enforcement authority to the Kentucky Attorney General** (the "Office of Consumer Protection") — there is no private right of action for individual consumers. The enforcement process is as follows:

1. Before initiating an action, the AG must provide written notice identifying the specific alleged violation(s).
2. The controller has **30 days** to cure the alleged violation and notify the AG in writing.
3. If the controller fails to cure within the 30-day period, the AG may file a civil action.
4. Civil penalties may reach **$7,500 per violation**.
5. The AG may also seek actual damages on behalf of aggrieved consumers and seek injunctive relief.

The KCDPA's cure period is **permanent** — it does not sunset — distinguishing it from states like Colorado (cure period expired July 1, 2024) and Connecticut (cure period expired December 31, 2024). A business that promptly remedies a violation upon AG notice faces no financial exposure under the KCDPA, making it one of the more forgiving enforcement regimes in the state privacy law landscape.

## Comparative Analysis [MEDIUM confidence]

The KCDPA is explicitly modeled on Virginia's VCDPA and occupies a position in the middle of the spectrum of state comprehensive privacy laws, leaning toward the business-friendly end.

### KCDPA vs. VCDPA (Virginia)

The two laws are structurally nearly identical: same thresholds (100,000/25,000+50%), same five consumer rights, same $7,500-per-violation penalty, same permanent cure period, same "monetary consideration only" sale definition. The KCDPA differs from VCDPA primarily in that [Virginia introduced a data broker registration regime](https://www.recordinglaw.com/us-laws/data-privacy-laws/us-state-privacy-laws-comparison/) in 2024 that Kentucky has not adopted.

### KCDPA vs. UCPA (Utah) and ICDPA (Iowa)

Utah and Iowa are more business-friendly: both use an opt-out (not opt-in) model for sensitive data, and Iowa lacks a data protection assessment requirement. The KCDPA is more protective than those two states, requiring opt-in consent for sensitive data processing — a meaningful distinction for health tech, biometric, and location-data businesses.

### KCDPA vs. CCPA (California) and MODPA (Maryland)

The KCDPA is substantially less demanding than California's CCPA/CPRA regime (no private right of action, no regulatory agency with rulemaking authority, narrow sale definition, no universal opt-out requirement) and far less demanding than Maryland's MODPA (no data minimization requirement tied to compatible necessity, no prohibition on selling sensitive data, no profit-motive applicability threshold). Organizations already compliant with the VCDPA are likely to be substantially KCDPA-compliant with minimal incremental effort.

### States with January 1, 2026 Effective Dates

The KCDPA shares its January 1, 2026 effective date with the Indiana Consumer Data Protection Act (INCDPA) and the Rhode Island Data Transparency and Privacy Protection Act (RIDTPPA), creating a cluster of new privacy law obligations that became operative simultaneously for businesses operating across multiple states.

## Impact Assessment [HIGH confidence]

### Affected Businesses

The KCDPA applies broadly to any Kentucky-connected business — whether incorporated in Kentucky or simply targeting Kentucky consumers — that meets the processing thresholds. Businesses most immediately affected include:

- **Technology and e-commerce companies** processing consumer data at scale
- **Advertising technology firms** engaged in targeted advertising or behavioral profiling
- **Data brokers and aggregators** deriving revenue from data sales
- **Health and wellness apps** processing sensitive health or biometric data
- **Financial services firms** not fully exempt under GLBA

Organizations already operating under the VCDPA (Virginia), CTDPA (Connecticut), or similar Virginia-model laws will find the KCDPA largely familiar and can leverage existing compliance programs with limited modification.

### Key Compliance Gaps to Address

Businesses preparing for the KCDPA should focus on the following potential gaps:

1. **Opt-in for sensitive data**: If your current program provides opt-out (or no mechanism) for sensitive data, you must implement affirmative consent mechanisms before January 1, 2026.
2. **Processor contracts**: Review all data processing agreements and update them to include KCDPA-required provisions.
3. **Privacy notice update**: Ensure your notice expressly addresses targeted advertising and data sales, and provides clear opt-out mechanisms, even if universal opt-out is not required.
4. **Consumer rights workflows**: Implement or extend your DSR (Data Subject Request) process to handle Kentucky consumer requests within the 45-day response window.

### Enforcement Outlook

As of the time of writing, the Kentucky AG has not yet announced enforcement actions under the KCDPA. The permanent cure period significantly reduces litigation risk for compliant organizations that promptly respond to AG notices. Given the business-friendly design of the statute, the enforcement burden on companies already compliant with peer Virginia-model laws is expected to be low. However, the AG's establishment of a dedicated KCDPA office page signals an intent to actively publicize the law and field consumer complaints.

## Action Items

- By **January 1, 2026** (effective date): Ensure privacy notice, consumer rights workflows (45-day response), opt-in consent for sensitive data, data minimization practices, and processor contracts are in place.
- By **June 1, 2026** (DPA deadline): Complete and document data protection assessments for all higher-risk processing activities created on or after January 1, 2026.
- **Immediate**: Audit your data processing inventory to identify Kentucky-resident data flows and map them against KCDPA sensitive data categories.
- **Immediate**: Update data processing agreements with all processors handling Kentucky consumer data to incorporate KCDPA-required contract terms.
- **Ongoing**: Monitor Kentucky AG communications and any rulemaking announcements; the AG office's KCDPA page at ag.ky.gov is the authoritative source.
- **Ongoing**: Track the Kentucky HB 692 (Automatic Content Recognition amendment) which adds ACR data as sensitive data under the KCDPA, effective July 1, 2027.
