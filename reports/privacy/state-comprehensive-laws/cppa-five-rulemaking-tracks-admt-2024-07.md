# CPPA Board Discusses Upcoming Formal Rulemaking on ADMT, Risk Assessments, Cybersecurity Audits, Insurance, and CCPA Updates

**Jurisdiction:** California | **Category:** Privacy | **Date:** July 23, 2024

## Executive Summary [HIGH confidence]

On July 16, 2024, the California Privacy Protection Agency (CPPA) Board convened a public meeting to discuss next steps in its pending formal rulemaking covering five distinct regulatory tracks: (1) automated decisionmaking technology (ADMT), (2) privacy risk assessments, (3) cybersecurity audits, (4) insurance sector coverage, and (5) updates to existing CCPA regulations. The Board declined to advance the package to formal rulemaking at that session, citing an incomplete Standardized Regulatory Impact Assessment (SRIA) and unresolved board-level disagreements over the scope of ADMT and risk assessment obligations. Board Member Alastair MacTaggart raised concerns that the draft ADMT definition was broad enough to capture routine computational tools far beyond the regulation's intended target. Staff were directed to revise the draft to narrow scope and return with a revised package. Following subsequent board meetings and a formal 45-day public comment period opened in November 2024, the CPPA Board unanimously adopted the finalized regulations on July 24, 2025; the California Office of Administrative Law approved and filed the regulations with the Secretary of State on September 23, 2025. Most obligations became operative on January 1, 2026, with ADMT compliance deferred to January 1, 2027.

## Background [HIGH confidence]

### The CPPA and Its Rulemaking Mandate

The California Privacy Protection Agency (CPPA) was established by Proposition 24 — the [California Privacy Rights Act (CPRA)](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=CIV&sectionNum=1798.100.) — passed by California voters in November 2020. The CPPA became the first dedicated state consumer privacy enforcement agency in the United States, with independent rulemaking and enforcement authority under the [California Consumer Privacy Act (CCPA)](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=CIV&sectionNum=1798.100.). CPRA amendments to the CCPA took effect January 1, 2023, and the CPPA assumed full rulemaking authority from the California Attorney General.

Among the CPPA's statutory mandates under the CPRA is the obligation to promulgate regulations governing cybersecurity audits, risk assessments, and ADMT — areas the CPRA explicitly flagged for further agency action but left to the CPPA to define in detail. The five-track rulemaking package discussed at the July 16, 2024 meeting represented the CPPA's primary vehicle for fulfilling these mandates.

### Pre-Rulemaking History (2021–2024)

The CPPA began stakeholder outreach on cybersecurity audits, risk assessments, and ADMT as early as 2021. In [February 2023](https://cppa.ca.gov/regulations/pre_rulemaking_activities_pr_02-2023.html), the agency issued a formal Invitation for Preliminary Comments on these topics, soliciting written input through March 27, 2023. In [November 2023](https://cppa.ca.gov/announcements/2023/20231127.html), the CPPA published a draft automated decisionmaking technology regulatory framework, characterizing it as "a new landmark for consumer control."

In April 2024, the CPPA held [three statewide pre-rulemaking stakeholder sessions](https://cppa.ca.gov/announcements/2024/20240424.html) across California to gather additional input on all five regulatory tracks. On July 15, 2024 — one day before the Board meeting — the CPPA publicly released a consolidated draft rulemaking package exceeding 200 pages, comprising proposed new rules on all five tracks overlaid onto the existing CCPA regulatory framework.

### Procedural Prerequisites for Formal Rulemaking

Before the CPPA can initiate formal rulemaking under California's Administrative Procedure Act (APA), it must complete a Standardized Regulatory Impact Assessment (SRIA) and submit it to the California Department of Finance for review and comment. The SRIA must quantify the economic impact of the proposed regulations on California businesses, including effects on job creation, business competitiveness, and investment. This procedural step — not yet complete at the time of the July 16 meeting — was one of the two reasons the Board did not vote to advance.

## The Five Regulatory Tracks [HIGH confidence]

The July 16, 2024 Board meeting discussed all five tracks in the rulemaking package. Each track carried distinct scope, applicability thresholds, and substantive obligations. The following analysis draws on [Hunton Andrews Kurth's contemporaneous coverage](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-information-security-law/cppa-board-discusses-upcoming-formal-rulemaking-on-automated-decisionmaking-technology-risk-assessments-cybersecurity-audits-insurance-and-updates-to-existing-regulations), [FKKS Technology Law's board meeting takeaways](https://technologylaw.fkks.com/post/102jdj9/takeaways-from-the-cppas-july-2024-board-meeting), and the [official CPPA regulations page](https://cppa.ca.gov/regulations/ccpa_updates.html).

### Track 1: Automated Decisionmaking Technology (ADMT)

The ADMT track was the most novel and controversial element of the package. The July 2024 draft defined ADMT as "any technology that processes personal information and uses computation to execute a decision, replace human decisionmaking, or substantially facilitate human decisionmaking." This definition was immediately critiqued as overbroad by board members and industry stakeholders alike.

Under the July 2024 draft, ADMT obligations were triggered when a business used ADMT for three covered use cases:

1. **Significant decisions** affecting a consumer — including decisions in financial services, housing, education, employment, and healthcare;
2. **Extensive profiling** — including behavioral advertising and other consumer profiling activities; and
3. **Training ADMT or AI models** for the above purposes.

For each covered use, the draft would have required businesses to:

- Provide **pre-use notices** informing consumers of the planned ADMT use before deployment;
- Offer consumers the **right to opt out** of the business's use of ADMT (with limited exceptions);
- Provide consumers **access rights** to information about how ADMT was used in decisions affecting them; and
- Honor consumer **appeal rights** regarding significant decisions made by ADMT.

**Key controversy**: Board Member Alastair MacTaggart criticized the broad ADMT definition for potentially sweeping in routine computational tools — spreadsheet calculations, basic recommendation engines — that present no meaningful risk to consumers. As reported by [McDermott Will & Emery](https://www.mcdermottlaw.com/insights/draft-ccpa-regulations-stalled-as-agency-struggles-with-applicability-of-admt-rules/), the draft CCPA regulations stalled at least in part because the agency struggled with determining the appropriate scope of the ADMT rules. The Board directed staff to narrow the definition and the trigger conditions.

**Evolution to final rules**: Following the July 2024 meeting, the CPPA significantly narrowed ADMT's scope through successive drafts. The final regulations adopted July 24, 2025 define ADMT as technology that processes personal information "to replace human decisionmaking or substantially replace human decisionmaking" — removing the "substantially facilitate" formulation that contributed to definitional breadth. The [finalized definition](https://cppa.ca.gov/regulations/ccpa_updates.html) provides that "substantially replace human decisionmaking" means the business uses the technology to make a decision "without human involvement," and specifies what constitutes adequate human involvement. AI references and the behavioral advertising trigger were removed from the final regulations, with the CPPA deferring AI-specific rules to allow the California legislature to address AI regulation first.

### Track 2: Privacy Risk Assessments

The risk assessment track would require covered businesses to complete written privacy risk assessments before initiating processing activities that present a "significant risk" to consumer privacy. The July 2024 draft listed triggering activities as:

- Selling or sharing personal information;
- Processing sensitive personal information (other than for employment-related purposes);
- Using ADMT for a significant decision or for extensive profiling (including behavioral advertising); and
- Training ADMT or AI models.

Risk assessments under the draft would need to involve relevant internal and external stakeholders, weigh the benefits of the processing activity against privacy risks to consumers, and produce a written report maintained by the business. A key board debate at the July 16 meeting concerned whether the risk assessment obligation should apply to all consequential data processing, or only to processing that used ADMT — a scope question the Board directed staff to clarify in any call for public comment.

**Evolution to final rules**: The [final risk assessment regulations](https://cppa.ca.gov/regulations/ccpa_updates.html) require businesses to conduct risk assessments before initiating activities and review them at least every three years, or within 45 calendar days of any material change to the processing activity. Businesses subject to risk assessments must begin compliance by January 1, 2026, with a grace period until December 31, 2027, to complete assessments for processing already underway before the effective date. Businesses must submit attestation summaries to the CPPA by April 1, 2028 (for assessments conducted in 2026 and 2027). As [Goodwin Law summarized](https://www.goodwinlaw.com/en/insights/publications/2025/07/alerts-practices-dpc-californias-new-privacy-and-cybersecurity-regulations), the CPPA's stated goal for risk assessments is to restrict or prohibit processing where risks to consumer privacy outweigh the benefits.

### Track 3: Cybersecurity Audits

The cybersecurity audit track would require qualifying businesses to conduct **annual cybersecurity audits** performed by a qualified, objective, and independent auditor (who may be internal but must not be responsible for the cybersecurity program being audited). Audits would cover at least 17 program components specified in the regulations. A key unresolved issue at the July 2024 stage was the applicability threshold: the draft presented multiple options — 250,000, 500,000, or 1,000,000 or more consumers — rather than a single settled figure, reflecting unresolved board policy choices.

The [Wilson Sonsini analysis](https://www.wsgr.com/en/insights/cppa-board-grapples-with-public-concerns-key-updates-on-upcoming-ai-risk-assessment-and-cybersecurity-regulations.html) noted that the cybersecurity audit draft would require businesses to first implement qualifying cybersecurity programs before the audit requirement became operational — a phased approach intended to ensure businesses built compliant programs before auditing against them.

**Evolution to final rules**: The [final cybersecurity audit regulations](https://cppa.ca.gov/regulations/ccpa_updates.html), approved September 23, 2025, apply to businesses whose prior-year annual gross revenues exceeded the inflation-adjusted monetary threshold (currently $26.62 million) AND who meet at least one of three alternative thresholds: (a) processed personal information of at least 250,000 consumers or households; (b) derived at least 50% of annual revenues from selling or sharing personal information; or (c) processed the sensitive personal information of at least 50,000 consumers or households. Audits must be evidence-based rather than management-attestation-based. Businesses must submit annual certifications of compliance to the CPPA signed under penalty of perjury, with staggered submission deadlines: April 1, 2028 (revenues over $100 million), April 1, 2029 ($50-100 million), or April 1, 2030 (under $50 million). All audit records must be retained for five years. As [Ropes & Gray reported](https://www.ropesgray.com/en/insights/alerts/2026/01/californias-ccpa-cybersecurity-audit-rule-takes-effect-what-businesses-need-to-know), the cybersecurity audit rules took effect January 1, 2026.

### Track 4: Insurance Sector Coverage

The insurance track proposed to clarify and extend CCPA/CPPA obligations to insurance companies, which have historically operated under a distinct regulatory framework — the California Insurance Code — that has sometimes been invoked to argue that insurance-related personal data falls outside the CCPA's scope. The July 2024 draft proposed to define "insurance company" and specify that the CCPA applies to personal data not separately governed by the California Insurance Code.

**Board dynamics**: Board members at the July 16 meeting flagged the insurance provisions as closely tied to pending California legislation on an insurance model code. Several members suggested the CPPA should hold off on finalizing insurance regulations until the legislature resolved the interplay between the Insurance Code and the CCPA, to avoid regulatory overlap or conflict.

**Evolution to final rules**: The [final regulations clarify](https://cppa.ca.gov/regulations/ccpa_updates.html) when insurance companies must comply with the CCPA. The final text provides illustrative examples — insurance companies must comply with the CCPA for personal data collected from website visitors and from employees — while carving out data governed by the California Insurance Code. As [Ogletree Deakins reported](https://ogletree.com/insights-resources/blog-posts/california-privacy-protection-agency-clarifies-application-of-the-ccpa-to-insurance-companies/), the final regulations provide the insurance industry with the clearest guidance to date on its CCPA obligations.

### Track 5: Updates to Existing CCPA Regulations

The fifth track encompassed approximately 72 pages of proposed amendments to the existing CCPA regulatory framework. These updates addressed technical and operational aspects of the existing regulatory scheme, including:

- Consent mechanisms and dark pattern prohibitions;
- Disclosure requirements and privacy notice standards;
- Technical standards for honoring consumer requests to know, delete, and opt out;
- Service provider and contractor compliance obligations; and
- Procedures for submitting consumer requests.

This track received less public attention than the more novel ADMT and cybersecurity tracks, but represented a substantial revision of the operational compliance landscape for all businesses subject to the CCPA.

## What Happened at the July 16, 2024 Meeting [HIGH confidence]

### The Board Did Not Vote to Advance

The CPPA Board declined to take a vote to advance the rulemaking package to formal rulemaking. As [Alston & Bird reported](https://www.alstonprivacy.com/cppa-board-declines-to-advance-ccpa-regulations-to-formal-rulemaking-cppa-highlights-enforcement-priorities/), the Board cited two overlapping reasons:

1. **Incomplete SRIA**: The CPPA staff had not yet completed the Standardized Regulatory Impact Assessment required before formal rulemaking could begin under the APA. Without the completed SRIA, a vote to advance would have been procedurally premature.
2. **Scope disagreements**: Board members, particularly Alastair MacTaggart, argued that the ADMT definition and risk assessment trigger provisions were too broad. MacTaggart expressed concern that the draft's scope could inadvertently cover a greater number of technologies than the rulemaking intended, including routine computational tools that pose no meaningful consumer risk.

The Board directed staff to revise the draft to narrow scope — particularly the ADMT definition and risk assessment trigger conditions — and return with a revised package for the September 2024 meeting.

### Enforcement Priorities Update

Alongside the rulemaking discussion, CPPA staff presented an update on enforcement priorities. Consumer complaints most frequently covered: the right to delete, unauthorized collection or disclosure of personal information, and failure to honor opt-out of sale or sharing. Additional complaint volume came from children's privacy, financial incentive programs, and loyalty program disclosures. This enforcement update — occurring alongside a rulemaking that had stalled on scope — underscored that the CPPA was simultaneously engaging in both active rulemaking and active enforcement.

### Stakeholder Concerns and Controversy

The July 16 meeting surfaced several areas of controversy beyond the board-level scope disputes:

- **ADMT breadth**: Technology companies and business groups argued the draft ADMT definition would cover standard software engineering practices, not merely AI systems presenting elevated consumer risk;
- **Behavioral advertising trigger**: Consumer advocates supported covering behavioral advertising under the ADMT and risk assessment triggers; industry stakeholders argued this was disproportionate given the nature of online advertising;
- **Insurance overlap**: Insurance industry representatives flagged potential conflict with the California Insurance Code framework and pending model code legislation;
- **Economic impact**: The preliminary compliance cost estimate — approximately $4.2 billion in year one for California businesses — was cited by both industry (as evidence of over-regulation) and consumer advocates (as evidence of the regulations' substance); and
- **SRIA timing**: Some observers questioned whether the CPPA had sufficient staff resources to complete the SRIA promptly, given the complexity of quantifying compliance costs across five regulatory tracks.

## Subsequent Regulatory Milestones [HIGH confidence]

The July 16, 2024 meeting was a deliberate pause, not a failure of the rulemaking. The package progressed through the following milestones:

| Date | Milestone |
|---|---|
| July 16, 2024 | Board declines to advance; directs staff to narrow scope and complete SRIA |
| August 2024 | CPPA releases revised draft incorporating board feedback |
| November 8, 2024 | [Board votes 4-1 to advance package to formal rulemaking](https://cppa.ca.gov/announcements/2024/20241108_2.html) |
| November 22, 2024 | [CPPA opens formal 45-day public comment period](https://www.alstonprivacy.com/cppa-opens-formal-public-comment-period-for-ccpa-proposed-regulations/) (originally scheduled to close January 14, 2025) |
| January 14, 2025 | First in-person and virtual public hearing |
| February 19, 2025 | [Extended close of written comment period and second public hearing](https://cppa.ca.gov/announcements/2025/20250116.html) (extension announced January 16, 2025 due to California wildfires) |
| May 2025 | CPPA releases modified proposed text with significant ADMT narrowing |
| July 24, 2025 | [Board unanimously adopts final regulations (5-0)](https://cppa.ca.gov/announcements/2025/20250923.html) |
| September 23, 2025 | California OAL approves regulations; filed with Secretary of State |
| January 1, 2026 | Most regulations become operative; risk assessment conduct obligation begins |
| January 1, 2027 | ADMT obligations (for significant decisions) become operative |

As [IAPP reported](https://iapp.org/news/a/cppa-board-finalizes-long-awaited-admt-risk-assessment-rules), the final July 24, 2025 vote followed "more than a year of drafting and debate" following the November 2024 formal rulemaking initiation. The process was contentious throughout: the [IAPP separately reported](https://iapp.org/news/a/after-contentious-meeting-cppa-moves-ai-rules-forward) that the final vote came "after a contentious meeting," with the removal of AI references from ADMT text and broadening of opt-out exceptions reflecting significant compromises from the original draft.

## Compliance Implications by Track [MEDIUM confidence]

The final regulations create staggered compliance obligations across the five tracks. Compliance officers and legal counsel should plan along the following lines:

### ADMT Compliance (Operative January 1, 2027)

Businesses must:
- **Identify ADMT use**: Determine which business systems qualify as ADMT under the final definition (technology that replaces or substantially replaces human decisionmaking, without human involvement).
- **Map significant decisions**: Identify processing activities that produce significant decisions affecting finances, housing, education, employment, or healthcare.
- **Build pre-use notice infrastructure**: Develop pre-use notices informing consumers of ADMT use before each qualifying deployment.
- **Implement opt-out and appeal workflows**: Create consumer-facing opt-out and appeal mechanisms for each covered ADMT use, including at least two opt-out methods (one consistent with the primary consumer interaction channel).
- **Train human reviewers**: Where businesses intend to use the human review exception to avoid opt-out obligations, ensure reviewers genuinely meet the final rule's three-part test for meaningful human involvement.

### Risk Assessment Compliance (Conduct: January 1, 2026; Report Submission: April 1, 2028)

Businesses must:
- **Conduct assessments before January 1, 2026** for any new qualifying processing activities initiated after that date.
- **Complete retroactive assessments by December 31, 2027** for processing already underway before January 1, 2026.
- **Involve relevant stakeholders**: Internal and external stakeholders must be included in the risk assessment process.
- **Document and maintain written reports**: Each risk assessment must produce a written report retained by the business.
- **Submit attestation to CPPA by April 1, 2028** covering assessments conducted in 2026 and 2027.
- **Refresh assessments every three years** or within 45 days of any material change.

### Cybersecurity Audit Compliance (Operative January 1, 2026; Certification Deadlines Staggered)

Businesses at or above the applicability threshold must:
- **Assess coverage**: Determine whether annual gross revenues exceed the adjusted monetary threshold ($26.62 million currently) and whether any of the three alternative consumer/revenue thresholds are met — (a) 250,000+ total consumers or households processed, (b) 50%+ of annual revenues from selling or sharing personal information, or (c) 50,000+ consumers or households whose sensitive personal information is processed.
- **Build audit-ready cybersecurity programs**: Implement programs covering the CPPA's 17 required components before the audit deadline.
- **Engage qualified auditors**: Retain independent, objective auditors (internal auditors permitted if not responsible for the cybersecurity program).
- **Retain audit records for five years**.
- **Submit annual certifications to the CPPA**, signed under penalty of perjury by executive leadership, on the applicable staggered deadline.

### Insurance Sector Compliance

Insurance companies must:
- **Map personal data by regulatory framework**: Identify which personal data is governed by the California Insurance Code and which falls under CCPA jurisdiction.
- **Apply CCPA obligations to non-Insurance-Code data**: Website visitor data, employee data, and other data not governed by the Insurance Code requires full CCPA compliance.
- **Update privacy notices and consumer rights infrastructure** for CCPA-covered data.

### CCPA Regulatory Updates

All CCPA-covered businesses should:
- **Audit existing consumer request workflows** against the updated technical and operational standards.
- **Review consent mechanisms** for dark pattern compliance — the CPPA issued a [September 2024 Enforcement Advisory](https://cppa.ca.gov/announcements/2024/20240904.html) specifically on dark patterns, and the updated regulations codify related obligations.
- **Evaluate service provider and contractor compliance programs** against the updated contractor obligation framework.

## Action Items

- **Complete ADMT inventory now**: Do not wait until the 2027 compliance date. Identifying qualifying ADMT uses, scoping significant decisions, and building opt-out infrastructure takes time. Begin inventory and gap analysis in 2026.
- **Launch risk assessment program**: January 1, 2026 is the operative date for new processing activities. Map all qualifying processing activities — sale/sharing, sensitive PI, ADMT for significant decisions — and begin documentation. The 2027 deadline for existing activities creates a grace period but not unlimited time.
- **Assess cybersecurity audit readiness**: Businesses above the applicability threshold have staggered certification deadlines (2028-2030), but building an audit-ready cybersecurity program typically takes 12-24 months. Begin program gap analysis in 2026. Note that the sensitive PI pathway (50,000+ consumers) means businesses with moderate total consumer volumes but significant sensitive data processing may be in scope even if they process fewer than 250,000 consumers overall.
- **Engage insurance counsel**: Insurance companies facing dual exposure under the Insurance Code and the CCPA should seek guidance on the boundary between the two frameworks under the final regulations.
- **Monitor CPPA guidance**: The CPPA is expected to issue interpretive guidance on scope questions left open by the rulemaking process. Monitor [cppa.ca.gov/announcements](https://cppa.ca.gov/announcements/) for updates.
- **Watch for AI-specific rulemaking**: The CPPA removed AI-specific provisions from the final ADMT regulations to allow the legislature to act first. If the California legislature enacts AI-specific privacy legislation, the CPPA may open a new rulemaking track. Monitor California legislative developments accordingly.
