# Vermont Enacts S.71 Data Privacy and Online Surveillance Act (Act 145)

**Jurisdiction:** Vermont | **Category:** Privacy | **Date:** 2026-06-16

## Summary [HIGH confidence]

Governor Phil Scott signed S.71, "An act relating to consumer data privacy and online surveillance," into law on June 16, 2026 — eight days after he initially vetoed it on June 8, 2026. Enacted as Act 145, the Vermont Data Privacy and Online Surveillance Act (VDPOSA) is Vermont's first comprehensive consumer privacy law and makes Vermont the 23rd state to enact such legislation. The Vermont House of Representatives had passed S.71 on May 26, 2026 by a vote of 129-3, with the Senate concurring the same day. The law establishes consumer rights over personal data, bans geofencing near health facilities, requires recognition of universal opt-out signals, and leaves enforcement exclusively with the Vermont Attorney General. Most provisions take effect January 1, 2028.

## Key Facts [HIGH confidence]

- The Vermont General Assembly passed S.71 on May 26, 2026: the [House voted 129-3](https://vermontbiz.com/news/2026/may/27/legislature-passes-data-privacy-bill-s71) and the Senate concurred the same day, sending the bill to Governor Scott.
- Governor Phil Scott vetoed S.71 on June 8, 2026, according to [The Record from Recorded Future News](https://therecord.media/vermont-governor-veto-comprehensive-data-privacy-bill). This was the governor's second veto of a comprehensive Vermont privacy bill; he had vetoed the predecessor bill, H.121, on June 13, 2024 (a veto the Senate sustained on June 17, 2024), per the [Office of Governor Phil Scott](https://governor.vermont.gov/press-release/action-taken-governor-phil-scott-legislation-june-13-2024).
- Governor Scott reversed course and signed S.71 into law on June 16, 2026, making Vermont the 23rd state to enact a comprehensive consumer privacy law, per [Hunton](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-cybersecurity-law-blog/vermont-becomes-23rd-state-with-comprehensive-consumer-privacy-law) and [DataGuidance](https://www.dataguidance.com/news/vermont-governor-signs-bill-consumer-data-privacy-and).
- The [official bill status page](https://legislature.vermont.gov/bill/status/2026/S.71) on the Vermont Legislature's website now reflects the bill's enacted status as Act 145.
- S.71 is Vermont's first comprehensive privacy law. It is the first enacted comprehensive consumer privacy statute in the state's history.
- S.71's most distinctive provision is a ban on geofencing within 1,850 feet of any health facility. No data broker or ad network may deploy a geofence to identify, track, collect data from, or send notifications to a consumer near a clinic, hospital, or rehabilitation center, per [State of Surveillance](https://stateofsurveillance.org/news/vermont-s71-data-privacy-law-health-geofencing-ban-federal-preemption-2026/).
- The law applies to controllers that, during the prior calendar year, process personal data of at least 35,000 consumers, process sensitive data of at least 3,000 consumers, or sell personal data of at least 3,000 consumers, per [ComplianceHub.Wiki](https://compliancehub.wiki/vermont-s71-data-privacy-online-surveillance-act-2026/).
- Consumer health data provisions apply more broadly, without the threshold requirements, to any business operating in Vermont or targeting Vermont residents, per [McDermott Will & Emery](https://www.mcdermottlaw.com/insights/vermont-passes-consumer-privacy-bill/).
- Enforcement is handled solely by the Vermont Attorney General; the law explicitly prohibits a private right of action for consumers. From January 1, 2028 through June 30, 2029, the AG must provide written notice and a 60-day cure period before initiating enforcement, per [Captain Compliance](https://captaincompliance.com/education/vermont-house-approves-amended-consumer-privacy-bill-sending-s-71-toward-governors-desk/).
- Most provisions take effect January 1, 2028, per [Vermont Business Magazine](https://vermontbiz.com/news/2026/may/27/legislature-passes-data-privacy-bill-s71).
- Key protections include: mandatory recognition of the Global Privacy Control browser signal, data minimization requirements limiting collection to what is "reasonably necessary," consumer rights to access, correct, delete, and transfer data, and opt-out rights for automated decision-making systems, per [IAPP](https://iapp.org/news/a/the-vermont-veto-is-a-step-backward-for-privacy).
- Notably, VDPOSA does not extend a blanket exemption to nonprofit organizations — unlike many peer state laws — and requires processors to enter data processing agreements with controllers, per [Mayer Brown](https://www.mayerbrown.com/en/insights/publications/2026/06/vermont-enacts-comprehensive-consumer-privacy-law).
- The law includes distinctive requirements not present in most peer statutes: mandatory disclosure in privacy notices of whether personal data is used to train large language models, and inclusion of "neural data" within the definition of sensitive personal information, per [Mayer Brown](https://www.mayerbrown.com/en/insights/publications/2026/06/vermont-enacts-comprehensive-consumer-privacy-law) and [Inside Privacy / Covington](https://www.insideprivacy.com/state-privacy/vermont-data-privacy-bill-signed-into-law/).
- Exemptions cover HIPAA-covered entities, FERPA-protected education records, noncommercial journalism, employment-related data, and federally regulated financial data under Gramm-Leach-Bliley, per [ComplianceHub.Wiki](https://compliancehub.wiki/vermont-s71-data-privacy-online-surveillance-act-2026/).
- Consumer Reports issued a statement of support upon the bill's passage, per [their advocacy press release](https://advocacy.consumerreports.org/press_release/consumer-reports-statement-on-passage-of-vermonts-comprehensive-privacy-bill/).
- EPIC submitted testimony supporting S.71, per [EPIC's published testimony](https://epic.org/documents/testimony-on-vermont-s-71-an-act-relating-to-consumer-data-privacy-and-online-surveillance/).

## Legislative History [HIGH confidence]

S.71 is the successor to Vermont's 2024 comprehensive privacy effort. Governor Scott vetoed H.121 — a broader bill that included a private right of action — on June 13, 2024, citing litigation risk to Vermont businesses. The Senate failed to override the 2024 veto on June 17, 2024 by a margin of 14-15 (a two-thirds supermajority was required).

The 2026 bill was deliberately structured to address the governor's 2024 objections. Lawmakers removed the private right of action and restricted enforcement to the Attorney General. The Senate first passed a pared-down version in March 2025 by a vote of 29-0, per [VTDigger](https://vtdigger.org/2025/03/27/final-reading-vermont-senate-passes-pared-down-data-privacy-bill/). The House Commerce Committee reviewed and amended it substantially before the chamber approved it 129-3 on May 26, 2026.

Governor Scott vetoed S.71 on June 8, 2026. The governor's stated concerns in the veto appeared to relate to broader business climate risk. The [IAPP characterized the veto](https://iapp.org/news/a/the-vermont-veto-is-a-step-backward-for-privacy) as a step backward for privacy. The exact quoted language from the June 8 veto statement has not been independently verified against the Governor's Office press release and is not reproduced here.

Eight days after his veto, on June 16, 2026, Governor Scott reversed course and signed S.71 into law as Act 145, making Vermont the 23rd state with a comprehensive consumer privacy law. The signing is confirmed by the [Vermont Legislature's official bill status page](https://legislature.vermont.gov/bill/status/2026/S.71) and reporting from [DataGuidance](https://www.dataguidance.com/news/vermont-governor-signs-bill-consumer-data-privacy-and), [Hunton](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-cybersecurity-law-blog/vermont-becomes-23rd-state-with-comprehensive-consumer-privacy-law), [Mayer Brown](https://www.mayerbrown.com/en/insights/publications/2026/06/vermont-enacts-comprehensive-consumer-privacy-law), and [Inside Privacy / Covington](https://www.insideprivacy.com/state-privacy/vermont-data-privacy-bill-signed-into-law/).

## Comparison to the 2024 H.121 Veto [MEDIUM confidence]

The 2026 S.71 differed from 2024's H.121 in several material respects:

| Feature | H.121 (2024, Vetoed) | S.71 / Act 145 (2026, Enacted) |
|---|---|---|
| Private right of action | Yes (broad) | No -- AG only |
| Geofencing ban near health facilities | No | Yes (1,850 ft radius) |
| Global Privacy Control recognition | Yes | Yes |
| Nonprofits covered | No | Yes |
| Automated decision-making rights | Limited | Expanded |
| Neural data as sensitive data | No | Yes |
| LLM training disclosure | No | Yes |
| Effective date | Would have been 2025 | January 1, 2028 |

The removal of the private right of action was the central concession designed to win the governor's signature. The law's addition of neural data protections and LLM training disclosure requirements place Vermont at the frontier of state privacy law, matching or exceeding provisions in peer statutes.

## Action Items

- Organizations doing business in Vermont or targeting Vermont residents should immediately assess whether they meet S.71's applicability thresholds: 35,000 consumers (personal data), 3,000 consumers (sensitive data or data sales). Most provisions take effect January 1, 2028.
- Consumer health data programs face broader applicability — the law's health data controller requirements apply without the general thresholds to any business operating in or targeting Vermont residents. Begin compliance gap assessment now.
- Businesses with geofencing capabilities near health facilities (within 1,850 feet of any clinic, hospital, or rehabilitation center) should treat this as an immediate-term remediation item, as the prohibition is clear and the effective date is January 1, 2028.
- Privacy notices will need to be updated to disclose whether personal data is used to train large language models — a Vermont-specific requirement not present in most peer state privacy laws. Legal teams should flag this for clients using consumer data for AI training.
- Organizations that collect or process "neural data" (brainwave data, neural signals) should ensure they categorize this as sensitive personal information under VDPOSA and apply the applicable opt-in consent requirements.
- A 60-day AG cure period applies from January 1, 2028 through June 30, 2029. Monitor the Vermont AG's office for enforcement guidance, implementing regulations, or statements on enforcement priorities before the cure period expires.
- Processors contracting with Vermont-covered controllers should expect data processing agreement requirements — update standard vendor agreements and DPA templates accordingly.
- Legal teams should track whether the Vermont legislature considers funding for AG enforcement as contemplated by the bill's legislative record; adequately funded AG enforcement could accelerate Vermont's posture relative to other states.
