# Delaware Personal Data Privacy Act (DPDPA / HB 154): Compliance Guide for Businesses

**Jurisdiction:** Delaware | **Category:** Privacy | **Date:** 2024-05-15

## Executive Summary [HIGH confidence]

Delaware enacted House Bill 154, the Delaware Personal Data Privacy Act (DPDPA), on September 11, 2023, becoming the twelfth state to pass a comprehensive consumer data privacy law. The law took effect January 1, 2025. The DPDPA follows the Virginia model broadly, but contains several provisions that are materially stricter or novel compared to peer state laws: it applies the lowest applicability threshold of any state comprehensive privacy law (35,000 consumers), is the first to expressly categorize pregnancy as sensitive data, requires opt-in consent before processing sensitive data, and creates a unique consumer right to obtain a list of third-party categories to whom personal data has been disclosed. Enforcement falls exclusively to the Delaware Attorney General, with civil penalties up to $10,000 per violation and a cure period that expired December 31, 2025. Organizations that are already complying with other Virginia-model state privacy laws still need to audit for Delaware-specific requirements — particularly around sensitive data consent, minor data protections, nonprofit coverage, and the third-party data deletion obligation.

## Background [HIGH confidence]

Delaware's enactment of the DPDPA in September 2023 was part of a wave of state-level privacy legislation that saw seven states pass comprehensive privacy statutes in calendar year 2023. Prior to the DPDPA, Delaware had only sector-specific privacy protections, including its existing data breach notification statute under Title 6, Chapter 12B of the Delaware Code. The DPDPA amends Title 6 of the Delaware Code to add a new subchapter on personal data privacy and consumer protection.

The bill, [House Bill 154](https://legis.delaware.gov/BillDetail?LegislationId=140388) of the 152nd General Assembly, was approved by the Delaware General Assembly on June 30, 2023, and signed into law by Governor John Carney on September 11, 2023, with an effective date of January 1, 2025 — giving businesses approximately 15 months to prepare.

Delaware's approach draws heavily from the [Connecticut Data Privacy Act (CTDPA)](https://portal.ct.gov/AG/Sections/Privacy/The-Connecticut-Data-Privacy-Act) but incorporates modifications reflecting Delaware's smaller population and more expansive vision for consumer protection. It was analyzed extensively by the [Future of Privacy Forum](https://fpf.org/blog/a-new-domicile-for-comprehensive-privacy-in-delaware/) as containing several first-of-their-kind provisions in U.S. state privacy law.

## Detailed Analysis [HIGH confidence]

### Applicability Thresholds

The DPDPA applies to any person that conducts business in Delaware or produces products or services targeted to Delaware residents and, during the preceding calendar year, either:

1. Controlled or processed the personal data of at least **35,000 Delaware residents** (excluding data controlled or processed solely to complete a payment transaction); or
2. Controlled or processed the personal data of at least **10,000 Delaware residents** and derived more than **20% of gross revenue from the sale of personal data**.

The 35,000-consumer threshold is the lowest primary coverage threshold of any enacted comprehensive state privacy law in the United States, as noted by [Akin Gump Strauss Hauer & Feld LLP](https://www.akingump.com/en/insights/blogs/ag-data-dive/delaware-data-protection-act-what-businesses-need-to-know). The 20% revenue-from-sale threshold is also comparatively low — Connecticut uses 25% and Virginia uses 50%. Organizations that fall below California or Virginia thresholds may nonetheless be covered under the DPDPA.

### Entity-Level Exemptions

Exempt entities include:

- **Government entities** and any contractor acting on behalf of a government entity when processing data solely for governmental purposes.
- **Financial institutions** or affiliates subject to the **Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act (GLBA)**.
- **Higher education institutions** (not-for-profit institutions of higher education).
- **Nonprofit organizations** are generally NOT exempt — Delaware joins Colorado and Oregon in covering most nonprofits. However, two narrow nonprofit exemptions exist: (a) nonprofits dedicated exclusively to preventing and addressing insurance fraud; and (b) nonprofits providing services to victims or witnesses of child abuse, domestic violence, human trafficking, sexual assault, violent felonies, or stalking.
- HIPAA **covered entities and business associates** are NOT exempted at the entity level (a notable departure from many peer laws).

As noted by [Davis Wright Tremaine](https://www.dwt.com/blogs/privacy--security-law-blog/2023/09/delaware-personal-data-privacy-act-enacted), the absence of a HIPAA entity-level exemption means healthcare-related businesses that are subject to HIPAA must still comply with the DPDPA for data not covered by HIPAA's preemptive scope.

### Data-Level Exemptions

The DPDPA exempts specific categories of data from its coverage, including:

- Protected health information (PHI) under HIPAA.
- Personal data subject to GLBA.
- Personal data regulated under FERPA, FCRA, the Driver's Privacy Protection Act, the Farm Credit Act, and the Airline Deregulation Act.
- Employee data and business-to-business data have limited exemptions for certain processing activities.

### Consumer Rights

Delaware residents (consumers) have the following rights under the DPDPA, per [the Delaware Attorney General's FAQ portal](https://attorneygeneral.delaware.gov/fraud/personal-data-privacy-portal/frequently-asked-questions/):

1. **Right to access** — confirm whether a controller is processing their personal data and access that data.
2. **Right to correct** — correct inaccuracies in their personal data.
3. **Right to delete** — request deletion of personal data, including data obtained from third parties.
4. **Right to data portability** — obtain a copy of their personal data in a portable format.
5. **Right to disclosure list** — obtain a list of the categories of third parties to whom the controller has disclosed their personal data. This right is unique to the DPDPA among state privacy laws, as analyzed by the [Future of Privacy Forum](https://fpf.org/blog/a-new-domicile-for-comprehensive-privacy-in-delaware/).
6. **Right to opt out** — opt out of processing for purposes of targeted advertising, sale of personal data, and profiling for decisions with legal or similarly significant effects.

Controllers must respond to authenticated consumer requests within 45 days, with a 45-day extension available when reasonably necessary. Appeals of denied requests must be responded to within 60 days.

### Sensitive Data: Expanded and Novel Categories

The DPDPA requires **opt-in consent** before processing sensitive data — a higher standard than opt-out that applies to regular personal data. Sensitive data categories under the DPDPA include:

- Racial or ethnic origin
- Religious beliefs
- **Mental or physical health condition or diagnosis, including pregnancy** — Delaware is the first state comprehensive privacy law to explicitly designate pregnancy as sensitive data, according to [Cleary Cybersecurity and Privacy Watch](https://www.clearycyberwatch.com/2023/09/broad-definition-of-sensitive-data-and-concern-for-childrens-and-teenagers-data-in-delaware-privacy-law-reflect-recent-trends-in-evolving-data-protection-landscape/).
- Sex life or sexual orientation
- **Status as transgender or nonbinary** — also a DPDPA first in U.S. state privacy law.
- Citizenship or immigration status
- Genetic data
- Biometric data processed to uniquely identify an individual
- Personal data of a known child (under 13)
- Precise geolocation data (within a 1,750-foot radius)

### Minor Data Protections (Ages 13-17)

The DPDPA prohibits controllers from selling personal data or processing it for targeted advertising purposes when the controller has actual knowledge, or willfully disregards, that the consumer is between 13 and 17 years of age — unless the controller obtains the consumer's consent. This is a notable expansion beyond child-only protections, extending to teenagers up to age 17.

### Third-Party Data: Unique Deletion Obligation

Unlike other state privacy laws that typically permit controllers to retain data obtained from third-party sources (e.g., data brokers) as long as they opt consumers out of all non-exempt processing, the DPDPA requires controllers to **actually delete** third-party data upon a valid deletion request. Controllers may retain only "a record of the deletion request and the minimum data necessary for the purpose of ensuring the consumer's personal data remains deleted from the controller's records." This creates materially stricter obligations for data broker clients and companies with extensive third-party data enrichment programs.

### Controller Obligations

**Privacy Notice:** Controllers must maintain a reasonably accessible, clear, and meaningful privacy notice that includes:
- Categories of personal data processed and the purposes for processing.
- How consumers may exercise their rights and appeal denied requests.
- Categories of data shared with third parties and the categories of those third parties.
- A mechanism for consumers to submit complaints to the Delaware Department of Justice (privacy@delaware.gov).

Privacy notices must clearly indicate they apply to Delaware residents — either covering all U.S. residents or specifically identifying Delaware residents if a state-specific notice is used. According to the [Delaware AG FAQ portal](https://attorneygeneral.delaware.gov/fraud/personal-data-privacy-portal/frequently-asked-questions/), a notice that does not make this clear does not comply with the DPDPA.

**Data Processing Agreements:** Controllers must enter into binding contracts with processors that specify: instructions for processing; the nature, purpose, and duration of processing; types of data and categories of consumers; and processor obligations including data security, deletion or return of data, and cooperation with consumer rights requests.

**Data Protection Assessments (DPAs):** Controllers that process data of more than **100,000 consumers** (other than solely for payment processing) must conduct and document data protection assessments for high-risk processing activities, including targeted advertising, processing sensitive data, selling personal data, and profiling for decisions with significant effects. As noted by [Akin Gump](https://www.akingump.com/en/insights/blogs/ag-data-dive/delaware-data-protection-act-what-businesses-need-to-know), DPA obligations apply to activities **created or generated after July 1, 2025** (six months after the effective date) and are not retroactive.

### Enforcement

Enforcement authority rests exclusively with the Delaware Attorney General. There is no private right of action. The Delaware AG launched a [personal data privacy portal](https://news.delaware.gov/2024/07/08/ag-jennings-launches-portal-to-help-businesses-prepare-for-personal-data-privacy-act-enforcement/) in July 2024 to assist businesses in preparing for enforcement. Violations may result in civil penalties up to **$10,000 per violation**.

**Cure Period:** Prior to January 1, 2026, if the AG determined a controller could remedy a violation, it was required to give 60 days' notice before initiating a lawsuit. This cure period **sunset on December 31, 2025**, meaning as of January 1, 2026, the AG may bring enforcement actions without prior cure notice.

## Impact Assessment [HIGH confidence]

### Affected Organizations

The DPDPA's low 35,000-consumer threshold means it captures a significantly broader population of businesses than Virginia, Texas, or Florida — all of which use higher thresholds. Organizations most at risk of being newly covered (i.e., covered by Delaware but not other state laws) include:

- **Mid-size e-commerce and SaaS companies** that have some Delaware user base but fall below the 100,000-consumer thresholds of larger states.
- **Healthcare-adjacent businesses** that are subject to HIPAA at the entity level but process non-PHI data (e.g., health and wellness apps, medical device companies, fitness platforms).
- **Nonprofits** (except the narrow carve-outs noted above) — which are exempt in many peer states but covered in Delaware.
- **Data brokers and companies relying on third-party data enrichment**, due to the stricter deletion obligation.
- **Companies serving or marketing to teenagers (13-17)**, due to the opt-in consent requirement for that age group.

### Compliance Requirements and Timelines

As of the effective date (January 1, 2025), all covered controllers were required to:
- Maintain a DPDPA-compliant privacy notice, including the Delaware AG complaint mechanism.
- Honor consumer rights requests (access, correct, delete, portability, disclosure list, opt-out).
- Obtain opt-in consent before processing sensitive data.
- Prohibit sale or targeted advertising of data for known 13-17 year olds without consent.
- Execute data processing agreements with all processors.

As of July 1, 2025:
- Data protection assessments required for high-risk processing activities (for controllers processing more than 100,000 consumers' data) — applicable to activities initiated after this date.

As of January 1, 2026:
- The 60-day cure period expired. The AG may now bring enforcement actions immediately upon discovering violations.

### Enforcement Outlook

The Delaware AG has signaled active engagement: the July 2024 launch of the business compliance portal and consumer FAQ infrastructure indicate an enforcement-ready posture. The expiration of the cure period as of 2026 heightens urgency for lagging organizations. The [DBL Lawyers analysis](https://www.dbllawyers.com/new-delaware-personal-data-privacy-act-2025/) notes that 2025 was a transitional enforcement year with cure rights available; 2026 is the year enforcement without warning becomes possible.

## Action Items

- Audit your data inventory to determine whether the 35,000-consumer or 10,000-consumer / 20%-revenue thresholds are met for Delaware residents — even if your company is below thresholds in larger states.
- Review privacy notice for DPDPA compliance, including the requirement to identify applicability to Delaware residents and include the Delaware AG complaint mechanism (privacy@delaware.gov).
- Map all sensitive data processing and confirm opt-in consent mechanisms are in place for each category, with particular attention to health/pregnancy data and transgender/nonbinary status.
- Implement a consumer rights request workflow capable of fulfilling the six DPDPA rights, including the unique right to a third-party disclosure list.
- Review third-party data sourcing practices and ensure deletion requests trigger actual deletion of third-party-sourced data (not merely opt-out suppression).
- Verify data processing agreements with all processors include the DPDPA-required provisions.
- If processing data of more than 100,000 consumers, initiate data protection assessments for high-risk activities occurring after July 1, 2025.
- Audit data practices regarding consumers ages 13-17 to ensure no sale or targeted advertising occurs without affirmative consent.
- Note that the 60-day cure period has expired as of January 1, 2026 — any violation is immediately actionable by the AG.
- If your organization is a nonprofit, confirm whether it falls within a narrow exemption; most nonprofits in Delaware are covered.
