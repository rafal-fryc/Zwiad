# Minnesota Consumer Data Privacy Act: Davis Wright Tremaine Compliance Analysis

**Jurisdiction:** Minnesota | **Category:** Privacy | **Date:** 2024-06-06

## Executive Summary [HIGH confidence]

On May 24, 2024, Minnesota Governor Tim Walz signed [HF 4757](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/bills/93/2024/0/HF/4757/versions/4/) into law, enacting the Minnesota Consumer Data Privacy Act (MCDPA), codified at [Minnesota Statutes, chapter 325M](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/325M/full). The law takes effect July 31, 2025 for most controllers, with postsecondary institutions regulated by the Minnesota Office of Higher Education receiving an extended deadline of July 31, 2029. Davis Wright Tremaine LLP (DWT) characterized the MCDPA — in their piece titled "Land of 10,000 Data Lakes" — as a law that substantially tracks the Virginia-model framework while introducing several commercially significant novel obligations, including the nation's first statutory mandatory data inventory requirement, uniquely broad profiling challenge rights, and a right for consumers to obtain a list of specific third-party data recipients. Enforcement belongs exclusively to the Minnesota Attorney General, who may impose civil penalties of up to $7,500 per violation, with a 30-day cure period in effect through January 31, 2026.

## Background [HIGH confidence]

Minnesota's MCDPA was enacted as part of [Laws 2024, chapter 121](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/bills/93/2024/0/HF/4757/versions/4/), a broad legislative package. The legislature passed HF 4757 on May 19, 2024, and Governor Walz signed it five days later. Minnesota became the 18th state to enact a comprehensive consumer data privacy statute, joining California, Virginia, Colorado, Connecticut, Utah, Iowa, Indiana, Tennessee, Montana, Texas, Oregon, Florida, Delaware, New Hampshire, New Jersey, Kentucky, and Nebraska.

The [Minnesota Revisor of Statutes](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/325M/full) codified the Act as chapter 325M, sections 325M.10 through 325M.21. The citation form "Minnesota Consumer Data Privacy Act" appears in section 325M.10. The statute may be contrasted with earlier, narrower Minnesota privacy statutes governing Internet service providers under the pre-existing chapter 325M (which was renumbered).

The law's structure closely follows the Virginia Consumer Data Protection Act (VCDPA) but incorporates elements from Oregon's Consumer Privacy Act (OCPA) and, in some respects, goes beyond both. DWT's analysis, published June 4, 2024, highlighted both the familiar architecture and the commercially important deviations, which compliance teams need to understand before the July 2025 effective date.

## Detailed Analysis [HIGH confidence]

### Applicability Thresholds

The MCDPA applies to legal entities that conduct business in Minnesota or produce products or services targeted to Minnesota residents and that, during a calendar year, either:

- control or process personal data of **100,000 or more** Minnesota consumers (excluding data processed solely to complete a payment transaction); or
- derive **more than 25% of gross revenue** from the sale of personal data and process personal data of **25,000 or more** Minnesota consumers.

These thresholds are standard within the Virginia-model framework. Notably, personal data processed solely for payment transactions is excluded from the 100,000-consumer count, mirroring the approach in other state statutes.

A significant carve-out exists for **small businesses** as defined by the U.S. Small Business Administration under [13 C.F.R. § 121](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-13/chapter-I/part-121). Small businesses are exempt from most MCDPA requirements, but no small business — regardless of size — may sell a consumer's **sensitive data** without prior consent. This prohibition applies universally.

### Entity and Data Exemptions

The MCDPA follows the standard pattern of entity-level and data-level exemptions, including exemptions for:

- HIPAA-covered entities and business associates (for HIPAA-regulated data);
- data regulated under the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act;
- employee and business-to-business contact data;
- nonprofit organizations (broader than Oregon's approach);
- government entities.

The law does not apply to data processed by a controller solely in the context of an individual's employment relationship with that controller.

### Consumer Rights

Consumers who are Minnesota residents may exercise the following rights under [section 325M.14](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/325M.14):

1. **Right to access** — confirm whether a controller is processing their personal data and to access that data.
2. **Right to correct** — correct inaccurate personal data.
3. **Right to delete** — request deletion of personal data.
4. **Right to portability** — obtain a copy of personal data in a portable, readily usable format.
5. **Right to opt out** — opt out of targeted advertising, sale of personal data, and profiling in furtherance of decisions with legal or similarly significant effects.
6. **Right to question profiling** — this is among the MCDPA's most novel provisions. If a consumer's data is used to make a profiling decision with **legal or similarly significant effects**, the consumer may: (a) question the decision; (b) be informed of the reasons the profiling resulted in the decision; (c) where feasible, be informed of what actions might have led to a different outcome; and (d) review the personal data used in the profiling.
7. **Right to obtain a list of specific third parties** — unlike most state privacy statutes, which require only disclosure of categories of third parties, Minnesota (following Oregon) requires disclosure of the **specific third-party entities** to which a consumer's personal data has been disclosed. If the controller cannot identify the specific third parties for a specific consumer, it may provide the list of all specific third parties to which it has disclosed **any consumer's** personal data.

Controllers must respond to opt-out requests as soon as feasibly possible and no later than **45 days** of receipt. Other consumer rights requests must be honored within **45 days**, with a 45-day extension available upon notice to the consumer.

### Controller Obligations

**Privacy Notice.** Controllers must provide a reasonably accessible, clear, and meaningful privacy notice disclosing: categories of personal data processed; the purposes of processing; how consumers may exercise rights; categories of personal data shared with third parties; and categories of third parties receiving personal data.

**Data Inventory (Novel).** The MCDPA is the first US state privacy law to codify a **mandatory data inventory** requirement. Controllers must maintain an inventory of the personal data they process sufficient to establish, implement, and maintain the administrative, technical, and physical security measures required under the Act. This inventory must also support the required documentation of policies and procedures (see below).

**Policy and Procedure Documentation (Novel).** Controllers must document and maintain a description of policies and procedures adopted to comply with the Act. This documentation must include: (a) the name and contact information of the chief privacy officer or the individual with primary responsibility for compliance; and (b) a description of policies and procedures designed to reflect MCDPA requirements in the design of the controller's systems. DWT's analysis flagged this as a significant departure from other state statutes, which typically require privacy notices and contracts but not documented internal compliance frameworks.

**Data Protection Agreements (DPAs).** Controllers engaging processors must execute binding contracts specifying: the instructions for processing; the nature and purpose of processing; the types of data subject to processing; duration; and the rights and obligations of both parties. This is standard across Virginia-model statutes.

**Data Protection Assessments (DPIAs).** Controllers must conduct data protection assessments for processing activities presenting heightened risk, including: targeted advertising; sale of personal data; profiling with legal or similarly significant effects; processing sensitive data; and any processing that presents a heightened risk of harm to consumers. DPIAs must be made available to the Minnesota AG upon request.

**Sensitive Data.** Controllers must obtain **opt-in consent** before processing sensitive data. Sensitive data categories under the MCDPA include: racial or ethnic origin; religious beliefs; mental or physical health diagnosis; sexual orientation; citizenship or immigration status; genetic data; biometric data processed for the purpose of uniquely identifying an individual; and personal data collected from known children. Precise geolocation data is also treated as sensitive.

**Authorized Agent — Browser Settings (Novel).** Consumers may designate authorized agents to exercise privacy rights on their behalf. The MCDPA uniquely permits such designation through an "Internet link or a browser setting." This aligns with the Global Privacy Control (GPC) signal framework and signals legislative recognition of browser-level opt-out mechanisms as valid legal instruments.

### Processor Obligations

Processors must: (a) adhere to the controller's processing instructions; (b) assist controllers in meeting their obligations; (c) upon request, make available information necessary to demonstrate compliance; and (d) allow for and contribute to reasonable assessments and inspections by the controller or its designated assessor, or alternatively arrange for an independent qualified assessor to conduct an annual assessment using an accepted control standard and provide the report to the controller.

### Enforcement

Enforcement authority rests exclusively with the [Minnesota Attorney General](https://www.ag.state.mn.us/Data-Privacy/). The AG may bring civil actions seeking injunctive relief and civil penalties of up to **$7,500 per violation** and may recover reasonable investigative costs.

Under [section 325M.20](https://law.justia.com/codes/minnesota/chapters-324-342/chapter-325m/section-325m-20/), from July 31, 2025 through **January 31, 2026**, the AG must issue a written warning letter identifying the alleged violations and provide the controller or processor with **30 days to cure** before filing suit. After January 31, 2026, no cure period applies and the AG may proceed directly with enforcement. There is no private right of action under the MCDPA.

The AG has established a consumer-facing portal at [PrivacyMN.com](https://ag.state.mn.us/Data-Privacy/) and issued a [July 28, 2025 press release](https://www.ag.state.mn.us/Office/Communications/2025/07/28_MCDPA.asp) announcing that the law was in effect and encouraging consumer complaints.

## Impact Assessment [HIGH confidence]

**Who is covered.** Any entity — regardless of whether it is domiciled in Minnesota — that collects data on 100,000 or more Minnesota consumers annually, or that derives more than 25% of its gross revenue from selling personal data while processing data on 25,000 or more Minnesota consumers. Businesses with national data practices almost certainly meet these thresholds. Small businesses (SBA-defined) are largely exempt but remain subject to the prohibition on selling sensitive data without consent.

**Key compliance burdens.** The data inventory and policy documentation requirements are operationally significant and go beyond what most other state privacy laws require. A controller compliant with Virginia's VCDPA or Colorado's CPA will need to supplement its compliance program with: (a) a formal data inventory maintained at the personal data category level; (b) documented policies naming a chief privacy officer or equivalent; and (c) a process for responding to consumer requests for lists of specific third-party recipients — a capability that requires third-party tracking many companies do not have.

**The profiling challenge mechanism** creates a new consumer-facing process that controllers must design. For any automated decision producing legal or similarly significant effects, the controller must be able to explain the decision, identify the data used, and — where feasible — articulate what actions the consumer could have taken to reach a different outcome. This requires audit-trail capabilities that pure opt-out compliance programs do not address.

**The browser-setting authorized agent provision** means that controllers must configure their systems to honor browser-level GPC signals as valid legal authorizations for opt-out requests. Controllers who previously treated GPC signals as advisory may now face legal exposure in Minnesota for failing to honor them.

**Enforcement risk.** The 30-day cure period through January 31, 2026 provided a limited grace period, but that window closed. As of February 1, 2026, the Minnesota AG may proceed directly to court without advance notice. Controllers without documented compliance programs face heightened risk because the AG can subpoena the mandatory policy documentation to assess whether a formal compliance framework exists.

## Action Items

- Verify whether the entity meets either MCDPA applicability threshold (100,000 consumers or 25%+ revenue from data sales + 25,000 consumers) and document the analysis.
- Conduct a data inventory mapping all categories of personal data processed, the purposes for processing, and the specific third parties to which data has been disclosed — this inventory is a legal requirement, not merely a best practice.
- Appoint or designate a chief privacy officer (or equivalent) and document their name and contact information as required by the Act.
- Update privacy notices to include categories of personal data shared, the specific third parties receiving data (or provide a mechanism for consumers to request this list), and consumer rights information specific to Minnesota.
- Establish a process for responding to profiling challenge requests, including the ability to explain automated decision logic, identify data used, and (where feasible) describe counterfactual pathways for consumers.
- Configure opt-out and rights-request systems to honor GPC browser signals as valid authorized agent designations under Minnesota law.
- Negotiate and execute data protection agreements with all processors, and request processor compliance documentation as required by the Act.
- Conduct and document data protection assessments for high-risk processing activities (targeted advertising, data sales, profiling, sensitive data processing).
- Confirm that sensitive data is not sold without prior consumer consent — this obligation applies even to otherwise-exempt small businesses.
- Monitor the Minnesota AG's enforcement posture at [ag.state.mn.us/Data-Privacy](https://ag.state.mn.us/Data-Privacy/) and the PrivacyMN.com consumer portal for enforcement signals. The cure period expired January 31, 2026; direct enforcement is now possible.
- For postsecondary institutions regulated by the Minnesota Office of Higher Education, confirm compliance planning targets the July 31, 2029 deadline.
