# Colorado and California Lead the Nation on Neural Data Privacy Regulation

**Jurisdiction:** Colorado, California | **Category:** Privacy | **Date:** October 21, 2024

## Executive Summary [HIGH confidence]

In 2024, Colorado and California became the first two U.S. states to enact consumer privacy legislation explicitly protecting neural data — information generated by measuring the activity of the central or peripheral nervous system. Colorado acted first: Governor Jared Polis signed [H.B. 24-1058](https://content.leg.colorado.gov/sites/default/files/documents/2024A/bills/2024a_1058_enr.pdf) on April 17, 2024, amending the [Colorado Privacy Act (CPA)](http://leg.colorado.gov/bills/hb24-1058) to add "biological data" (including neural data) to its roster of sensitive data requiring affirmative opt-in consent before processing. The law took effect August 7, 2024. California followed: Governor Gavin Newsom signed [S.B. 1223](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/billTextClient.xhtml?bill_id=202320240SB1223) on September 28, 2024, amending the California Consumer Privacy Act (CCPA) to classify "neural data" as sensitive personal information, effective January 1, 2025. Both laws were championed by the NeuroRights Foundation and passed with near-unanimous or unanimous legislative votes. Together they establish the first regulatory framework for consumer neurotechnology data in the United States, covering EEG wearables, neurofeedback devices, sleep trackers, and emerging brain-computer interface (BCI) products sold outside of clinical settings.

## Background [HIGH confidence]

### The Rise of Consumer Neurotechnology

The legislative push for neural data protection emerged from a concrete market development: brain-monitoring devices are no longer confined to hospital settings. Consumer-grade EEG headbands, neurofeedback wearables, sleep-tracking devices, and emerging BCI products now collect and transmit data about users' nervous system activity directly to mobile applications. [A 2024 audit by the NeuroRights Foundation of 30 consumer neurotechnology companies](https://www.neurorightsfoundation.org/advocacy/united-states) found that 96.7% of companies reserve the right to transfer brain data to third parties, most policies are vague on sale or brokerage, and 29 of 30 companies provide no meaningful limitations on their access to brain data. These findings underscored a regulatory gap: existing frameworks addressed clinically generated neural data under HIPAA but left consumer-facing products almost entirely unregulated.

### The NeuroRights Foundation's Legislative Campaign

The NeuroRights Foundation — a Columbia University-affiliated nonprofit — sponsored or actively supported both the Colorado and California bills. The Foundation has briefed officials at the National Security Council, U.S. Department of State, and Members of Congress on neurotechnology governance. Both bills passed with extraordinary bipartisan margins: California's SB 1223 passed the State Senate 38-0; Colorado's HB 24-1058 passed the General Assembly with near-unanimous votes, with only three dissenting votes in the House and zero in the Senate. As [KFF Health News reported](https://kffhealthnews.org/news/article/colorado-california-montana-states-neural-data-privacy-laws-neurorights/), neural data protection is among the rare issues that unite lawmakers across the political spectrum.

### Prior Regulatory Architecture

Both states' laws extend pre-existing comprehensive privacy statutes rather than creating standalone neurodata legislation:

- **Colorado**: The [CPA, enacted via SB 21-190](https://content.leg.colorado.gov/sites/default/files/2021a_190_signed.pdf) on July 7, 2021 (effective July 1, 2023), is a Virginia-model omnibus consumer privacy law that pre-classified multiple categories of data — including genetic data and biometric data — as sensitive, requiring affirmative opt-in consent before processing. H.B. 24-1058 adds biological and neural data to this existing taxonomy.

- **California**: The CCPA, first enacted in 2018 and significantly amended by the California Privacy Rights Act (CPRA) in 2020, already treated biometric data, genetic data, and precise geolocation as "sensitive personal information" subject to heightened restrictions. SB 1223 inserts neural data into this existing list, alongside a companion bill, [A.B. 1008](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/billNavClient.xhtml?bill_id=202320240AB1008), which clarifies that personal information can exist in digital and AI-generated formats.

## Detailed Analysis [HIGH confidence]

### Colorado H.B. 24-1058: Definitions and Structure

H.B. 24-1058 amends [C.R.S. § 6-1-1303](https://content.leg.colorado.gov/sites/default/files/documents/2024A/bills/2024a_1058_enr.pdf) by inserting two nested definitions:

**"Biological data"** — Data generated by the technological processing, measurement, or analysis of an individual's biological, genetic, biochemical, physiological, or neural properties, compositions, or activities, or of an individual's body or bodily functions, which data is "used or intended to be used, singly or in combination with other personal data, **for identification purposes**."

**"Neural data"** — Information generated by the measurement of the activity of an individual's central or peripheral nervous systems and that can be processed by or with the assistance of a device.

A structural distinction noted by [Hunton Andrews Kurth](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-information-security-law/colorado-amends-privacy-act-with-h-b-1058-adding-new-protections-for-biological-and-neural-data) is that the biological data definition requires an identification purpose, while the neural data definition carries **no such qualifier**. The Colorado legislature expressly declared that because neural data contains distinctive information about individual brain and nervous system structure, it always carries sensitive information capable of linking data to an identified or identifiable individual — meaning it receives absolute sensitive-data treatment regardless of whether the controller intends to use it for identification.

### California S.B. 1223: Definition and CCPA Integration

[S.B. 1223](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/billTextClient.xhtml?bill_id=202320240SB1223) adds the following definition to the CCPA:

**"Neural data"** — Information that is generated by measuring the activity of a consumer's central or peripheral nervous system, **and that is not inferred from nonneural information**.

This definition, analyzed by [Morrison Foerster](https://www.mofo.com/resources/insights/241011-a-mofo-privacy-minute-q-a-california-revises-ccpa), includes an important exclusion: data *inferred* from non-neural sources (e.g., behavioral profiling, click patterns, or other proxy measurements) does not qualify as neural data under SB 1223 even if the inference concerns a mental or neurological state. Direct physiological measurement is required. This contrasts with Colorado's formulation, which does not contain an analogous inferential exclusion for neural data (though it applies to the biological data parent definition).

The CCPA classifies neural data, by virtue of SB 1223, as "sensitive personal information" — the highest protection tier. Under the CCPA's existing framework, this triggers:

- Consumers' right to **limit use and disclosure** of sensitive personal information to purposes reasonably necessary for services requested;
- Businesses' obligation to provide a **"Limit the Use of My Sensitive Personal Information"** disclosure mechanism;
- Stricter requirements for data processing agreements with service providers and contractors;
- Mandatory privacy notice disclosure regarding categories of sensitive personal information collected.

### Consent Models: A Critical Divergence

The two states adopt fundamentally different consent models for neural data — a distinction that [BakerHostetler highlighted in its October 2024 analysis](https://www.bakerdatacounsel.com/blogs/colorado-and-california-get-ahead-of-neural-data-regulation/) as the most operationally significant difference:

| Feature | Colorado (CPA / H.B. 24-1058) | California (CCPA / S.B. 1223) |
|---|---|---|
| Consent model | **Opt-in** — affirmative consent required before processing | **Opt-out** — consumers may direct businesses to limit use/disclosure |
| Employer/employee coverage | CPA generally excludes employee data; H.B. 24-1058 does **not** extend to employees | CCPA **includes** employee personal information; neural data protections apply in employment contexts |
| Threshold to be covered | 100,000+ consumer records OR 25,000+ records + data sale revenue | $25M+ revenue OR 100,000+ consumer records OR 50%+ revenue from selling/sharing personal data |
| Enforcement | Colorado AG and district attorneys; no private right of action | California AG; CPPA rulemaking authority; consumers have private right of action for certain data breaches |

The employment distinction is practically significant: California employers who use neurotechnology for employee wellness, productivity, or safety monitoring must comply with CCPA's neural data provisions. Colorado employers who fall below the CPA's consumer-facing thresholds may not be directly covered for their workforce neurotechnology use.

### Companion Bill: California A.B. 1008

Signed on the same day as SB 1223, [A.B. 1008](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/billNavClient.xhtml?bill_id=202320240AB1008) amends the CCPA's definition of "personal information" to clarify that personal information may exist in abstract digital formats, including AI system outputs capable of producing personal information. This has direct relevance to neural data: AI models trained on brain activity data, or capable of inferring neurological states, may themselves constitute personal information repositories. As analyzed by [McDermott Will & Emery](https://www.mwe.com/insights/california-amends-key-ccpa-definitions/), A.B. 1008 operationalizes the CCPA's coverage of AI-generated personal information alongside the neural data provisions of SB 1223.

### Scope Limitations: What Is Excluded

Both laws carve out data already regulated under federal health law:

- **HIPAA exclusion (both states)**: Data that constitutes protected health information (PHI) processed by HIPAA-covered entities or their business associates is excluded from both the CPA and CCPA. Clinically generated EEG or EMG data remains governed by HIPAA.
- **Colorado's identification qualifier**: Biological data (but not neural data) must be used or intended for identification purposes to fall within H.B. 1058's coverage. Raw physiological outputs not used for identification do not qualify as "biological data" under this definition.
- **California's inference exclusion**: Neural data inferred from non-neural inputs is outside SB 1223's scope.

The [Future of Privacy Forum's "Goldilocks Problem" analysis](https://fpf.org/blog/the-neural-data-goldilocks-problem-defining-neural-data-in-u-s-state-privacy-laws/) notes that Colorado's neural data definition (anchored to CNS/PNS activity measurement) and California's (same anchor, with the additional non-inference qualifier) are among the broadest definitional approaches enacted by any state — broad enough to capture consumer wearables measuring galvanic skin response, heart rate variability, and electromyography alongside explicit EEG devices, depending on product design.

## Impact Assessment [MEDIUM confidence]

### Industries Most Affected

[ArentFox Schiff](https://www.afslaw.com/perspectives/alerts/california-and-colorado-establish-protections-neural-data) and [Hunton Andrews Kurth](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-cybersecurity-law-blog/california-amends-ccpa-to-cover-neural-data-and-clarify-scope-of-personal-information) identify several industry segments with heightened compliance exposure:

- **Consumer neurotechnology manufacturers**: EEG-based sleep trackers, neurofeedback bands (e.g., Muse, Flow Neuroscience), focus-enhancement wearables, and consumer BCIs must now obtain affirmative opt-in consent (Colorado) and provide limiting mechanisms (California) before collecting and processing brain activity data from state residents.
- **Health and wellness app developers**: Digital mental health platforms, meditation apps with biofeedback, and cognitive assessment tools that ingest peripheral nervous system signals (heart rate variability as a stress proxy, skin conductance readings) should assess whether their sensor inputs fall within the "measurement of CNS/PNS activity" threshold.
- **Fitness technology companies**: Multi-modal wellness devices combining neural and non-neural biometric signals require legal analysis of whether any component crosses into neural data territory.
- **Employers using workplace neurotechnology**: In California, employers deploying neurotechnology for safety monitoring (drowsiness detection, concentration assessment) must ensure compliance with CCPA's neural data provisions for California employee data.
- **Third-party data brokers and analytics platforms**: Businesses that receive neural data from device manufacturers for analytics, research, or advertising purposes are subject to the same sensitive-data restrictions as primary collectors.

### The Silicon Valley Factor

California's inclusion of neural data in the CCPA is disproportionately significant because many of the world's leading neurotechnology companies — including Neuralink, Kernel, Precision Neuroscience, and numerous consumer wearable manufacturers — are headquartered in or operate primarily in California. As noted by the [NeuroRights Foundation](https://www.neurorightsfoundation.org/advocacy/united-states), "Silicon Valley is located in California and many neurotechnology companies are headquartered or operate there" — making California's CCPA amendment a de facto global compliance benchmark for the industry.

### The Employer Compliance Gap

[BakerHostetler's analysis](https://www.bakerdatacounsel.com/blogs/colorado-and-california-get-ahead-of-neural-data-regulation/) specifically flags that employers using neurotechnology may face meaningfully different obligations depending on jurisdiction. California employers must treat employee neural data as sensitive personal information under the CCPA; Colorado employers are generally outside the CPA's employee data coverage, though HB 1058 does not affirmatively create an employee carve-in. Companies with operations in both states need separate compliance tracks for the same underlying neural monitoring technology.

### Regulatory Momentum: A Growing Patchwork

Colorado and California's 2024 legislation has directly catalyzed activity in additional states. As of early 2025, [Morrison Foerster's survey](https://www.mofo.com/resources/insights/250317-more-states-propose-privacy-laws-safeguarding-neural-data) identified Connecticut, Illinois, Massachusetts, Minnesota, and Vermont as having pending neural data privacy legislation. Montana enacted its own [neural data law (SB 163)](https://legiscan.com/MT/bill/SB163/2025) in 2025, becoming the third state with active neural data protections, with an effective date of October 1, 2025. At the federal level, Senators Cantwell, Schumer, and Markey introduced the [MIND Act](https://www.dwt.com/blogs/privacy--security-law-blog/2025/10/senate-mind-act-neural-data-ftc-regulation) in September 2025, directing the FTC to study and recommend federal neural data standards.

## Action Items

- Audit all consumer-facing and enterprise products to identify whether any sensor, API, or hardware component measures central or peripheral nervous system activity — this includes EEG, EMG, EDA (electrodermal activity), and similar modalities — and determine whether that data is transmitted to a backend system.
- For Colorado operations: implement affirmative opt-in consent flows for neural or biological data collection from Colorado residents before August 7, 2024 (already effective); update privacy notices to disclose the category of data processed; complete and document a data protection assessment for each neural/biological data processing activity.
- For California operations: update CCPA privacy notices to include neural data as a sensitive personal information category; provide a "Limit the Use of My Sensitive Personal Information" mechanism effective January 1, 2025; ensure service provider and contractor data processing agreements address neural data.
- Assess employer-specific obligations: California employers using neurotechnology for any workforce purpose (safety monitoring, productivity tools, wellness programs) must apply CCPA neural data protections to California employee data as of January 1, 2025.
- Review third-party data sharing agreements to ensure downstream processors of neural data comply with state law restrictions on processing, sharing, and sale of neural data.
- Monitor additional state legislative activity: Connecticut, Illinois, Massachusetts, Minnesota, and Vermont have active or proposed neural data legislation; build consent and assessment infrastructure adaptable to a multi-state patchwork.
- Assess whether Colorado's "identification purpose" qualifier for biological data (distinct from neural data) narrows or widens coverage of specific product data streams relative to California's inference exclusion in SB 1223.
