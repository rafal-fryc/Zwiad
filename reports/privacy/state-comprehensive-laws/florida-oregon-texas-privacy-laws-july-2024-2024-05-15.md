# Florida, Oregon, and Texas Comprehensive Privacy Laws Take Effect July 1, 2024

**Jurisdiction:** Florida | Oregon | Texas | **Category:** Privacy | **Date:** 2024-05-15

## Executive Summary [HIGH confidence]

On July 1, 2024, three new state comprehensive consumer privacy laws became enforceable: the [Florida Digital Bill of Rights (FDBR)](https://www.flsenate.gov/Session/Bill/2023/262), enacted as Florida SB 262; the [Oregon Consumer Privacy Act (OCPA)](https://olis.oregonlegislature.gov/liz/2023R1/Measures/Overview/SB619), enacted as Oregon SB 619; and the [Texas Data Privacy and Security Act (TDPSA)](https://capitol.texas.gov/tlodocs/88R/billtext/html/HB00004F.htm), enacted as Texas HB 4. All three laws were signed by their respective governors in 2023 and joined California, Colorado, Connecticut, Utah, and Virginia as states with omnibus consumer data privacy regimes. While they share a common framework of consumer rights and controller obligations, each law has materially distinct applicability thresholds, enforcement mechanisms, and novel provisions that require separate compliance analysis. Organizations operating across these states should not assume that compliance with earlier state frameworks is sufficient without law-by-law review.

## Background [HIGH confidence]

The rapid proliferation of state consumer privacy laws accelerated significantly in 2023 and 2024, with no federal comprehensive privacy law enacted to provide a preemptive national standard. As of July 1, 2024, eight states had comprehensive privacy laws in effect. The three laws covered here — Florida's FDBR, Oregon's OCPA, and Texas's TDPSA — represent distinct legislative philosophies. Florida's law is the most narrowly targeted, applying effectively only to large technology platforms. Oregon's law is the most expansive in terms of defined data categories, explicitly covering inferred and derived data. Texas's law is the broadest in applicability scope, lacking the revenue and consumer-volume thresholds found in all other comparable state laws.

Florida Governor Ron DeSantis signed SB 262 on June 6, 2023. Oregon Governor Tina Kotek signed SB 619 on July 18, 2023. Texas Governor Greg Abbott signed HB 4 on June 18, 2023. All three statutes set July 1, 2024 as their primary effective date, with limited delayed provisions.

The legislative backdrop was a sustained effort by state legislatures to fill the void left by the repeated failure of federal privacy bills, including the American Data Privacy and Protection Act (ADPPA), which stalled in Congress. The July 2024 effective dates mark the largest single-day expansion of state consumer privacy law coverage in U.S. history to that point.

## Detailed Analysis [HIGH confidence]

### Florida Digital Bill of Rights (FDBR / SB 262)

The FDBR is codified in Florida Statutes Part V of Chapter 501. Its most distinctive — and limiting — feature is its extremely narrow applicability threshold. The law applies only to for-profit entities that: (a) conduct business in Florida or produce products or services targeted at Florida consumers; (b) collect personal data about those consumers; (c) generate more than **$1 billion in global gross annual revenues**; and (d) satisfy at least one of three additional conditions: derive at least 50% of global annual revenues from the online sale of advertisements, operate a consumer smart speaker and voice command service with a cloud-connected virtual assistant using hands-free verbal activation, or operate an app store or digital distribution platform offering at least 250,000 software applications for consumers to download.

As analyzed by [Davis Wright Tremaine](https://www.dwt.com/blogs/privacy--security-law-blog/2023/06/florida-digital-bill-of-rights-data-privacy) and [White & Case](https://www.whitecase.com/insight-alert/florida-enacts-digital-bill-rights-joining-growing-privacy-landscape), this threshold is designed to capture a narrow set of large technology platforms — effectively major advertising-dependent platforms, smart speaker operators (such as Amazon), and large app store operators (such as Apple and Google). The vast majority of businesses are not subject to the FDBR's consumer-facing rights framework.

Consumer rights under the FDBR include: the right to opt out of targeted advertising, the sale of personal data, and profiling with significant effects; the right to opt out of collection or processing of sensitive data (including precise geolocation); and the right to opt out of collection of personal data through voice or facial recognition technology. The FDBR's definition of "sensitive data" includes data revealing racial or ethnic origin, religious beliefs, mental or physical health conditions, genetic or biometric data, precise geolocation, immigration status, sexual orientation, and data of known children.

A notable cross-applicability provision requires **all businesses** (regardless of whether they meet the $1 billion revenue threshold) to obtain consent before selling sensitive personal data. This creates a compliance obligation for a broader universe of companies with respect to sensitive data sales specifically.

Enforcement is by the Florida Attorney General, with civil penalties up to **$50,000 per violation** — the highest single-violation cap of the three laws. Penalties may reach **$150,000** for violations involving a child under 18 (where the controller has actual knowledge of the child's age) and for failures to delete or correct personal data or continued sale of personal data after a consumer has exercised an opt-out right. There is no private right of action.

The official enrolled text is available at [flsenate.gov](https://www.flsenate.gov/Session/Bill/2023/262/BillText/er/HTML).

### Oregon Consumer Privacy Act (OCPA / SB 619)

The OCPA is codified at ORS 646A.570 through 646A.589. It applies to entities that conduct business in Oregon or produce products or services targeted to Oregon residents and that, during a calendar year, either: (a) control or process personal data of **100,000 or more Oregon consumers** (excluding data processed solely to complete payment transactions), or (b) control or process personal data of **25,000 or more Oregon consumers** while deriving 25% or more of annual gross revenues from the sale of personal data.

The OCPA stands out for two unique provisions not found in other state laws as of its effective date:

**1. Inclusion of "derived data" in the definition of personal data.** The OCPA explicitly includes inferred and derived data in its definition of personal data, as noted by the [Future of Privacy Forum](https://fpf.org/blog/were-on-to-oregon-sixth-state-privacy-law-of-2023-creates-new-consumer-rights-and-protections/) and [DLA Piper](https://www.dlapiper.com/en/insights/publications/2023/07/oregon-enacts-latest-comprehensive-consumer-data-privacy-law). This means that data inferred from behavioral patterns or algorithmic modeling — if reasonably linkable to a consumer — falls within the scope of consumer rights (access, correction, deletion, portability). No other comprehensive state privacy law at the time of OCPA's passage included derived data in this manner.

**2. Third-party disclosure list right.** Oregon consumers may request a list of specific named third parties to which the controller has disclosed their personal data (or any personal data). Most other state laws require only disclosure of the *categories* of third parties. The OCPA's requirement for specific named third parties creates significant operational complexity for controllers, who must maintain and produce granular disclosure records on request.

Oregon's OCPA also applies to **nonprofit organizations** — unlike most other state privacy laws that exempt nonprofits. However, nonprofits received an extended compliance deadline of **July 1, 2025**, one full year after for-profit entities. [Oregon and Colorado are the only two states](https://www.constangy.com/constangy-cyber-advisor/countdown-to-3-new-data-privacy-laws-texas-oregon-florida) with comprehensive privacy laws that apply to nonprofits.

Consumer rights under the OCPA mirror other Virginia-model laws: right to access, correct, delete, and obtain a portable copy of personal data; right to opt out of targeted advertising, sale of personal data, and profiling with significant effects. For sensitive data processing, Oregon requires an opt-in consent model.

Enforcement is by the Oregon Attorney General exclusively. The law included a **30-day cure period** for alleged violations, which sunset on January 1, 2026. There is no private right of action. The official enrolled text is at [olis.oregonlegislature.gov](https://olis.oregonlegislature.gov/liz/2023R1/Downloads/MeasureDocument/SB619/Enrolled).

### Texas Data Privacy and Security Act (TDPSA / HB 4)

The TDPSA is codified at Chapter 541 of the Texas Business and Commerce Code. Its applicability threshold is the most expansive of any U.S. state comprehensive privacy law: it applies to entities that conduct business in Texas or produce products or services consumed by Texas residents, **process or engage in the sale of personal data**, and are **not a small business** as defined by the U.S. Small Business Administration. Critically, there is no revenue threshold and no minimum number of consumers whose data must be processed for the law to apply.

As analyzed by [Goodwin Procter](https://www.goodwinlaw.com/en/insights/publications/2024/06/alerts-practices-dpc-texas-new-privacy-law-goes-into-effect-enforcement) and [Bass, Berry & Sims](https://www.bassberry.com/news/texas-data-privacy-and-security-act/), the TDPSA's breadth means that medium-sized businesses that would fall below the thresholds of comparable state laws may still be subject to TDPSA requirements — except that small businesses (as defined by the SBA) are categorically excluded from the primary framework. Notably, small businesses that sell sensitive personal data must still obtain consumer consent.

Consumer rights under the TDPSA include: the right to confirm whether the controller is processing personal data; the right to access, correct, and delete personal data; the right to obtain data in a portable format; and the right to opt out of targeted advertising, sale of personal data, and certain profiling. Controllers must respond within 45 days, with an optional 45-day extension. A notable delayed effective date applies to **authorized agent opt-out requests**: consumers cannot designate an authorized agent for opt-out requests until **January 1, 2025**.

For sensitive data, the TDPSA requires **opt-in consent** before processing. The definition of sensitive data encompasses precise geolocation data, racial or ethnic origin, religious beliefs, mental or physical health diagnoses, sexual orientation, citizenship and immigration status, genetic or biometric data, and the personal data of children under 13.

Texas Attorney General Ken Paxton announced in June 2024 the formation of a dedicated **privacy enforcement team** within the Consumer Protection Division, described by the AG as among the largest state privacy enforcement teams in the country. The team's mandate includes enforcement of the TDPSA, the Texas Data Broker Act, and the Capture or Use of Biometric Identifier Act (CUBI), as well as federal privacy laws including COPPA and HIPAA. In **January 2025**, the Texas AG filed the first enforcement action under the TDPSA, [suing Allstate and its subsidiary Arity](https://www.texasattorneygeneral.gov/news/releases/attorney-general-ken-paxton-sues-allstate-and-arity-unlawfully-collecting-using-and-selling-over-45) for allegedly collecting, using, and selling the location data of Texas drivers without authorization. Per [Section 541.155 of the Texas Business and Commerce Code](https://law.justia.com/codes/texas/2023/business-and-commerce-code/title-11/subtitle-c/chapter-541/subchapter-d/section-541-155/), civil penalties under the TDPSA can reach **$7,500 for each violation**; there is no private right of action, and there is a **30-day cure period** (with no sunset date, unlike Oregon). The official enrolled text is at [capitol.texas.gov](https://capitol.texas.gov/tlodocs/88R/billtext/html/HB00004F.htm).

## Impact Assessment [MEDIUM confidence]

### Who Is Affected

The three laws collectively expand the universe of covered organizations significantly, though in different ways:

- **Large technology platforms** are the primary target of Florida's FDBR. Companies that derive substantial revenue from online advertising, operate major app stores, or operate smart speaker systems with voice assistants must assess FDBR applicability immediately if they have Florida users.
- **Mid-market and larger businesses** operating in Oregon or Texas face broad compliance obligations regardless of whether they have privacy programs tailored to California or Virginia standards. Texas's SBA small business exclusion is the only carve-out for smaller companies; Oregon's threshold of 100,000 consumers captures companies well below Fortune 500 scale.
- **Nonprofit organizations** operating in Oregon must comply by July 1, 2025 — an unusual requirement not present in most other state regimes.
- **Businesses with AI/inference pipelines** face heightened obligations under Oregon's OCPA due to the derived data definition. Any entity that generates inferences or model outputs linked to Oregon consumers must extend data subject rights to that derived data.

### Compliance Requirements and Timelines

Per the [Dickinson Wright analysis](https://www.dickinson-wright.com/news-alerts/client-alert-jodka-better-3-new-state-privacy-laws) that sourced this finding, organizations with existing privacy programs aligned to California, Colorado, Connecticut, Utah, or Virginia laws will not need to overhaul their programs entirely. However, law-specific gaps must be closed, including:

- **Privacy notice updates**: Each law requires specific disclosures. Oregon requires disclosure of the ability to obtain a named third-party list. Texas requires disclosure of the categories of personal data collected and the purposes for processing.
- **Data processing agreements**: New or updated data processing addenda with vendors may be required for TDPSA and OCPA compliance.
- **Consent mechanisms for sensitive data**: All three laws require opt-in consent for sensitive data processing. Organizations must audit whether existing consent flows satisfy each state's requirements.
- **Consumer request workflows**: Response timelines are consistent (45 days, with extensions), but Oregon's derived data inclusion and third-party disclosure list requirements demand additional operational procedures.
- **Children's data**: All three laws incorporate heightened requirements for data of minors. Florida's FDBR imposes tripled penalty exposure for violations involving children under 18.

### Enforcement Outlook

The Texas AG's formation of a dedicated privacy enforcement team and the filing of the first TDPSA enforcement action in January 2025 signals that TDPSA enforcement is active and likely to expand. Oregon's cure period sunsetted January 1, 2026, removing the pre-enforcement safety valve for Oregon violations. Florida's FDBR, while narrow in applicability, carries the highest per-violation penalty ceiling and is expected to focus primarily on the handful of large tech platforms within its scope.

## Action Items

- Assess whether your organization meets Florida's FDBR applicability criteria (revenue > $1 billion AND one of the three technical conditions). If yes, a dedicated FDBR compliance review is required prior to enforcement.
- Review your organization's applicability under Oregon's OCPA (100,000 Oregon consumers or 25,000 consumers + 25% data-sale revenue). If applicable, specifically audit whether AI/ML inference outputs or behavioral models are linked to Oregon consumers and may constitute "derived data" subject to access and deletion rights.
- For Oregon: implement a process to respond to consumer requests for a list of specific named third parties to whom their personal data has been disclosed — this is operationally more demanding than category-level disclosure required by other state laws.
- Assess Texas TDPSA applicability: if you conduct business in Texas and are not an SBA-defined small business, you are almost certainly covered. Confirm whether small-business exclusion applies using current SBA size standards for your NAICS code.
- Update privacy notices for all three states to address law-specific disclosure requirements.
- Review consent mechanisms for sensitive data processing against each state's opt-in requirements.
- Establish a 45-day consumer request response tracking system that covers TDPSA and OCPA, in addition to any existing state law workflows.
- Monitor Texas AG enforcement actions for signals about enforcement priorities under the TDPSA, particularly in advertising technology, data broker, and biometric data sectors.
