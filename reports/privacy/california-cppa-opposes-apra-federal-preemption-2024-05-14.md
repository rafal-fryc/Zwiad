# CPPA Board Opposes American Privacy Rights Act; Defers CPRA Rulemaking Modifications

**Jurisdiction:** California | **Category:** Privacy | **Date:** May 14, 2024

## Executive Summary [MEDIUM confidence]

In April and May 2024, the California Privacy Protection Agency (CPPA) board took two significant actions that shaped California's data privacy landscape. First, on April 16, 2024, the CPPA released an official letter to Congress outlining its opposition to the American Privacy Rights Act (APRA) discussion draft, arguing that as drafted the bill would weaken protections Californians already enjoy under the California Consumer Privacy Act (CCPA) and the California Privacy Rights Act (CPRA). Second, the board moved certain pending CPRA regulatory modifications — including rules on automated decisionmaking technology (ADMT), cybersecurity audits, and risk assessments — back to a pre-formal-rulemaking phase, delaying the timeline for those regulations to take effect. Together, these actions represent the CPPA staking out a strong position against federal preemption of state privacy law while simultaneously managing the pacing of its own rulemaking agenda.

## Background [HIGH confidence]

### The California Privacy Regulatory Framework

California's consumer privacy regime is among the most comprehensive in the United States. The [California Consumer Privacy Act (CCPA)](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=CIV&sectionNum=1798.100.), enacted in 2018, established baseline rights for Californians including the right to know, delete, and opt out of the sale of personal information. In November 2020, California voters passed [Proposition 24](https://cppa.ca.gov/regulations/), the California Privacy Rights Act (CPRA), which significantly strengthened those protections and created the CPPA as an independent enforcement agency — the first dedicated state privacy regulator in the United States.

The CPPA has been engaged in a multi-year rulemaking process to implement the CPRA's new requirements. As of early 2024, outstanding regulatory topics included cybersecurity audit requirements, privacy risk assessment obligations, and rules governing automated decisionmaking technology (ADMT). The [March 8, 2024 CPPA board meeting](https://cppa.ca.gov/meetings/materials/20240308_transcript.pdf) featured a 3-2 vote to continue work on revised ADMT and risk assessment draft regulations, with formal rulemaking anticipated to begin in July 2024.

### The American Privacy Rights Act

In April 2024, a bipartisan proposal for the first comprehensive U.S. federal data privacy law — the [American Privacy Rights Act (APRA)](https://www.congress.gov/bill/118th-congress/house-bill/8818) — was unveiled by Senator Maria Cantwell (D-WA) and Representative Cathy McMorris Rodgers (R-WA). The APRA was subsequently introduced as [H.R. 8818](https://www.congress.gov/bill/118th-congress/house-bill/8818) in the 118th Congress. It aimed to establish federal minimum data privacy rights, create a national data security standard, and empower individuals to sue for violations.

A core — and deeply controversial — feature of the bill was its preemption clause. Section 20 of the APRA expressly sought to preempt state privacy laws, establishing a uniform national ceiling rather than the floor-plus approach seen in statutes like HIPAA, GLBA, and FCRA, where states have historically been permitted to enact stronger protections.

## Detailed Analysis [MEDIUM confidence]

### CPPA's April 16, 2024 Opposition Letter

On April 16, 2024, the CPPA published an [official letter to the chairs of the House Energy & Commerce Committee and the Innovation, Data, and Commerce Subcommittee](https://cppa.ca.gov/announcements/2024/20240416.html) outlining the agency's specific objections to the APRA discussion draft. The letter framed the CPPA's position as support for federal privacy legislation in principle, but opposition to the specific form APRA had taken.

Key objections raised by the CPPA included:

**Federal preemption as a ceiling, not a floor.** The CPPA argued that unlike prior federal privacy statutes (HIPAA, GLBA, FCRA), APRA would not set a minimum baseline upon which states could build. Instead, it would preempt nearly all state privacy laws, including the CCPA and the [California Delete Act](https://oag.ca.gov/news/press-releases/attorney-general-bonta-congressional-leaders-federal-data-privacy-law-should-set). The agency's position, echoed by California Attorney General Rob Bonta and a coalition of 15 state attorneys general, was that such preemption would "freeze" privacy protections in place for decades, preventing states from adapting to new technologies or harms.

**Elimination of the CPPA's independent authority.** The APRA draft proposed vesting the Federal Trade Commission with primary enforcement responsibility, while simultaneously removing the CPPA's authority to bring enforcement actions or conduct audits against non-compliant businesses. The CPPA characterized this as overriding the will of California voters, who in 2020 specifically authorized the creation of an independent state privacy regulator.

**Gaps in sensitive data coverage.** The APRA's definition of "sensitive covered data" did not include sexual orientation, union membership, or immigration status — categories explicitly protected under California law. The CPPA argued these omissions would leave significant populations exposed.

**Data broker protections weakened.** Rather than granting Californians a direct right to delete data held by data brokers (as the California Delete Act does), the APRA's approach provided only a "Do Not Collect" mechanism, which the CPPA argued was substantially weaker in practical effect.

Following the April letter, the CPPA formalized its opposition on June 26, 2024, publishing a [full statement opposing the American Privacy Rights Act](https://cppa.ca.gov/announcements/2024/20240626.html) and an accompanying [opposition letter to Congress regarding H.R. 8818](https://cppa.ca.gov/pdf/cppa_letter_opposing_apra.pdf).

### Deferral of CPRA Regulatory Modifications

Concurrent with the APRA debate, the CPPA board moved several pending CPRA implementing regulations to the back burner. The board had been working on draft rules covering:

- Cybersecurity audit obligations for businesses meeting specified thresholds
- Privacy risk assessment requirements
- Rules governing automated decisionmaking technology (ADMT), including profiling
- Updates to harmonize existing CCPA regulations with CPRA amendments
- Special rules for insurance companies

As noted by [Alston & Bird's privacy blog](https://www.alstonprivacy.com/cppa-board-declines-to-advance-ccaa-regulations-to-formal-rulemaking-cppa-highlights-enforcement-priorities/), the CPPA board declined to advance these regulations to formal rulemaking in mid-2024, in part due to internal disagreement over the scope of ADMT obligations. Board Member Alastair MacTaggart, the primary funder of Proposition 24, publicly criticized the breadth of the draft regulations. The CPPA board tasked staff with narrowing the scope of the proposed rules, with a revised package expected at a later board meeting.

The CPPA held [three statewide pre-rulemaking stakeholder sessions in April–May 2024](https://cppa.ca.gov/announcements/2024/20240424.html) to gather preliminary public input on the ADMT, risk assessment, and cybersecurity audit regulations, demonstrating continued engagement even while formal rulemaking was delayed.

### Status of the APRA in Congress

The APRA failed to advance to a House floor vote before the 118th Congress adjourned at the end of 2024. The bill was not reintroduced in the 119th Congress, leaving the federal privacy landscape unchanged. States, including California, retained their independent consumer privacy regimes.

## Impact Assessment [MEDIUM confidence]

### Impact on California Businesses

For businesses operating in California, the CPPA's opposition to the APRA and the deferral of CPRA implementing regulations had several practical effects:

- **No short-term regulatory relief.** Despite the deferral of formal rulemaking on ADMT and cybersecurity audit rules, existing CCPA/CPRA obligations (and the CPPA's enforcement authority, restored by the [February 2024 Court of Appeal decision](https://www.fbm.com/publications/court-reinstates-cppa-enforcement-authority-and-confirms-no-delay-necessary-for-enforcement-of-future-ccpa-regulations/)) remained fully in effect.
- **Uncertainty over ADMT and cybersecurity requirements.** Businesses with ADMT-dependent products (recommendation engines, credit scoring, hiring algorithms) faced continued uncertainty about the specific compliance obligations that would eventually be imposed under the CPRA's ADMT framework.
- **APRA preemption risk eliminated (for now).** The APRA's failure to pass meant businesses did not need to immediately assess whether their CPPA compliance programs would be superseded by a weaker federal standard.

### Multi-State and National Compliance Significance

The CPPA's opposition to federal preemption carries significance beyond California. As [noted by multiple law firm analyses](https://www.clym.io/blog/apra-faces-opposition-due-to-privacy-concerns), California's CCPA/CPRA framework has served as a de facto national standard for companies that do business across state lines. Any federal law that preempted the CCPA without maintaining equivalent protections would create a net reduction in privacy rights for tens of millions of Americans.

The 15-state coalition formed by California AG Rob Bonta, opposing APRA's preemption approach, signals that state-level opposition to federal preemption of privacy law is not uniquely Californian — it represents a structural tension between state and federal privacy governance that will persist in future federal legislative attempts.

### Compliance Timeline Considerations

- **Existing CPRA regulations:** Fully in effect as of March 2024 (following restoration of enforcement authority by the Court of Appeal).
- **ADMT and cybersecurity audit regulations:** Timeline was pushed back from the originally anticipated July 2024 formal rulemaking start. The CPPA board declined to advance the regulations to formal rulemaking on July 16, 2024. A Notice of Proposed Rulemaking was ultimately issued on November 22, 2024, opening the formal comment period. Final regulations on ADMT, cybersecurity audits, and risk assessments were adopted by the CPPA board on July 24, 2025, approved by the Office of Administrative Law on September 22, 2025, and take effect January 1, 2026 (with phased compliance deadlines extending through 2030).
- **APRA:** Did not pass the 118th Congress; no federal preemption occurred.

## Action Items

- Monitor CPPA rulemaking on ADMT, risk assessments, and cybersecurity audits — the CPPA CCPA updates page (cppa.ca.gov/regulations/ccpa_updates.html) publishes current draft status.
- Assess exposure of any ADMT-dependent products or services (recommendation engines, scoring algorithms, behavioral advertising) against the CPPA's draft ADMT framework, even while regulations remain in pre-formal-rulemaking status.
- Track federal privacy legislation in the 119th Congress — while APRA did not pass, the question of a national privacy standard with preemption provisions is likely to resurface. The CPPA's stated position (floor-not-ceiling) should inform internal analysis of any new federal proposals.
- Ensure CPRA compliance programs remain current — the deferral of new regulations does not reduce obligations under existing CCPA/CPRA rules or the CPPA's data minimization enforcement advisory issued in April 2024.
- Data brokers operating in California should maintain compliance with the California Delete Act, which the CPPA defended in its APRA opposition letters as a key protection that APRA would have undermined.
