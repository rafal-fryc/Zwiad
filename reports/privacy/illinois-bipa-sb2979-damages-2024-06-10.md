# Illinois Legislature Modifies Damages Rule Under BIPA (SB 2979 House Passage)

**Jurisdiction:** Illinois | **Category:** Privacy | **Date:** 2024-06-10

## Executive Summary [HIGH confidence]

On May 16, 2024, the Illinois House of Representatives voted 81-30 to approve [Senate Bill 2979](https://www.ilga.gov/legislation/BillStatus.asp?DocNum=2979&GAID=17&DocTypeID=SB&LegId=152094&SessionID=112&GA=103), sending the bill to Governor J.B. Pritzker following the Senate's earlier 46-13 vote on April 11, 2024. SB 2979 amends the Biometric Information Privacy Act (BIPA) — the nation's most litigated biometric privacy statute — to replace per-scan damages accrual with a single per-person recovery standard. The amendment directly overrides the Illinois Supreme Court's 2023 ruling in *Cothron v. White Castle System, Inc.*, which had exposed a single employer with approximately 9,500 employees to potential class-wide liability exceeding $17 billion. Governor Pritzker subsequently signed the bill into law on August 2, 2024, as [Public Act 103-0769](https://www.ilga.gov/Legislation/publicacts/view/103-0769), effective immediately. The legislation marks the first amendment to BIPA since the statute's 2008 enactment and represents the most significant structural change to the law's enforcement regime in its sixteen-year history.

## Background [HIGH confidence]

### Illinois BIPA: Statutory Architecture

Illinois enacted BIPA in 2008 as one of the first state laws in the nation to require private entities to obtain informed written consent before collecting biometric identifiers such as fingerprints, facial geometry, retina scans, hand scans, and voiceprints. [Section 15 of BIPA (740 ILCS 14/15)](https://www.ilga.gov/Legislation/ILCS/Articles?ActID=3004&ChapterID=57&Print=True) imposes substantive obligations: entities must provide notice, obtain written release, establish a publicly available retention schedule, and refrain from profiting from biometric data. BIPA's defining feature is its [Section 20 private right of action (740 ILCS 14/20)](https://law.justia.com/codes/illinois/chapter-740/act-740-ilcs-14/), which authorizes statutory damages of $1,000 per negligent violation and $5,000 per intentional or reckless violation, plus reasonable attorneys' fees and injunctive relief. Unlike most state privacy statutes that vest enforcement exclusively in the attorney general, BIPA's private right of action created a robust plaintiff class action ecosystem.

BIPA litigation expanded dramatically after the Illinois Supreme Court's 2019 decision in [*Rosenbach v. Six Flags Entertainment Corp.*](https://law.justia.com/cases/illinois/supreme-court/2019/123186.html), which held that a plaintiff need not allege actual injury beyond a bare statutory violation to have standing. That holding transformed BIPA into a litigation vehicle capable of generating large class actions based solely on technical non-compliance with notice and consent requirements.

### The *Cothron v. White Castle* Decision

The damages exposure problem crystallized in the Illinois Supreme Court's February 17, 2023 decision in [*Cothron v. White Castle System, Inc.*, 2023 IL 128004](https://law.justia.com/cases/illinois/supreme-court/2023/128004.html). Latrina Cothron, a White Castle manager, alleged that White Castle required employees to scan their fingerprints to access pay stubs and computers without obtaining proper consent under BIPA until 2018, approximately a decade after the statute took effect.

The Illinois Supreme Court held, in response to a certified question from the Seventh Circuit, that "a claim accrues under the Act with every scan or transmission of biometric identifiers or biometric information without prior informed consent." Under this per-scan accrual standard, White Castle faced potential liability exceeding [$17 billion for approximately 9,500 current and former employees](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/business_law/resources/business-law-today/2023-may/illinois-supreme-court-finds-white-castle-could-face-up-to-17b-in-damages/) who may have scanned fingerprints hundreds of times each. The majority acknowledged the "potentially annihilative" nature of such damages but declined to limit them, instead inviting the Illinois General Assembly to address the policy dimensions of the damages question.

The Illinois Supreme Court denied rehearing in *Cothron* on July 18, 2023, and the case settled shortly thereafter. The ruling triggered immediate legislative activity, with industry groups and employers pressing for statutory intervention to prevent similarly catastrophic exposure across the hundreds of then-pending BIPA class actions.

### Legislative Introduction

Senator Bill Cunningham introduced SB 2979 on January 31, 2024, directly responding to the *Cothron* court's explicit invitation for legislative action. The bill was the product of negotiations among business groups, plaintiffs' attorneys, and legislative leadership, and faced limited public opposition given that the *Cothron* damages framework had been widely criticized as producing outcomes disproportionate to any cognizable privacy harm.

## Detailed Analysis [HIGH confidence]

### Core Statutory Change: Per-Person Recovery Standard

SB 2979 amends [Section 20 of BIPA (740 ILCS 14/20)](https://law.justia.com/codes/illinois/chapter-740/act-740-ilcs-14/) by inserting two new subsections that address collection and disclosure violations separately.

**Collection violations (Section 15(b)):** Under the amendment, a private entity that "in more than one instance, collects, captures, purchases, receives through trade, or otherwise obtains the same biometric identifier or biometric information from the same person using the same method of collection" in violation of Section 15(b) has committed "a single violation" for which the aggrieved person is entitled to "at most, one recovery." ([WilmerHale analysis](https://www.wilmerhale.com/en/insights/blogs/wilmerhale-privacy-and-cybersecurity-law/20240605-illinois-legislature-modifies-damages-rule-under-bipa))

**Disclosure violations (Section 15(d)):** Similarly, a private entity that discloses the same biometric identifier or information from the same person to the same recipient using the same method in violation of Section 15(d) has committed a single violation entitling the aggrieved person to at most one recovery, regardless of the number of times the disclosure occurred. ([Nixon Peabody analysis](https://www.nixonpeabody.com/insights/alerts/2024/05/20/bipa-reform-bill-addressing-one-time-claim-accrual-and-electronic-signature-passess))

The practical effect is dramatic. Under the *Cothron* per-scan framework, an employee whose fingerprint was scanned 1,500 times without consent could recover up to $7.5 million (1,500 × $5,000 for intentional violations). Under the amended per-person framework, that same employee's maximum recovery is $5,000.

### Electronic Signature Amendment

SB 2979 also modernizes BIPA's consent mechanism by amending the definition of "written release" to include an "electronic signature," defined as "an electronic sound, symbol, or process attached to or logically associated with a record and executed or adopted by a person with the intent to sign the record." This aligns BIPA's consent requirement with standard digital workflow practices and eliminates uncertainty about whether electronic onboarding systems, digital kiosks, or mobile application consent flows satisfy the "written" requirement. ([Shook, Hardy & Bacon analysis](https://www.shb.com/intelligence/client-alerts/pds/may-2024-bipa-amendment-wolfe-searle))

### Scope Limitations in the Amendment

The amendment's per-person standard applies specifically to the same biometric identifier collected from the same person using the same method of collection. This framing has several potential interpretive limitations:

- Different collection methods (e.g., a fingerprint scanner versus facial recognition software) would likely generate separate potential recoveries even for the same individual.
- Disclosures to different recipients would likely each support a separate per-person recovery.
- The amendment does not address violations involving different types of biometric identifiers from the same person.

As noted by [Perkins Coie](https://perkinscoie.com/insights/blog/illinois-plan-limit-privacy-violation-damages-opens-new-door), some practitioners anticipated that plaintiffs' attorneys would respond by increasing focus on cases involving multiple collection methods or disclosure to multiple recipients, where the per-scan logic under *Cothron* continues to offer heightened exposure.

### Post-Enactment Retroactivity Litigation

The amendment's text did not address its temporal scope, which prompted immediate litigation in pending BIPA class actions. Plaintiffs argued the amendment applied only prospectively; defendants argued for retroactive application that would reduce damages in existing lawsuits. Multiple federal district courts initially sided with plaintiffs on prospective application only. The Seventh Circuit subsequently resolved the issue by holding that the amendment applies retroactively to all pending cases, though that ruling postdates this finding's scope — see the [related report on retroactivity](reports/privacy/illinois-bipa-7th-circuit-retroactivity-2026-04-12.md).

## Impact Assessment [HIGH confidence]

### Reduced Class Action Exposure

The amendment fundamentally reshapes BIPA litigation economics. Under the per-scan framework, an employer with 10,000 employees using fingerprint time clocks — each scanning three times per day over two years (roughly 2.2 million scans) — faced theoretical exposure of $11 billion for intentional violations. Under the per-person framework, that same employer's maximum exposure is $50 million ($5,000 × 10,000 people). While still significant, the revised exposure is commercially manageable and negotiable, removing the "bet-the-company" dynamics that characterized pre-amendment BIPA litigation.

### Settlement Dynamics

[WilmerHale observed](https://www.wilmerhale.com/en/insights/blogs/wilmerhale-privacy-and-cybersecurity-law/20240605-illinois-legislature-modifies-damages-rule-under-bipa) that the change would allow BIPA defendants to exercise more leverage during settlement negotiations, as the threat of catastrophic per-scan damages had historically driven large settlements irrespective of underlying liability merit. The amendment is expected to reduce the volume of BIPA class filings over time, though the statute's private right of action, notice requirements, and per-person liability exposure remain intact incentives for litigation.

### Affected Entities and Industries

Any private entity collecting or disclosing biometric data of Illinois residents remains subject to BIPA's full substantive obligations. The amendment most directly benefits:

- **Employers** using biometric time and attendance systems (the largest single category of BIPA defendants)
- **Technology companies** providing biometric authentication products used by Illinois employees or consumers
- **Retailers and hospitality companies** deploying facial recognition for security or loss prevention
- **Social media platforms** using facial geometry tagging features

### Ongoing Compliance Obligations

The amendment does not alter BIPA's core requirements. Private entities must still:

- Provide written notice of biometric data collection purposes and retention schedule
- Obtain informed written (including electronic) consent before collecting biometric identifiers
- Establish and comply with a publicly available biometric data retention and destruction schedule
- Refrain from profiting from biometric data
- Protect biometric data using reasonable security standards

Per-person statutory damages, attorneys' fees, and injunctive relief remain available for any violation of these obligations.

## Action Items

- **Audit consent workflows immediately** to confirm all biometric data collection is preceded by proper notice and written release. The amendment's electronic signature authorization now permits digital consent mechanisms — organizations not yet using electronic onboarding for biometric consent should update their processes.
- **Quantify existing litigation exposure** under the amended per-person standard. For any pending BIPA matters, recalculate maximum damages using the new framework and assess whether current settlement postures reflect the reduced ceiling.
- **Review biometric data retention policies** to ensure compliance with BIPA's schedule-based destruction requirements, which remain unchanged and continue to generate class exposure.
- **Train HR and compliance staff** on BIPA's substantive requirements, emphasizing that the amendment reduces damages but does not affect liability for unconsented collection or disclosure.
- **Monitor retroactivity developments** in Illinois state courts. While the Seventh Circuit has ruled on retroactive application to federal cases, Illinois state courts retain independent authority to interpret the amendment's temporal scope for cases pending in state court.
- **Assess multi-method collection scenarios** for heightened risk: if your organization uses multiple distinct biometric collection methods for the same employees or consumers, legal analysis of whether each method generates a separate per-person recovery under the amendment's "same method of collection" qualifier is warranted.
