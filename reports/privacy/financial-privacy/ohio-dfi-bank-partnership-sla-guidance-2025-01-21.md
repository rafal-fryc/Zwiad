# Ohio DFI Bank Partnership Alert: AFC Challenges Small Loan Act Licensing Reversal

**Jurisdiction:** Ohio | **Category:** Privacy / Financial Privacy | **Date:** January 21, 2025

## Executive Summary [MEDIUM confidence]

In late December 2024, the Ohio Department of Financial Institutions (DFI) issued a "Bank Partnership Alert" entitled "Important Bank Partnership and 2025 Licensure Information," asserting that non-bank entities compensated for arranging consumer loans of $5,000 or less must obtain a license under the Ohio Small Loan Act (ORC Chapter 1321) — even when the loans are originated by federally insured banks. This represented a complete reversal from the DFI's long-standing prior interpretation, which had expressly told fintech companies registered under the General Loan Law that they did not need an SLA license for bank-partnership activities. On January 14, 2025, the [American Fintech Council (AFC)](https://fintechcouncil.org) submitted a formal letter calling on the DFI to rescind the guidance or grant a 120-day compliance extension. As of October 31, 2025, the DFI reversed course, withdrawing its prior interpretation and declaring it would not require non-bank entities to obtain SLA licenses for arranging bank loans. This sequence illustrates the risks that unannounced mid-stream guidance reversals pose to bank-fintech partnership models operating under state licensing frameworks.

## Background [MEDIUM confidence]

### The Ohio Small Loan Act Framework

The Ohio Small Loan Act (ORC Chapter 1321) requires any person who engages in the business of lending money in amounts of $5,000 or less, or who contracts for or receives interest and charges greater than an unlicensed lender would be permitted to charge, to first obtain a license from the Division of Financial Institutions. The statute is administered by the [Ohio DFI](https://dfi.ohio.gov), which has broad authority to interpret its application to new business models.

Ohio also maintains a separate General Loan Law (GLL), which governs a different category of consumer lending. Fintech companies operating in bank-partnership models had historically registered under the GLL rather than the SLA, on the basis of prior DFI communications indicating that their arrangement activities were already covered under the GLL. This prior interpretation specifically informed fintech companies that they did not need to obtain a separate SLA license even when their activities involved arranging consumer loans below the $5,000 threshold in partnership with federally insured banks.

### Bank-Fintech Partnership Models

Bank-fintech partnerships — sometimes called "marketplace lending" or "bank-as-a-service" models — involve a federally chartered or state-chartered bank that originates loans using the fintech company as a front-end platform, marketer, or loan arranger. The originating bank's federal charter preempts state usury laws under the National Bank Act or the Federal Deposit Insurance Act. However, the licensing status of the non-bank partner — and whether it independently requires a state license as a "loan arranger" — has been a persistent and evolving question in state regulatory law.

This model has faced increasing scrutiny at the state level. Massachusetts brought an enforcement action against EasyPay Finance under a "true lender" theory in 2024. Other states have enacted or proposed laws specifically regulating earned wage access providers and other fintech-adjacent lending products that fall into gaps in existing licensing frameworks.

### The December 2024 Guidance Reversal

Around Christmas 2024, the Ohio DFI issued the "Important Bank Partnership and 2025 Licensure Information" alert without prior notice or comment period. The guidance asserted that non-bank entities that arrange consumer loans of $5,000 or less in exchange for compensation must obtain an SLA license, regardless of whether the underlying loans are originated by a federally insured bank. This position expressly departed from prior DFI communications and reversed the Department's previous stance that GLL-registered fintechs did not need SLA licenses for bank-partnership activities. The DFI issued expanded guidance and FAQs in January 2025 that reinforced this position and made clear that license requirements would apply to many parties engaged in arranging or brokering Ohio consumer loans.

## Detailed Analysis [MEDIUM confidence]

### The AFC's January 14, 2025 Letter

On January 14, 2025, the [American Fintech Council](https://fintechcouncil.org/press-releases/american-fintech-council-afc-urges-additional-clarity-and-review-of-ohio-department-of-financial-institutions-guidance-on-responsible-bank-partnerships) submitted a formal letter to the Ohio DFI, urging the Department to re-examine its bank partnership guidance. The AFC's principal concerns and requests included:

**Rescission or extension.** The AFC asked the DFI to rescind the guidance entirely or, at minimum, grant a 120-day extension to allow affected companies time to prepare compliance programs or license applications. The abrupt change — issued with no transition period — left fintech firms operating in Ohio with immediate exposure.

**Regulatory reversal without process.** The AFC emphasized that the guidance represented a fundamental departure from the DFI's own longstanding interpretation without notice-and-comment or other procedural safeguards. Prior DFI communications had explicitly told fintech companies that they did not need SLA licenses; companies relied on those representations in structuring their Ohio operations.

**Consumer access and market disruption.** The AFC argued that the new licensing requirement, if enforced, would render Ohio operations economically unviable for many fintech lenders, removing responsible credit options from Ohio consumers and reducing competition. This framing is consistent with the AFC's broader advocacy position that bank-fintech partnership models expand credit access to underserved populations.

**Clarification on substantive compliance standards.** Beyond the licensing question, the AFC letter (per the [AFC advocacy filing](https://www.fintechcouncil.org/advocacy/letter-regarding-ohio-department-of-financial-institutions-recent-bank-partnership-alert)) sought clearer standards for the conditions under which fintech companies may participate in bank partnerships, including data privacy and security requirements for the bank-fintech data relationship, complaint handling roles and escalation procedures, and due diligence frameworks for ongoing partnership oversight. These requests acknowledged that even if licensing requirements were relaxed, the underlying compliance expectations for bank partners remain uncertain.

### Legal Basis for the DFI's Original Position

The DFI's December 2024 guidance rested on a reading of [ORC Section 1321.02](https://codes.ohio.gov/ohio-revised-code/section-1321.02), which requires licensure for persons who engage in the business of lending in amounts of $5,000 or less or who exact, contract for, or receive interest and charges in connection with such loans. The Department's position was that arranging or brokering loans on behalf of a bank, in exchange for compensation, constitutes "engaging in the business" of small loan lending within the meaning of the statute, notwithstanding the bank-charter origins of the loans.

The counter-argument, asserted by the AFC and industry participants, is that federally insured banks' loan origination authority preempts state law application to the bank's partners, or alternatively that a party who does not itself lend or charge interest cannot be subject to a lending license requirement. These arguments track the ongoing national debate over "true lender" rules and federal preemption in the marketplace lending context.

### The January 2025 Revised Alert and FAQ

The DFI did not immediately accept the AFC's request to rescind or extend the guidance. Instead, the [DFI published a revised bank partnership alert](https://www.dltlaw.com/blog/2025/01/ohio-regulator-publishes-revised-bank-partnership-alert/) in January 2025 that maintained the Department's position that SLA licensing was required, while providing additional detail on how the requirement applied to different partnership structures. A [FAQ document issued by the DFI on January 31, 2025](https://www.dltlaw.com/blog/2025/02/ohio-regulator-publishes-bank-partnership-guidance-faqs/) (with Dreher Tomkies' blog coverage appearing in February) addressed specific questions raised by industry participants. These supplementary documents did not grant the requested extension.

### The October 31, 2025 Reversal

On October 31, 2025, the Ohio DFI reversed course entirely. The updated guidance, summarized by [Sheppard Mullin / National Law Review](https://natlawreview.com/article/ohio-dfi-withdraws-prior-interpretation-suspends-licensing-bank-loan-arrangers) and [Mayer Brown](https://www.mayerbrown.com/en/insights/publications/2026/01/ohio-walks-back-prior-small-loan-act-guidance-eases-licensing-position-for-bank-partnerships), provides:

- **Licensing suspension**: The DFI will not require any non-bank entity compensated for arranging bank loans, in any amount, to obtain a license under the SLA unless or until further notice.
- **Zero-interest exemption**: Entities that make or arrange loans of $5,000 or less without charging interest are exempt from licensing for calendar years 2025 and 2026.
- **Retroactive non-enforcement**: The DFI will not pursue enforcement action against any entity that engaged in bank loan arrangement in calendar year 2025 without a valid SLA license, regardless of whether the entity pursued a license application following the December 2024–January 2025 guidance.
- **Section 1321.141 non-application**: The DFI committed not to enforce ORC Section 1321.141 (which prohibits SLA licensees from making certain small short-term loans and contains anti-evasion provisions) against entities whose activities, under the updated guidance, are not subject to licensure.

The reversal represents a complete vindication of the AFC's January 2025 position and restores the pre-December 2024 regulatory environment for bank-fintech partnerships in Ohio, at least on a non-enforcement basis.

## Impact Assessment [MEDIUM confidence]

### Who Is Affected

The December 2024–January 2025 guidance episode affected marketplace lending platforms, earned wage access providers, point-of-sale lending companies, and any other non-bank entity that arranges or brokers consumer loans of $5,000 or less in Ohio in partnership with a federally insured bank. The October 2025 reversal provides relief for all such entities, eliminating near-term licensing exposure and removing the threat of enforcement action for 2025 activity.

Financial institutions that serve as originating bank partners in these arrangements are affected indirectly; the DFI's guidance also raised questions for bank due diligence obligations regarding their fintech partners' licensing status.

### Compliance Implications

Following the October 31, 2025 reversal:

- Non-bank entities that applied for SLA licenses following the December 2024 guidance but did not receive them may benefit from the DFI's non-enforcement commitment for 2025. The practical status of those applications should be confirmed directly with the DFI.
- Entities operating in Ohio under bank-partnership arrangements should review the updated guidance language for any conditions or limitations not captured in secondary reporting, as the DFI indicated the suspension applies "unless or until further notice" — signaling potential future changes.
- The zero-interest loan exemption (covering 2025–2026) appears designed for earned wage access products and other fee-based rather than interest-based lending arrangements. Entities in this space should verify whether their product structure qualifies.

### Broader Industry Significance

The Ohio DFI episode is one of several state-level challenges to bank-fintech partnership models in recent years. Massachusetts' "true lender" enforcement against EasyPay Finance, Colorado's Depository Institutions Deregulation and Monetary Control Act (DIDMCA) opt-out provisions, and various states' earned wage access licensing debates all reflect the same underlying regulatory tension: whether state consumer lending laws apply to the non-bank partner in a bank-as-a-service arrangement.

Ohio's reversal does not resolve this tension at a national level. It provides near-term relief for Ohio-domiciled partnerships but leaves open the question of whether the DFI will revisit its interpretation again in a future rulemaking cycle. The AFC's calls for notice-and-comment process before guidance reversals remain unaddressed.

## Action Items

- Monitor the Ohio DFI website (dfi.ohio.gov) for any further updates to the bank partnership guidance, which the October 2025 document characterized as effective "unless or until further notice."
- Fintech entities that submitted SLA license applications in response to the December 2024 guidance should follow up with the DFI to confirm the disposition of those applications and clarify whether withdrawal is appropriate.
- Entities offering zero-interest loan products in Ohio should document their product structure to confirm eligibility for the 2025–2026 interest-free exemption articulated in the October 2025 guidance.
- For companies with multistate bank-partnership operations, benchmark Ohio's updated position against pending licensing requirements in other states where similar guidance has been issued — including Massachusetts, California, and Colorado.
- Engage the AFC's ongoing advocacy efforts for nationwide clarity on bank-fintech partnership compliance standards, particularly data privacy, complaint handling, and third-party risk management expectations.
- Track any Ohio legislative response: if the DFI reverses again via guidance, industry participants may seek statutory clarification through the Ohio General Assembly.
