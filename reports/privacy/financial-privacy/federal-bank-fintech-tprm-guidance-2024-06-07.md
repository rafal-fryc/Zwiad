# Third-Party Risk Management in Bank-Fintech Partnerships: Federal Guidance and Industry Strategies

**Jurisdiction:** Federal | **Category:** Privacy / Financial Privacy | **Date:** June 2024

## Executive Summary [MEDIUM confidence]

In mid-2024, federal banking regulators intensified oversight of bank-fintech partnerships through a series of coordinated guidance publications and enforcement actions, creating significant compliance obligations for sponsor banks and their fintech partners. On May 3, 2024, the [OCC, FDIC, and Federal Reserve jointly released a Third-Party Risk Management Guide for Community Banks](https://www.occ.gov/news-issuances/news-releases/2024/nr-ia-2024-46.html), supplementing June 2023 interagency guidance. In July 2024, the same agencies issued a [joint statement on bank arrangements with third parties to deliver deposit products](https://www.fdic.gov/news/financial-institution-letters/2024/agencies-issue-statement-bank-arrangements-third-parties) and opened a public request for information (RFI) on bank-fintech arrangements. These actions were catalyzed in part by the April 2024 collapse of middleware provider Synapse Financial Technologies, which froze $265 million in end-user funds and exposed systemic gaps in sponsor bank oversight. Troutman Pepper's Payments Pros podcast, featuring Georgia Banking Company's Nathan Ottinger, highlights the practitioner-level strategies that well-managed sponsor banks are deploying in response to this environment: selective client onboarding, direct relationship cultivation, rigorous ongoing monitoring, and early investment in legal counsel.

## Background [HIGH confidence]

The bank-fintech partnership model — often called Banking-as-a-Service (BaaS) — allows non-bank fintech companies to offer deposit accounts, payments, and lending products by contracting with FDIC-insured sponsor banks. The fintech company handles the customer-facing experience while the sponsor bank provides the regulated infrastructure, holds the deposits, and remains legally accountable for compliance.

Federal banking agencies have addressed third-party risk management for decades, but the rapid growth of BaaS partnerships created novel oversight challenges. Each agency historically issued its own guidance, creating inconsistency. The [June 2023 Interagency Guidance on Third-Party Relationships: Risk Management](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2023/06/09/2023-12340/interagency-guidance-on-third-party-relationships-risk-management) — published by the OCC ([Bulletin 2023-17](https://www.occ.gov/news-issuances/bulletins/2023/bulletin-2023-17.html)), FDIC ([FIL-23-29](https://www.fdic.gov/news/financial-institution-letters/2023/fil23029.html)), and Federal Reserve — was the first unified interagency framework, replacing each agency's separate guidance. It established a five-stage risk management lifecycle (planning, due diligence, contract negotiation, ongoing monitoring, and termination) and applied consistent standards across banking organization types and sizes.

The 2023 guidance explicitly covered relationships with fintech companies and confirmed that banks cannot delegate their regulatory responsibility to third parties: a bank that engages a fintech to perform a regulated function remains fully accountable for compliance with applicable law. This principle became central to the 2024 enforcement wave.

Georgia Banking Company, headquartered in Atlanta, Georgia, operates a specialized Payments and Technology Banking division focused on serving payments companies, money transmitters, and fintechs. Georgia is notable as a payments hub — it hosts one of the largest concentrations of payment technology companies in the United States and is home to the [Merchant Acquirer Limited Purpose Bank (MALPB) charter](https://www.venable.com/insights/publications/2024/01/georgia-on-the-mind-an-explainer-for-payments) created under Georgia law, which allows non-bank merchant acquirers to obtain a special-purpose bank charter under the Georgia Department of Banking and Finance.

## Detailed Analysis [HIGH confidence]

### May 2024 Community Bank Third-Party Risk Management Guide

On May 3, 2024, the OCC, FDIC, and Federal Reserve released the [Third-Party Risk Management: A Guide for Community Banks](https://www.occ.gov/news-issuances/news-releases/2024/nr-ia-2024-46.html) (the "Community Bank Guide"). The guide serves as a companion document to the June 2023 interagency guidance, tailored to the operational realities and resource constraints of smaller institutions.

Key provisions of the Community Bank Guide include:

- **Risk-tiered oversight**: Banks must apply more rigorous risk management for third parties supporting "higher-risk activities," including critical activities — those whose failure would significantly impact the bank's operations, customers, or compliance posture.
- **Five-stage lifecycle**: The guide elaborates on each stage of the 2023 guidance's lifecycle framework with community bank-specific examples and checklists.
- **Due diligence depth**: During selection, banks must assess a third party's financial condition, operational resilience, information security controls, and ability to comply with applicable law — including BSA/AML, consumer protection, and data security requirements.
- **Contract provisions**: The guide specifies contract terms that banks should seek, including audit rights, data access, performance metrics, and termination-for-cause provisions.
- **Ongoing monitoring**: Banks must establish monitoring protocols proportional to the risk and criticality of the relationship, not simply at onboarding.

The guide explicitly disclaims creating new legal requirements; it clarifies existing supervisory expectations and offers illustrative practices. Nonetheless, it signals what examiners will assess.

### July 2024 Joint Statement and Request for Information

On July 25, 2024, the three agencies issued a [Joint Statement on Banks' Arrangements With Third Parties to Deliver Bank Deposit Products and Services](https://www.fdic.gov/news/financial-institution-letters/2024/agencies-issue-statement-bank-arrangements-third-parties), along with a [Request for Information](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2024/07/31/2024-16838/request-for-information-on-bank-fintech-arrangements-involving-banking-products-and-services) (RFI) published in the Federal Register. The joint statement identified specific supervisory concerns based on examination experience with BaaS arrangements:

- **Recordkeeping failures**: Sponsor banks that relied entirely on fintech intermediaries to maintain ledgers of beneficial owner positions in For Benefit Of (FBO) accounts without independent reconciliation capability.
- **BSA/AML delegation**: Banks that effectively delegated Customer Identification Program (CIP) and Customer Due Diligence (CDD) functions to fintech partners without adequate oversight or independent testing.
- **Operational resilience gaps**: Banks that lacked contingency plans to serve customers or transfer accounts if a fintech partner failed.
- **Multi-tier structures**: Arrangements involving a bank, a middleware provider, and multiple fintech clients creating opacity about where customer funds were actually held.

The RFI sought public comment on the full range of bank-fintech arrangements, including deposits, payments, and lending, as well as risk management practices and competition considerations. The [OCC issued companion bulletin 2024-21](https://www.occ.gov/news-issuances/bulletins/2024/bulletin-2024-21.html) and the [OCC issued bulletin 2024-20](https://www.occ.treas.gov/news-issuances/bulletins/2024/bulletin-2024-20.html) covering the joint statement on deposit products.

### The Synapse Collapse: Catalyst for Intensified Scrutiny

The April 2024 bankruptcy of [Synapse Financial Technologies](https://www.bankingdive.com/news/a-running-list-of-baas-banks-hit-with-consent-orders-in-2024/729121/) crystallized the risks that regulators had been warning about. Synapse served as a middleware layer between sponsor banks and fintech clients, maintaining the authoritative ledger of end-user fund positions across multiple bank partners. When Synapse collapsed, a shortfall of $65 million to $96 million emerged between what end users were owed and what partner banks held. Former FDIC Chair Jelena McWilliams, appointed as trustee, spent months attempting to reconcile the ledger discrepancy.

The Synapse episode accelerated regulatory action on several fronts. The FDIC proposed a recordkeeping rule in October 2024 requiring banks that hold custodial deposits for third parties to maintain their own records of beneficial owner positions — eliminating reliance on middleware ledger data. Evolve Bank & Trust, a major Synapse partner, received a consent order from the Federal Reserve in 2024 citing "unsafe and unsound banking practices." Other BaaS-active banks (Cross River, Blue Ridge, Coastal Community) faced heightened examination scrutiny.

### Industry Response: Georgia Banking Company's Framework

The [Troutman Pepper Payments Pros podcast episode](https://www.troutman.com/insights/third-party-risk-management-in-bank-fintech-partnerships-strategies-and-insights.html) (June 5, 2024) featuring Nathan Ottinger of Georgia Banking Company illustrates the operational posture that sophisticated sponsor banks are adopting in response to regulatory pressure. Ottinger highlighted several practices Georgia Banking Company employs:

- **Selective specialization**: Rather than pursuing broad BaaS growth, the bank focuses on specific payment types and industry segments where its team has deep expertise and can conduct meaningful oversight — deliberately avoiding new product categories or structures it cannot adequately monitor.
- **Direct client relationships**: The bank cultivates direct relationships with the beneficial owners of fintech clients rather than relying solely on the fintech intermediary's representations. This addresses the opacity concern raised in the joint statement.
- **Onboarding rigor**: Thorough due diligence at client onboarding — including assessment of the fintech's compliance infrastructure, legal counsel quality, and leadership track record — rather than relying on standardized questionnaires.
- **Ongoing portfolio management**: Active monitoring of existing clients, not just onboarding screening. This includes periodic reviews, transaction monitoring escalations, and direct communication with fintech management.
- **Legal counsel investment**: Ottinger emphasized the importance of fintechs retaining experienced legal counsel familiar with money transmission and banking law — noting that inadequate legal infrastructure at the fintech level creates risk exposure for the sponsor bank.

Georgia's regulatory environment is relevant context. The Georgia Department of Banking and Finance [regulates money transmitters](https://dbf.georgia.gov/money-service-businesses/money-transmitters) under O.C.G.A. §§ 7-1-680 through 7-1-698, requiring licensure, financial reserves, background checks, quarterly reporting, and five-year recordkeeping. Fintechs operating in Georgia that transmit money — or that partner with Georgia-chartered banks — must navigate both state licensing requirements and federal safety-and-soundness expectations.

### 2024 Enforcement Landscape

According to [Duane Morris's December 2024 summary of regulatory developments](https://www.duanemorris.com/articles/2024_regulatory_developments_bank_fintech_partnerships_1224.html), more than a quarter of FDIC enforcement actions in 2024 targeted sponsor banks involved in embedded finance partnerships. Common findings across enforcement actions included:

- Weaknesses in board and management oversight of fintech partnerships
- Failures in the third-party risk management program
- Deficiencies in BSA/AML compliance programs dependent on fintech-provided data
- Non-compliance with consumer protection regulations including UDAAP

The [Skadden August 2024 analysis](https://www.skadden.com/insights/publications/2024/08/us-banking-agencies-ramp-up-scrutiny) and [Sidley Austin analysis](https://www.sidley.com/en/insights/newsupdates/2024/08/us-banking-agencies-enhance-focus-on-bank-fintech-arrangements) both noted that the agencies signaled continued enhanced scrutiny, particularly for novel products and consumer protection contexts.

## Impact Assessment [MEDIUM confidence]

### Affected Entities

**Sponsor banks** face the greatest immediate compliance burden. Any FDIC-insured bank that partners with fintech companies to deliver deposit accounts, payments products, or lending services must review its third-party risk management program against the May 2024 Community Bank Guide and the July 2024 joint statement. Smaller community banks that entered BaaS arrangements to generate fee income without adequate risk infrastructure are at heightened enforcement risk.

**Fintech companies** are affected indirectly but substantially. Sponsor banks facing heightened regulatory pressure are re-evaluating their fintech client portfolios. Fintechs with weak compliance infrastructure, insufficient legal counsel, or unclear beneficial ownership structures are finding it difficult to maintain or establish sponsor bank relationships. The collapse of multiple BaaS partnerships in 2024 reduced the supply of willing sponsor banks, increasing the cost and difficulty of securing banking access.

**Multi-tier BaaS structures** involving middleware providers are under particular scrutiny. Following Synapse, regulators have signaled that the bank — not the middleware layer — bears ultimate responsibility for customer fund recordkeeping and reconciliation. Middleware-reliant structures that cannot demonstrate independent bank-level reconciliation capability face examination findings.

**Georgia-headquartered entities**: Georgia Banking Company's Payments and Technology Banking division directly serves the large Georgia payments ecosystem. Payments companies, money transmitters, and fintechs operating through Georgia-chartered banks or seeking Georgia money transmitter licenses face both federal third-party risk expectations (as applied through their sponsor bank relationships) and Georgia DBF regulatory requirements.

### Compliance Requirements

For sponsor banks, the 2024 guidance cycle establishes the following practical compliance expectations:

1. **Independent recordkeeping**: Banks must be able to independently verify end-user fund positions in FBO accounts without relying solely on fintech or middleware ledger data.
2. **BSA/AML ownership**: Banks cannot delegate CIP and CDD to fintech partners as a substitution for bank-level controls; oversight of the fintech's performance of delegated functions must be demonstrable.
3. **Contractual protections**: Agreements with fintech partners must include audit rights, data access rights, performance standards, and workable termination/transition provisions.
4. **Ongoing monitoring programs**: Examiners expect documented, executed monitoring — not just policies — covering financial condition of the fintech, operational incidents, compliance metrics, and consumer complaint trends.
5. **Contingency planning**: Banks must demonstrate they can protect customers if a fintech partner fails, including account transfer or direct servicing capabilities.

### Enforcement Outlook

The regulatory posture established in 2024 is expected to persist. The [Corporate Compliance Insights analysis](https://www.corporatecomplianceinsights.com/will-bank-fintech-partnerships-face-regulatory-scrutiny-2025/) notes that bank-fintech partnerships will face continued scrutiny in 2025 and beyond, with regulators applying the enhanced expectations articulated in 2024. Banks that cannot demonstrate risk management maturity commensurate with their fintech partnership activities should expect examination findings and potential formal enforcement.

## Action Items

- Sponsor banks should conduct a gap assessment of their third-party risk management programs against the [May 2024 Community Bank Guide](https://www.occ.gov/news-issuances/news-releases/2024/nr-ia-2024-46.html) and the five-stage lifecycle framework from the [June 2023 interagency guidance](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2023/06/09/2023-12340/interagency-guidance-on-third-party-relationships-risk-management).
- Banks with FBO deposit structures should immediately assess whether they can independently reconcile end-user fund positions without relying on middleware ledger data; if not, a remediation roadmap is needed.
- Fintech companies seeking sponsor bank relationships should audit their own compliance infrastructure — BSA/AML program, legal counsel quality, beneficial ownership transparency — before approaching potential bank partners.
- Banks should review all fintech partner contracts against the contract-provision expectations in the 2024 Community Bank Guide, particularly audit rights and termination/transition provisions.
- Monitor the Federal Register for any final rule emerging from the July 2024 RFI and the FDIC's October 2024 proposed recordkeeping rule, both of which may impose new binding requirements on deposit-taking BaaS arrangements.
- Georgia-domiciled payments companies and money transmitters should ensure compliance with Georgia DBF licensing requirements under O.C.G.A. §§ 7-1-680 through 7-1-698 as part of their overall regulatory posture before seeking or maintaining sponsor bank relationships.
