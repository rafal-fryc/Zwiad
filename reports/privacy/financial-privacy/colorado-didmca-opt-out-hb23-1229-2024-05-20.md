# Colorado's DIDMCA Opt-Out: Federal Preemption Challenge, Preliminary Injunction, and Implications for Fintech Lending

**Jurisdiction:** Colorado / Federal | **Category:** Privacy / Financial Regulation | **Date:** 2024-05-20

## Executive Summary [HIGH confidence]

Colorado enacted [House Bill 23-1229](https://leg.colorado.gov/bills/hb23-1229) on June 5, 2023, becoming the first U.S. state in over four decades to exercise its right under Section 525 of the [Depository Institutions Deregulation and Monetary Control Act of 1980 (DIDMCA)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/12/1831d) to opt out of federal interest rate preemption for state-chartered banks. Codified at [Colo. Rev. Stat. § 5-13-106](https://leg.colorado.gov/bills/hb23-1229), the law was scheduled to take effect on July 1, 2024, and would have required all state-chartered, FDIC-insured banks making consumer loans to Colorado residents to comply with Colorado's interest rate caps (currently 21% for most consumer loans) regardless of where the bank is located. On May 16, 2024 — the date of the original finding — a federal district court held a preliminary injunction hearing in [National Association of Industrial Bankers v. Weiser, No. 1:24-cv-00812-DDD-KAS](https://dockets.justia.com/docket/colorado/codce/1:2024cv00812/232596) (D. Colo.), with three industry trade associations arguing that Colorado's interpretation of the opt-out exceeded statutory authority. The district court subsequently granted the preliminary injunction on June 18, 2024, temporarily blocking enforcement — though that decision was later reversed by the Tenth Circuit in November 2025. This litigation sits at the crossroads of federal banking preemption, state consumer protection, and the business model of fintech-bank partnerships.

## Background [HIGH confidence]

### The Federal Interest Rate Preemption Framework

The Depository Institutions Deregulation and Monetary Control Act of 1980 was enacted by Congress to level the competitive playing field between nationally chartered banks and state-chartered banks. Prior to DIDMCA, the Supreme Court's 1978 decision in *Marquette National Bank v. First of Omaha Service Corp.* had already held that national banks — governed by the National Bank Act — could "export" the interest rate of their home state to borrowers in other states, regardless of the borrower's state's usury limits. DIDMCA extended functionally equivalent authority to FDIC-insured state-chartered banks and credit unions through [12 U.S.C. § 1831d](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/12/1831d), allowing them to charge the higher of: (a) 1 percent above the ninety-day commercial paper rate at the relevant Federal Reserve Bank, or (b) the rate permitted by the laws of the state where the bank is located.

This "interest rate exportation" right enables a state-chartered bank located in, say, Utah — which has no usury ceiling — to extend consumer loans nationwide at rates that would exceed the usury limits of the borrower's home state. The practical consequence is that a bank chartered in a permissive state can serve consumers across the country without being constrained by the varying rate caps of each state where its customers reside. The framework became the legal backbone of marketplace and fintech lending, as online lenders partnered with state-chartered banks to originate loans under the banks' preemption rights, then purchased those loans.

### The Opt-Out Mechanism

Section 525 of DIDMCA, codified at [12 U.S.C. § 1831d(b)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/12/1831d), contains an important carve-out. States retain the right to withdraw from federal preemption by enacting a law (or certifying a voter referendum) that "explicitly and by its terms" states that the state does not want Section 521 to apply "with respect to loans made in such State." The critical phrase — "loans made in such State" — is the core legal dispute in the Colorado litigation.

When DIDMCA was enacted in 1980, a number of states initially exercised this opt-out right: Colorado, Iowa, Maine, Massachusetts, Nebraska, North Carolina, Wisconsin, and Puerto Rico all opted out. However, as federal interest rates declined in the early 1980s and the economic pressure that originally motivated opt-outs faded, most states repealed their opt-out legislation. By the time Colorado re-enacted its opt-out in 2023, only [Iowa and Puerto Rico](https://afsaonline.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/09/DIDMCA-Legislative-History-September-2024.pdf) had never rescinded theirs. Iowa's opt-out had historically not been aggressively enforced but more recently became the subject of state regulatory action.

### Colorado's 2023 Re-Enactment

Colorado's consumer protection policymakers had grown increasingly concerned about high-rate consumer loans extended to Colorado residents by out-of-state state-chartered banks. The state had previously used "true lender" theories to challenge high-rate bank partnership programs, resulting in an Assurance of Discontinuance that imposed conduct standards on certain fintech-bank partnerships operating in Colorado.

In April 2023, Colorado introduced [HB 23-1229](https://leg.colorado.gov/bills/hb23-1229) to amend the state's Uniform Consumer Credit Code (UCCC). The bill was amended in late April to carve out "general purpose credit cards" from its scope, before passing the legislature and being signed by Governor Jared Polis on June 5, 2023. As codified at Colo. Rev. Stat. § 5-13-106, the law provides that the federal interest rate preemption of DIDMCA does not apply to consumer credit transactions made in Colorado, effective July 1, 2024. Colorado thus became the first modern state to re-enact a DIDMCA opt-out after having previously repealed one.

The Colorado action spurred legislative interest in other states. By early 2024, [Minnesota, Rhode Island, Nevada, and the District of Columbia](https://www.consumerfinancialserviceslawmonitor.com/2024/02/rhode-island-and-minnesota-latest-states-with-bills-opting-out-of-federal-banking-law-allowing-interest-rate-exportation/) had introduced or were pursuing analogous opt-out measures, signaling a broader national trend.

## Detailed Analysis [HIGH confidence]

### The Trade Association Lawsuit

On March 25, 2024, three trade associations — the [National Association of Industrial Bankers (NAIB)](https://www.aba.com/advocacy/policy-analysis/district-of-colorado-amicus-brief), the [American Financial Services Association (AFSA)](https://www.fintechcouncil.org/press-releases/american-fintech-council-afc-rejects-colorados-attempt-to-undermine-federal-interstate-banking-law), and the [American Fintech Council (AFC)](https://www.fintechcouncil.org/press-releases/american-fintech-council-afc-rejects-colorados-attempt-to-undermine-federal-interstate-banking-law) — filed suit in the U.S. District Court for the District of Colorado, as [Case No. 1:24-cv-00812-DDD-KAS](https://dockets.justia.com/docket/colorado/codce/1:2024cv00812/232596), naming Colorado Attorney General Philip J. Weiser and Uniform Consumer Credit Code Administrator Martha Fulford as defendants. The plaintiffs sought a declaratory judgment that the Colorado opt-out law is void or inapplicable to their members' loans, and a preliminary injunction blocking its enforcement before the July 1, 2024 effective date.

The plaintiffs' central legal argument was that Colorado's opt-out could only apply to "loans made in" Colorado — and that, per the plain statutory text and the history of DIDMCA's enactment, a loan is "made" at the lender's location, not the borrower's location. Under this reading, loans originated and administered by out-of-state banks from locations outside Colorado would not be "made in" Colorado even if the borrower resides in Colorado, meaning the federal preemption would continue to apply.

### The May 16, 2024 Preliminary Injunction Hearing

On May 16, 2024, the district court held the preliminary injunction hearing that was the subject of the original finding. The hearing occurred against the backdrop of competing amicus filings. The [FDIC filed an amicus brief](https://bankingjournal.aba.com/2024/06/aba-files-coalition-amicus-brief-urging-colorado-district-court-to-grant-preliminary-injunction-in-rate-opt-out-lawsuit/) in April 2024 in support of Colorado's position — a significant intervention given the FDIC's role as the primary federal regulator of state-chartered banks. In response, the [American Bankers Association (ABA) and Consumer Bankers Association (CBA)](https://bankingjournal.aba.com/2024/06/aba-files-coalition-amicus-brief-urging-colorado-district-court-to-grant-preliminary-injunction-in-rate-opt-out-lawsuit/) filed their own coalition amicus brief opposing the FDIC's position and urging the court to grant the preliminary injunction.

### The June 18, 2024 Preliminary Injunction Order

On June 18, 2024, the [district court granted the preliminary injunction](https://bankingjournal.aba.com/2024/07/colorado-district-court-grants-preliminary-injunction-in-rate-opt-out-lawsuit/), blocking Colorado from enforcing its rate and fee limitations on loans not "made in" Colorado. The court applied a textual and structural analysis of DIDMCA, concluding that "the plain and ordinary answer to the question of who 'makes' a loan is the bank, not the borrower," and that where a loan is made depends on the lender's location and where the lender performs loan-making functions — not on the borrower's physical location in Colorado.

The order preliminarily enjoined Colorado from enforcing HB 23-1229's rate caps on any loan made by members of the three trade associations to the extent that: (1) the loan is not "made in" Colorado as determined by lender location and function; and (2) the applicable interest rate exceeds what would otherwise be permitted under Colorado law. The injunction took effect on July 1, 2024, the date the opt-out law would have otherwise become operative.

### The Tenth Circuit's November 2025 Reversal

The State of Colorado appealed, and the case reached the [Tenth Circuit Court of Appeals](https://www.consumerfinancialserviceslawmonitor.com/2025/11/tenth-circuit-narrows-didmca-opt%E2%80%91out-preemption-colorado-may-apply-its-rate-caps-to-loans-from-state-chartered-banks-to-borrowers-located-in-colorado/). In a [2-1 decision issued November 10, 2025](https://www.mayerbrown.com/en/insights/publications/2025/11/didmca-opt-out-update-tenth-circuit-reverses-colorado-preliminary-injunction), the Tenth Circuit reversed the district court's preliminary injunction, holding that a loan is "made in" a state when either the lender or the borrower is located there. Under this interpretation, Colorado's opt-out validly extends to all loans made to Colorado-located borrowers, regardless of where the bank is physically located. A dissent argued that the majority's borrower-location rule was inconsistent with DIDMCA's text and purpose. The Tenth Circuit subsequently granted en banc rehearing, vacating the panel decision — meaning the case remained in flux as of early 2026.

### Federal Preemption Theory: Why the Statutory Phrase "Loans Made In" Matters

The entire litigation turns on what Congress meant by "loans made in such State" when it drafted the opt-out provision in 1980. Two competing interpretive frameworks emerged:

**Lender-location rule (plaintiffs' view, adopted by district court):** The loan is "made" where the lender is located and performs the acts of extending credit — reviewing the application, approving the loan, disbursing funds. Because Section 521 preempts state usury laws to protect interstate lending, it would be anomalous to allow a state to reassert usury control over a bank operating outside its borders based solely on the borrower's residence. This view aligns with the National Bank Act's "located in" framework from *Marquette*.

**Borrower-location rule (Colorado's view, adopted by Tenth Circuit panel):** The loan is "made" where it is delivered to the borrower — i.e., where the borrower receives the proceeds. Colorado argued Congress intended states to protect their own residents from high-rate lending, and a purely lender-centric rule would gut the opt-out provision entirely: any bank could evade a state's opt-out simply by being chartered elsewhere.

The [ABA's amicus brief](https://www.aba.com/advocacy/policy-analysis/national-association-of-industrial-v-weiser) to the Tenth Circuit argued the district court's ruling should be affirmed as necessary to preserve uniform interstate credit markets. The Cadwalader Wickersham & Taft alert ([the original source of this finding](https://www.cadwalader.com/fin-news/index.php?eid=787&nid=102)) observed that the hearing raised fundamental questions about state sovereignty over consumer lending and whether Colorado's approach, if sustained, would effectively subject out-of-state banks to a patchwork of conflicting state rate caps.

### Scope and Carve-Outs

HB 23-1229, as amended, contains several notable scope limitations:

1. **Consumer credit only:** The opt-out applies to consumer credit transactions — commercial or business loans are not affected.
2. **State-chartered banks only:** DIDMCA Sections 521-523 do not apply to national banks, which derive their interest rate exportation authority directly from the National Bank Act (12 U.S.C. § 85). Colorado's opt-out therefore cannot affect national bank lending. This creates a competitive asymmetry: a national bank can charge Colorado borrowers rates above 21%, but a state-chartered bank (if the opt-out is enforced) cannot.
3. **General purpose credit card exclusion:** An amendment added in April 2023 excluded "general purpose credit cards" from the UCCC's rate limits, preserving the ability of card issuers (largely relying on preemption from Delaware and South Dakota) to offer revolving credit products in Colorado without rate restriction.
4. **Post-July 1, 2024 transactions only:** The law applies to consumer credit transactions made after the effective date.

## Impact Assessment [HIGH confidence]

### State-Chartered Banks and Credit Unions

The most directly affected entities are FDIC-insured, state-chartered banks and NCUA-insured credit unions that rely on interest rate exportation to offer consumer loans nationwide at rates exceeding Colorado's 21% ceiling. These include installment lenders, auto finance companies, and marketplace lenders chartered in permissive states (Utah, Delaware, Nevada, South Dakota). If the opt-out is ultimately enforced — which the Tenth Circuit's (now-vacated) panel decision suggested is legally permissible — these institutions face a binary choice: reduce rates on Colorado loans to comply with state caps, or exit the Colorado consumer lending market.

### Fintech-Bank Partnership Programs

The Colorado action is particularly consequential for fintech companies that partner with state-chartered banks under "bank as originator" models. In these arrangements, the bank originates the loan, with the fintech typically purchasing or servicing it shortly afterward. The fintech's business model depends on the bank's ability to export home-state rates. If Colorado can subject bank-originated loans to its rate caps based solely on borrower location, the entire partnership model for Colorado consumers becomes economically unviable for high-rate products. The [American Fintech Council](https://www.fintechcouncil.org/press-releases/american-fintech-council-afc-rejects-colorados-attempt-to-undermine-federal-interstate-banking-law) characterized Colorado's position as an attempt to "undermine federal interstate banking law" and a threat to consumer access to credit.

### National Banks and the Asymmetry Problem

Because national banks are not subject to DIDMCA opt-outs (their preemption derives from the National Bank Act), a successful Colorado opt-out would create a regulatory asymmetry in which national banks and their fintech partners could continue offering high-rate products in Colorado while state-chartered bank partners could not. Critics of the opt-out argued this asymmetry would not protect consumers — it would simply push lending activity toward national bank partners or toward unregulated non-bank lenders not subject to any federal interest rate authority.

### Rate Cap Practical Effects

Colorado's current interest rate ceiling for most consumer loans under its UCCC is 21%. This ceiling would be enforced against covered out-of-state state-chartered bank loans if the opt-out survives legal challenge. Lenders offering personal installment loans, auto loans, and similar products at rates between 21% and 36% (or higher) would face the most immediate business impact. Products priced above 36% APR — common in small-dollar and "near-prime" lending — would be entirely foreclosed for state-chartered bank originations to Colorado consumers.

### Multi-State Contagion Risk

Perhaps the most significant systemic concern is contagion to other states. [Minnesota, Rhode Island, Nevada, and the District of Columbia](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/business_law/resources/business-law-today/2024-february/didmca-opt-out-true-lender-legislative-proposals-watch/) introduced analogous legislation in early 2024, and other states in the progressive policy coalition were monitoring the Colorado litigation closely. A judicial ruling sustaining Colorado's opt-out would create a strong legal roadmap for other states and potentially fragment the national consumer credit market into a patchwork of state-specific rate regimes — at least for state-chartered bank originations.

### Iowa Precedent

Iowa's long-standing opt-out, which it has begun to enforce in recent years through actions against installment lenders, provided a partial preview of these dynamics. The [AFSA Issue Brief on DIDMCA](https://afsaonline.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/DIDMCA-Issue-Brief.pdf) noted that Iowa's renewed enforcement demonstrated that dormant opt-outs could be weaponized, and that Colorado's more aggressive and explicit opt-out law represented a significant escalation.

## Action Items

- **Monitor the en banc Tenth Circuit proceedings:** The full Tenth Circuit granted en banc rehearing after the November 2025 panel reversal, vacating that decision. Track the en banc briefs and argument schedule — the outcome will set binding precedent for all states in the Tenth Circuit (Colorado, Utah, Wyoming, Kansas, Oklahoma, New Mexico).
- **Assess portfolio exposure now:** State-chartered banks and their fintech partners should identify the volume of consumer loans originated to Colorado-resident borrowers and model the economic impact of a 21% rate cap at scenarios with and without borrower-location-based enforcement.
- **Evaluate charter structure:** Entities relying on state bank partnerships for rate exportation should evaluate whether restructuring through a national bank charter or a national bank partnership (not subject to DIDMCA opt-outs) would reduce regulatory risk.
- **Track sister-state legislation:** Closely monitor opt-out bills in Minnesota, Rhode Island, Nevada, and DC. If any of those laws are enacted and not preliminarily enjoined, the multi-state compliance burden will increase substantially.
- **Review true lender posture in Colorado:** Colorado's prior Assurance of Discontinuance on true lender practices creates a layered compliance obligation independent of the DIDMCA opt-out. Fintech-bank partnerships operating in Colorado should ensure their structure satisfies both the true lender standard and any rate cap that may ultimately be enforceable.
- **Watch for FDIC rulemaking:** The FDIC's pro-Colorado amicus filing signals that the current agency posture may not strongly defend interest rate exportation rights. If FDIC regulations are revised to support borrower-location rules, this could have implications beyond the Colorado litigation.
