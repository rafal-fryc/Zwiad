# House Discussion Draft Would Modernize GLBA Title V and Broadly Preempt State Financial Privacy Laws

## Executive Summary [HIGH confidence]

A [discussion draft](https://docs.house.gov/meetings/BA/BA00/20260317/119049/BILLS-119pih-makesimprovementstoTitleVoftheGLBAt.pdf) released by Rep. Bill Huizenga (R-MI), chair of the House Financial Services Subcommittee on Oversight and Investigations, would rewrite Title V of the [Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act (GLBA)](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/privacy-security/gramm-leach-bliley-act) to impose modern data-minimization obligations, create new consumer access and deletion rights, expand the definition of nonpublic personal information (NPI) to cover biometric, geolocation, and access-credential data, strengthen opt-out rights, and — most consequentially — [expressly preempt state privacy and security laws](https://www.consumerfinancialserviceslawmonitor.com/2026/03/house-financial-services-committee-discussion-draft-to-amend-glba-and-preempt-state-regimes/) that impose requirements on NPI handled by financial institutions. The draft was the focal point of a March 17, 2026 full committee hearing titled ["Updating America's Financial Privacy Framework for the 21st Century"](https://iapp.org/news/a/a-view-from-dc-the-federal-plan-to-modernize-and-preempt-financial-privacy-rules), and stakeholder comments are now being submitted on the [committee's formal Request for Information](https://financialservices.house.gov/news/documentsingle.aspx?DocumentID=410833).

## Background [HIGH confidence]

Title V of GLBA, enacted in 1999, is the principal federal privacy statute for banks, credit unions, insurers, securities firms, and other "financial institutions" as defined by the statute. Its core obligations — annual privacy notices, limited opt-outs for sharing with nonaffiliated third parties, and the Safeguards Rule — have remained largely static for a quarter century, even as the financial data ecosystem has expanded to include data aggregators, open banking APIs, and machine-learning-based analytics.

In the absence of federal updates, states have layered additional obligations on top of GLBA. Most of the 21 state comprehensive privacy statutes enacted since 2018 contain entity-level GLBA exemptions, but several — notably California (CCPA/CPRA), Vermont's insurance data security law, and New York's DFS Part 500 cybersecurity regulation — impose material obligations on financial institutions either data-wide or sector-specifically. IAPP's April 10, 2026 analysis describes the current landscape as a "fragmented market" that the draft seeks to unify ([IAPP, "A view from DC"](https://iapp.org/news/a/a-view-from-dc-the-federal-plan-to-modernize-and-preempt-financial-privacy-rules)).

## Key Provisions of the Discussion Draft [HIGH confidence]

Drawing on the [discussion draft text published by the House Financial Services Committee](https://docs.house.gov/meetings/BA/BA00/20260317/119049/BILLS-119pih-makesimprovementstoTitleVoftheGLBAt.pdf), the [Consumer Financial Services Law Monitor analysis by Troutman Pepper Locke](https://www.consumerfinancialserviceslawmonitor.com/2026/03/house-financial-services-committee-discussion-draft-to-amend-glba-and-preempt-state-regimes/) (republished on [JD Supra](https://www.jdsupra.com/legalnews/house-financial-services-committee-3432533/)), and the [ABA Banking Journal](https://bankingjournal.aba.com/2026/03/lawmakers-consider-potential-changes-to-data-privacy-law/), the draft contains the following core elements:

- **Shift from disclosure to treatment.** The draft reframes Title V from a notice-and-opt-out regime to a substantive regulation of the collection, use, retention, and disclosure of nonpublic personal information (NPI).
- **Statutory data minimization.** Financial institutions would be required to limit processing of NPI to what is necessary for legitimate business, legal, or regulatory purposes — a first-of-its-kind federal data-minimization mandate in the financial sector.
- **Expanded definition of NPI.** The draft broadens GLBA's definition of "nonpublic personal information" to expressly include **biometric data, geolocation data, and access credentials**, capturing categories of financial-adjacent data (login tokens, device-location signals, fingerprint/face templates used in mobile banking) that have historically sat at the margins of Title V coverage ([Troutman Pepper Locke analysis](https://www.consumerfinancialserviceslawmonitor.com/2026/03/house-financial-services-committee-discussion-draft-to-amend-glba-and-preempt-state-regimes/)).
- **New §503A consumer access and deletion rights.** A new Section 503A would codify individual rights — for the first time under GLBA — to (i) obtain their NPI held by a financial institution, (ii) receive a list of categories of recipients with whom that NPI has been shared, and (iii) request deletion of NPI after the customer relationship ends, subject to exceptions for legal, regulatory, and Fair Credit Reporting Act (FCRA) obligations ([Troutman Pepper Locke](https://www.consumerfinancialserviceslawmonitor.com/2026/03/house-financial-services-committee-discussion-draft-to-amend-glba-and-preempt-state-regimes/); [discussion draft](https://docs.house.gov/meetings/BA/BA00/20260317/119049/BILLS-119pih-makesimprovementstoTitleVoftheGLBAt.pdf)).
- **Enhanced opt-out rights.** Consumers would be able to direct a financial institution not to share NPI with nonaffiliated third parties both at initial disclosure and at any time thereafter, eliminating the existing "initial notice" trigger structure.
- **Coverage of data aggregators.** The draft formally defines "financial data aggregator" as a covered entity, extending Title V obligations to aggregators and downstream recipients that today sit outside GLBA's core scope, while carving out service providers, FCRA-regulated consumer reporting agencies, and certain professional advisors.
- **Broad express preemption.** The draft rewrites GLBA's preemption clause to expressly supersede state statutes and regulations that impose privacy or security requirements on NPI subject to Title V. State insurance regulators would retain authority to enforce GLBA duties through "consistent, comparable" regulation, but other state consumer data privacy and security laws would not apply to financial institutions with respect to NPI.

## Stakeholder Positions [HIGH confidence]

**Industry — generally supportive of preemption.** The American Bankers Association told the committee that "it is critical that any new federal privacy law preempt existing state laws to avoid inconsistent and duplicative requirements" ([ABA Banking Journal](https://bankingjournal.aba.com/2026/03/lawmakers-consider-potential-changes-to-data-privacy-law/)). America's Credit Unions publicly [endorsed the draft's preemption relief while flagging implementation concerns](https://www.americascreditunions.org/news-media/news/draft-data-privacy-bill-includes-important-relief-needs-additional-updates), and [PYMNTS reports credit-union trade groups are actively lobbying in favor](https://www.pymnts.com/credit-unions/2026/credit-unions-back-house-financial-privacy-bill/).

**Consumer advocates — opposed to preemption.** A [coalition letter organized by the National Consumer Law Center](https://www.nclc.org/resources/coalition-letter-to-house-financial-services-modernize-gramm-leach-bliley-but-do-not-nullify-state-consumer-protections/) and a [separate EPIC letter with multiple sign-ons](https://epic.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/Letter-to-House-FSC-Modernize-Financial-Data-Privacy-without-Nullifying-State-Protections-with-sign-ons.pdf) urge Congress to modernize GLBA without nullifying state protections. EPIC specifically warned that making GLBA a preemptive standard without strengthening its substantive protections would contract, rather than expand, the overall scope of US financial privacy law ([EPIC statement](https://epic.org/epic-urges-house-financial-services-committee-to-protect-financial-privacy/)).

## Procedural Status [HIGH confidence]

This remains a **discussion draft**, not an introduced bill — the [draft PDF posted on docs.house.gov](https://docs.house.gov/meetings/BA/BA00/20260317/119049/BILLS-119pih-makesimprovementstoTitleVoftheGLBAt.pdf) is headed "H. R. ll" with a placeholder bill number, confirming that it has not been formally introduced. The [House Financial Services Committee's RFI](https://financialservices.house.gov/news/documentsingle.aspx?DocumentID=410833) solicited written feedback on the draft and on broader questions about federal consumer financial privacy reform. The [March 17, 2026 full committee hearing](https://iapp.org/news/a/a-view-from-dc-the-federal-plan-to-modernize-and-preempt-financial-privacy-rules) aired the proposal publicly for the first time. No markup has been scheduled as of the date of this report, and no Congress.gov entry will exist until the draft is formally introduced with a bill number.

## Impact Analysis [MEDIUM confidence]

- **Financial institutions** would gain a single federal compliance standard in place of overlapping state regimes, reducing multi-state compliance cost but requiring substantial operational changes to meet new data-minimization, expanded-NPI, and opt-out-at-any-time obligations, plus new access/deletion request intake and fulfillment workflows under §503A.
- **State attorneys general and insurance regulators** would lose the ability to apply general-purpose consumer privacy statutes to GLBA data; enforcement would re-concentrate at the federal banking agencies, the FTC, and the CFPB.
- **Data aggregators and open-banking intermediaries** — many of which currently argue they fall outside GLBA — would be pulled into the regulatory perimeter as expressly defined "financial data aggregators."
- **State comprehensive privacy laws** would see their financial-data carve-outs effectively widened: provisions that currently apply to GLBA-covered entities data-wide (e.g., California's limited application to employees and B2B contacts) would be displaced as to NPI — including newly in-scope biometric, geolocation, and credential data.
- **Cross-over with pending federal AI preemption efforts.** The draft's preemption architecture parallels the approach President Trump took in his December 2025 executive order on national AI policy, suggesting a coordinated federal strategy to centralize data and AI governance.

## Action Items for Compliance Teams

- Map current state-law financial-privacy obligations that would be displaced (CCPA/CPRA as applied to GLBA-covered entities, NYDFS Part 500, Vermont insurance data security, state data-broker laws reaching financial affiliates, and state biometric privacy laws such as Illinois BIPA as applied to financial-sector biometric processing).
- Inventory NPI data flows to nonaffiliated third parties and assess readiness for a "revocable at any time" opt-out architecture.
- Identify biometric, geolocation, and access-credential data stores that would be newly in scope under the expanded NPI definition and evaluate retention, vendor, and safeguards posture accordingly.
- Build or extend DSAR-style workflows to support §503A access, categories-of-recipients disclosure, and post-relationship deletion requests, with documented exceptions for FCRA and other legal-hold scenarios.
- Evaluate vendor contracts with data aggregators in light of likely direct GLBA coverage.
- Submit comments on the House Financial Services RFI if the organization has not already done so; the committee remains in active listening mode.
- Monitor for formal bill introduction and any parallel Senate Banking Committee activity.
