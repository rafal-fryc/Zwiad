# Maine's Comprehensive Privacy Bill (LD 1822) Fails on Final House Vote

## Executive Summary [HIGH confidence]

The Maine House of Representatives on April 9, 2026, voted for the second time in a week against concurring with the Senate's version of [Legislative Document 1822](https://legislature.maine.gov/legis/bills/display_ps.asp?LD=1822&snum=132), the Maine Online Data Privacy Act, effectively ending the bill's consideration before the 132nd Legislature adjourns on April 15. The bill had been defeated 80-68 in the House a week earlier — triggered in part by five Democratic legislators reversing their positions on a procedural concurrence vote — then revived 18-14 by the Senate, and sent back to the House where it failed again amid a final lobbying push by Maine business groups. This is the second consecutive legislative session in which a comprehensive privacy proposal has died in Maine. Sponsor Rep. Amy Kuhn (D-Falmouth) had modeled the bill on Maryland's 2024 Online Data Privacy Act, one of the strictest state privacy laws in the country.

## Background [MEDIUM confidence]

The state privacy law landscape has expanded rapidly in recent years. As of early 2026, [20 states have comprehensive privacy laws in effect](https://www.multistate.us/insider/2026/2/4/all-of-the-comprehensive-privacy-laws-that-take-effect-in-2026), with Indiana, Kentucky, and Rhode Island joining the ranks on January 1, 2026. Maine's LD 1822, titled "An Act to Enact the Maine Online Data Privacy Act," had been considered one of the strongest privacy bills in the country. Notably, the bill would have granted consumers a right no other state provides: the right to know the actual names of every third party to which a controller sells their personal data ([Verrill](https://www.verrill-law.com/news/maines-house-of-representatives-adopts-comprehensive-privacy-act/)). The bill [previously passed the House in amended form](https://www.maine.gov/housedems/news/house-passes-amended-version-kuhn-data-privacy-bill) and then passed the Maine Senate, but because the Senate adopted changes, it required House concurrence on the amended version.

## What Happened [HIGH confidence]

LD 1822 had an unusually turbulent path. Both chambers initially approved versions of the bill in March 2026, but the House and Senate could not agree on an exemption for political organizations — a late-added carve-out that the [Maine Morning Star reported](https://mainemorningstar.com/2026/03/05/new-political-group-exemption-in-data-privacy-proposal-narrows-support/) had narrowed support among privacy advocates and some Democrats. The bill [bounced between chambers](https://mainemorningstar.com/2026/03/26/data-privacy-deja-vu-as-bill-bounces-between-chambers-over-exemption-confusion/) over exemption language throughout late March.

In the first week of April, five key Democrats reversed their positions on the concurrence vote, and the House defeated LD 1822 by a vote of 80-68. The [Maine Senate voted 18-14 on April 6](https://www.centralmaine.com/2026/04/06/maine-senate-votes-to-keep-controversial-data-privacy-bill-alive/) to keep the bill alive and send it back to the House for reconsideration. The House, however, [rejected the Senate version again](https://mainemorningstar.com/2026/04/09/maine-rejects-comprehensive-data-privacy-law-again/) on April 9, 2026. With the 132nd Legislature scheduled to adjourn April 15 and no procedural vehicle remaining to revive the text, the bill is dead for the session.

Business groups — concerned primarily about restrictions on targeted advertising and compliance costs — mounted a final lobbying push in the days before the House vote, as chronicled by the [Maine Morning Star](https://mainemorningstar.com/2026/04/03/fear-of-the-unknown-toll-on-businesses-again-likely-to-sink-data-privacy-bill/). Several House Democrats joined Republicans in opposition.

## Bill Substance [HIGH confidence]

As reflected in the [official Maine Legislature text and status page](https://legislature.maine.gov/legis/bills/display_ps.asp?LD=1822&snum=132) and described in the sponsor's [House Democrats announcement](https://www.maine.gov/housedems/news/house-approves-kuhn-bill-strengthen-maines-data-privacy-laws), LD 1822 would have:

- Applied to businesses that collect or process personal data of Maine residents (with specific applicability thresholds set below those in most other state laws).
- Required **data minimization** — collection and retention limited to what is reasonably necessary to provide a requested product or service.
- Granted consumers the standard suite of rights: confirmation, access, correction, deletion, portability, and opt-out of targeted advertising, sale, and profiling.
- Provided the right to know the **actual names of every third party** to which a controller sells personal data — a right no other state law provides.
- **Banned targeted advertising to minors** and prohibited the sale of children's data.
- Restricted the collection of **biometric information** (e.g., fingerprints) unless strictly necessary.
- Established strong civil rights protections against data-driven discrimination.
- Set an effective date of September 1, 2027 (delayed from an earlier July 2026 date via House amendment).

The bill closely tracked Maryland's Online Data Privacy Act, which is widely viewed by industry as the most restrictive state consumer privacy law on the books.

## Political-Organization Exemption Controversy [MEDIUM confidence]

A central point of contention was a proposed exemption for political organizations. The [Maine Morning Star reported in March](https://mainemorningstar.com/2026/03/05/new-political-group-exemption-in-data-privacy-proposal-narrows-support/) that the exemption was added during negotiation and drew opposition from consumer advocates who viewed it as an inappropriate carve-out. The House and Senate repeatedly disagreed over the scope of that language, contributing to the ping-pong between chambers and ultimately to Democratic defections on the final concurrence vote.

## Multi-State Context [MEDIUM confidence]

Maine's defeat occurred against a backdrop of several other states facing April legislative session deadlines:

- **Alabama** (session closes April 16): A consumer data privacy bill passed the House unanimously (104-0) on February 25, 2026, and was voted out of a Senate committee.
- **Kansas** (session closes April 10): Active privacy legislation faced an imminent deadline.
- **Kentucky** (session closes April 15): Privacy bills under consideration (Kentucky already has a comprehensive privacy law effective January 1, 2026).
- **Nebraska** (session closes April 17): Privacy legislation pending (Nebraska also already has a comprehensive privacy law in effect).
- **Tennessee** (session closes April 24): Privacy legislation pending before session close (Tennessee's comprehensive privacy law is already in effect).

Ohio also introduced [HB 807](https://www.legislature.ohio.gov/legislation/136/hb807) on March 31, 2026, a Democrat-sponsored bill that would prohibit government and private entities from selling sensitive personal data to data brokers for profit. As a Democrat-sponsored bill in a Republican-controlled legislature, its near-term prospects remain uncertain.

## Significance and Outlook [HIGH confidence]

- **Maine remains without a comprehensive privacy law** as the U.S. state tally reaches 21 (Alabama enacted its Personal Data Protection Act the same week).
- **Second failure in two sessions**: a prior comprehensive privacy proposal died in the 131st Legislature, underscoring persistent business-coalition resistance in a state often viewed as privacy-friendly (Maine has a sector-specific [ISP privacy law](https://www.mainelegislature.org/legis/statutes/35-A/title35-Asec9301.html) enacted in 2019).
- **Signaling effect on the 'Maryland model'**: LD 1822's defeat, combined with slower uptake of Maryland-style data minimization requirements in other states, suggests the dominant state template remains the Virginia/Connecticut model rather than the stricter Maryland framework.
- **Next session**: Rep. Kuhn or her successor will likely reintroduce a version in the 133rd Legislature convening in December 2026; the political-exemption dispute and the targeted-advertising objections will need to be resolved before a bill can survive floor votes in both chambers.

## Action Items for Clients

- **Businesses with Maine customer data**: No immediate compliance obligations arise; continue to track Maine ISP privacy requirements (35-A M.R.S. § 9301) and applicable sector rules.
- **Privacy program owners**: Monitor the 133rd Legislature's opening filings (December 2026) for a reintroduced Online Data Privacy Act; the Maryland-based template is the likely baseline.
- **Multi-state compliance teams**: Continue to prioritize Maryland, California, and the five 2026 enactments (including Alabama) as the near-term pacing items; Maine is not on the 2026 compliance critical path.
- **Ohio tracking**: Review Ohio HB 807 text once committee hearings are scheduled to assess the scope of "permitted purposes" exceptions.
