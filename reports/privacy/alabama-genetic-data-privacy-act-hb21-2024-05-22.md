# Alabama Enacts Genetic Data Privacy Act (HB 21): Consumer Protections for DTC Genetic Testing Companies

**Jurisdiction:** Alabama | **Category:** Privacy | **Date:** May 22, 2024

## Executive Summary [HIGH confidence]

On May 16, 2024, Alabama Governor Kay Ivey signed [House Bill 21](https://alison.legislature.state.al.us/files/pdf/SearchableInstruments/2024RS/HB21-enr.pdf) into law, enacting the Alabama Genetic Data Privacy Act (AGDPA), codified at [Alabama Code Title 8, Chapter 43](https://law.justia.com/codes/alabama/title-8/chapter-43/). The law regulates consumer-facing direct-to-consumer (DTC) genetic testing companies, imposing consent, transparency, and consumer rights obligations on those companies' collection, use, and disclosure of genetic data and biological samples. Key provisions include mandatory express consent requirements, prohibitions on disclosure to insurers and employers without written consent, and consumer rights to access, delete, and revoke consent for their genetic information. The law, effective October 1, 2024, is enforced exclusively by the Alabama Attorney General's Consumer Division, with civil penalties up to $3,000 per violation and no private right of action. Alabama's enactment joins more than a dozen states now regulating DTC genetic testing, continuing a bipartisan national trend aimed at protecting one of the most sensitive categories of personal data.

## Background [HIGH confidence]

### The Growth of Consumer Genetic Testing

The direct-to-consumer genetic testing industry has grown rapidly since companies like 23andMe and AncestryDNA began offering at-home DNA kits, which typically provide consumers with ancestry information, disease risk assessments, and health trait reports. These products require consumers to submit biological samples — typically saliva — which companies analyze to generate genetic data. The sensitivity of genetic information is distinct from other personal data: it is immutable, reveals information about not only the tested individual but also their family members, and can be used for medical underwriting, employment screening, and law enforcement identification.

### Prior State Legislative Activity

Responding to concerns about DTC companies' data practices, states began regulating this sector beginning in 2018. As of 2024, Alabama became part of a group exceeding thirteen states with dedicated DTC genetic privacy statutes, joining Arizona, California, Florida, Kentucky, Maryland, Montana, Nebraska, Tennessee, Texas, Utah, Virginia, and Wyoming, according to [Covington & Burling analysis via Lexology](https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=ec7be11d-be80-45e2-8902-30ccd4b03a00). The majority of these state laws follow a common framework, though states vary on specific elements such as data localization requirements (Montana), property rights in genetic data (Texas), and law enforcement access standards (Montana requires warrants after June 1, 2025).

The [Future of Privacy Forum reported](https://fpf.org/blog/the-dna-of-genetic-privacy-legislation-montana-tennessee-texas-and-virginia-enter-2024-with-new-genetic-privacy-laws-incorporating-fpfs-best-practices/) that in 2024, Montana, Tennessee, Texas, and Virginia enacted updated genetic privacy legislation incorporating best practices for the sector. Alabama's 2024 law fits squarely within this trend.

### Alabama Legislative History

HB 21 was introduced in the 2024 Regular Session by Representative Brown, assigned to the House Judiciary Committee, and progressed through engrossed and enrolled versions before being signed into law. The bill was amended at least once during the Judiciary Committee stage, as reflected in a [February 2024 amendment filing](https://trackbill.com/s3/bills/AL/2024/HB/21/amendments/vejalll-1.pdf). The enrolled final text is available from the [Alabama Legislature's ALISON system](https://alison.legislature.state.al.us/files/pdf/SearchableInstruments/2024RS/HB21-enr.pdf) and is codified under Title 8 (Commercial Law and Consumer Protection) of the Alabama Code.

## Detailed Analysis [HIGH confidence]

### Scope and Covered Entities

The AGDPA applies to "genetic testing companies," defined as any person, other than a health care provider, that directly solicits a biological sample from a consumer for analysis in order to provide products or services to the consumer. The products covered include disclosure of genetic links to population groups based on ethnicity, geography, or anthropology, among other outputs. This definition is explicitly designed to capture the DTC genetic testing sector and excludes health care providers, which are separately regulated under HIPAA.

### Consent Requirements

The statute imposes layered consent obligations. Genetic testing companies must obtain a consumer's **express consent** for the collection, use, and disclosure of genetic data. The consent must identify specifically who may have access to the consumer's sample and data, and must obtain permission to retain the biological sample and genetic data for future testing. This initial consent framework applies at the outset of any genetic testing relationship.

Beyond initial consent, the law requires companies to obtain fresh express consent **each time** the company:

1. Transfers a biological sample or genetic data to a third party for a reason other than provision of the product or service ordered;
2. Uses the biological sample or genetic data for a purpose other than the ordered product or service; or
3. Markets to a consumer based on the consumer's genetic data.

This transactional consent requirement is notable because it prevents companies from relying on a single blanket consent to authorize ongoing secondary uses — a common industry practice criticized by privacy advocates.

### Research Transfers

The law imposes a distinct requirement for research data transfers. If a company seeks to transfer a consumer's biological sample or genetic data to a third party for **independent research**, or for research sponsored by the genetic testing company for product or service research and development, scientific publication, or promotion, it must obtain **informed consent in compliance with 45 C.F.R. part 46** — the federal Common Rule governing human subjects research, per [Covington & Burling's analysis](https://www.insideprivacy.com/health-privacy/alabama-enacts-genetic-privacy-bill/). This integration of Common Rule standards into a state commercial statute is significant, as it aligns DTC genetic research practices with the standards historically applied to academic and clinical research.

### Prohibited Disclosures

The AGDPA prohibits genetic testing companies from disclosing a consumer's genetic data to:

- Any person issuing **health, life, disability, or long-term care insurance**;
- Any **employer or prospective employer**;
- **Law enforcement**, without express written consent.

These categorical prohibitions address the most significant harm vectors for genetic data misuse: discriminatory underwriting, employment discrimination, and non-consensual law enforcement use. The prohibition on law enforcement disclosure without consent is qualified by an exemption for biological samples or genetic data lawfully obtained by law enforcement pursuant to a criminal investigation — meaning the prohibition applies to voluntary disclosures by companies, not to data already in law enforcement's possession through proper legal channels.

### Consumer Rights

The law creates a set of consumer-facing rights:

- **Transparency**: Companies must provide notice to consumers regarding their privacy practices and the collection, use, and disclosure of genetic data, including disclosures of de-identified genetic data to third parties for research.
- **Access and deletion**: Consumers may access and request deletion of their genetic data. Upon a deletion request, the company must comply as soon as reasonably possible, and in no event more than **30 days** after the request.
- **Revocation and return**: Consumers may revoke any consent previously provided under the law, including consent to store their biological sample. If a consumer revokes consent that resulted in a transfer of the biological sample or genetic data to a third party, the company must secure the **return** of the biological sample and genetic data as soon as reasonably possible, and in no event more than **60 days** after revocation.

### Exemptions

The law exempts from coverage:

1. **Academic and nonprofit research**: The collection, use, or retention of biological samples or genetic data for noncommercial purposes, including research and instruction, by a public or private institution of higher learning or any entity owned or operated by such an institution.
2. **Law enforcement**: Biological samples or genetic data lawfully obtained by law enforcement pursuant to a criminal investigation.

These exemptions are relatively narrow compared to some other state laws and do not include broad exemptions for clinical laboratory testing or pharmaceutical research, which may create compliance questions for companies at the intersection of DTC and clinical genetic services.

### Enforcement

The Consumer Division of the Alabama Office of the Attorney General has exclusive enforcement authority. The penalty structure allows civil penalties of up to **$3,000 per violation**. The law contains no private right of action, meaning individual consumers cannot sue companies directly under the statute. This enforcement model is common among state genetic privacy laws, though the relatively modest per-violation penalty cap distinguishes it from statutes in other sectors where penalty exposure can be substantially higher.

## Impact Assessment [MEDIUM confidence]

### Affected Companies

The law directly targets the DTC genetic testing industry, which includes well-known companies such as 23andMe, AncestryDNA, Invitae, Nebula Genomics, and their competitors. Companies selling genetic testing services to Alabama residents must comply regardless of where the company is incorporated or headquartered, consistent with the consumer-protection jurisdictional approach common in state privacy law.

Companies offering pharmacogenomic testing, genealogical services, or ancestry databases that solicit biological samples directly from consumers fall squarely within scope. However, health care providers and clinical laboratories operating under physician orders are excluded.

### Compliance Requirements

Companies subject to the AGDPA needed to be ready for compliance by **October 1, 2024**. Key compliance obligations include:

- Updating privacy notices to satisfy the law's transparency requirements;
- Revising consent workflows to ensure express, itemized consent is obtained at each stage of data use — including separate consent for third-party transfers, secondary uses, and marketing;
- Implementing Common Rule-compliant informed consent processes for research transfers;
- Building mechanisms for consumers to exercise access, deletion, and revocation rights, with back-end capability to retrieve or return data from third parties within 60-day windows;
- Auditing third-party data sharing arrangements with insurers and employers to eliminate non-consensual transfers;
- Ensuring that law enforcement data requests are not fulfilled voluntarily without consumer consent and that legally required disclosures are documented separately.

### Broader Regulatory Landscape

The AGDPA's enactment adds to the patchwork of state DTC genetic privacy laws that companies must navigate. As noted by [Troutman Pepper Locke](https://www.troutman.com/insights/locke-lord-quickstudy-trends-in-state-and-federal-regulation-of-consumer-genetic-testing/), the majority of these laws follow a similar framework but vary in significant respects. Companies operating nationally must map each state's specific requirements — for example, Montana's data localization prohibition on storing genetic data in countries designated as foreign adversaries, or Texas's property rights framework. Alabama does not include data localization or property rights provisions; its framework is more narrowly focused on consent, consumer rights, and prohibited disclosures.

The [2025 enactment of additional state genetic privacy laws](https://www.insideprivacy.com/health-privacy/multiple-states-enact-genetic-privacy-legislation-in-a-busy-start-to-2025/) and [continued legislative activity in 2026](https://www.insideprivacy.com/health-privacy/several-states-introduce-new-genetic-privacy-bills-in-early-2026/) confirm that this area remains active. Companies should expect continued state-level proliferation absent federal preemption.

### Enforcement Outlook

Because this law has no private right of action and a relatively modest penalty structure ($3,000 per violation), enforcement risk is concentrated in systematic or high-profile violations that attract AG attention. Alabama's AG Consumer Division has broad authority to investigate but limited resources compared to larger states. Companies should nonetheless take compliance seriously: per-violation penalties can aggregate across large consumer databases, and AG investigations in one state often trigger peer reviews in others.

## Action Items

- Audit current consent workflows for Alabama consumers — verify that express, itemized consent is obtained separately for initial collection, third-party transfers, secondary uses, and marketing.
- Update privacy notices for the DTC genetic testing context to satisfy the AGDPA's disclosure requirements, including transparency about de-identified data sharing for research.
- Implement consumer rights mechanisms: deletion within 30 days of request; third-party retrieval/return within 60 days of revocation.
- Eliminate any existing data-sharing agreements or practices that transfer genetic data to insurers or employers without express written consent.
- Establish a process for research transfers that satisfies 45 C.F.R. Part 46 (federal Common Rule) informed consent requirements.
- Assess whether any law enforcement data request policies adequately protect against voluntary, non-consensual disclosures.
- Map the company's full geographic compliance obligations across all state DTC genetic privacy laws and track ongoing legislative developments in 2025-2026.
