# FCC 2024 Net Neutrality Order Extends Section 222 Privacy Protections to Broadband -- Then Gets Vacated

**Jurisdiction:** Federal | **Category:** Privacy | **Date:** 2024-05-23

## Executive Summary [HIGH confidence]

On April 25, 2024, the Federal Communications Commission adopted a [Declaratory Ruling, Order, Report and Order, and Order on Reconsideration](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2024/05/22/2024-10674/safeguarding-and-securing-the-open-internet-restoring-internet-freedom) (the "Safeguarding Order" or "Open Internet Order") that reclassified broadband Internet access service (BIAS) as a "telecommunications service" under Title II of the Communications Act of 1934, in WC Docket Nos. 23-320 and 17-108 (FCC 24-52). The reclassification subjected BIAS providers to Section 222 of the Communications Act, which requires telecommunications carriers to protect the confidentiality of customer proprietary network information (CPNI). It also opened FCC authority under Section 201(b) to impose broader data security obligations on broadband providers. Privacy experts and law firms noted that the Safeguarding Order's privacy implications were as consequential as its net neutrality rules. The effective date was July 22, 2024. However, on January 2, 2025, the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Sixth Circuit [vacated the Safeguarding Order in its entirety](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/ca6/24-3449/24-3449-2025-01-02.html) in *Ohio Telecom Association v. FCC*, No. 24-3449, holding that broadband remains an unregulated "information service" and that the FCC lacked statutory authority to impose Title II treatment. Broadband providers' data protection obligations have reverted to the FTC's general unfair and deceptive practices authority under Section 5 of the FTC Act.

## Background [HIGH confidence]

### Broadband Classification History

The regulatory status of broadband Internet access has oscillated with changes in FCC composition. Under the 2015 Open Internet Order, the FCC classified BIAS as a telecommunications service under Title II, bringing it within the reach of Section 222 (CPNI) and Section 201(b) (just and reasonable practices). In 2017, the FCC's Restoring Internet Freedom Order (FCC 17-166, WC Docket No. 17-108) reversed course, reclassifying BIAS as an information service and stripping the FCC of its direct authority to regulate broadband privacy. In 2016, while BIAS was still a Title II service, the FCC had adopted broadband-specific privacy rules under Section 222 (FCC 16-148), but those rules were overturned by Congress under the Congressional Review Act in April 2017 before they could take effect.

The consequence of the 2017 reclassification was that broadband providers were removed from FCC privacy jurisdiction and left subject only to the Federal Trade Commission's authority to police unfair or deceptive acts and practices. The FTC, however, lacks the rulemaking authority to issue binding privacy regulations for common carriers, creating a regulatory gap for broadband data protection.

### The 2024 Open Internet NPRM and Order

Following a change in FCC leadership, the Commission initiated a new proceeding in September 2023 under WC Docket No. 23-320 to revisit broadband classification. On [April 25, 2024, the FCC voted 3-2](https://www.cooley.com/news/insight/2024/2024-05-08-fcc-reinstates-network-neutrality-rules) to adopt the Safeguarding Order, restoring BIAS classification as a telecommunications service under Title II and reinstating open Internet rules prohibiting blocking, throttling, and paid prioritization. The order was published in the Federal Register on [May 22, 2024](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2024/05/22/2024-10674/safeguarding-and-securing-the-open-internet-restoring-internet-freedom), with most provisions effective July 22, 2024.

### FTC-FCC Coordination

Alongside the Safeguarding Order, the FCC and FTC signed a [Memorandum of Understanding on April 29, 2024](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/2024-04-29-fcc-ftc-open-internet-mou.pdf), reinforcing coordination on broadband privacy and consumer protection enforcement. This reflected the two agencies' overlapping but legally distinct jurisdictions over ISPs.

## Detailed Analysis [HIGH confidence]

### Section 222 CPNI Obligations Applied to Broadband

The most significant privacy implication of the Safeguarding Order was the application of [Section 222 of the Communications Act](https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/FCC-24-52A1.pdf) to BIAS providers. Section 222 imposes three main categories of obligation on telecommunications carriers:

1. **Confidentiality of CPNI**: Carriers must protect the confidentiality of customer proprietary network information, which includes call records, routing data, and -- when applied to broadband -- information about the volume, timing, and destination of Internet traffic, as well as customer location data.
2. **Use restrictions**: Carriers may not use CPNI to market their own services or share CPNI with affiliates without customer permission. Under the 2024 Order, BIAS providers would have been required to obtain prior approval before using CPNI for cross-service marketing.
3. **Non-CPNI customer information**: Section 222(a) also imposes a general duty to protect the confidentiality of non-CPNI proprietary customer information, extending beyond the narrower CPNI definition.

The FCC acknowledged that it had not yet adopted BIAS-specific CPNI implementing rules. It waived existing voice-service CPNI rules "to the extent such rules are applicable to BIAS as a telecommunications service" pending new rulemaking. [Cooley LLP noted](https://www.cooley.com/news/insight/2024/2024-05-15-fcc-network-neutrality-order-shakes-up-broadband-privacy) that until new rules took effect, BIAS providers faced uncertainty about precisely how to comply, as the existing implementing rules were calibrated for voice services, not broadband.

### Section 201(b) Data Security Authority

Beyond Section 222, the Safeguarding Order recognized FCC authority under [Section 201(b)](https://www.privacyworld.blog/2024/06/the-fccs-net-neutrality-order-going-beyond-blocking-throttling-and-fast-lanes/) of the Communications Act to require BIAS providers to implement "just and reasonable" practices for data security and cybersecurity. The FCC announced it would initiate a rulemaking to require BIAS providers to adopt cybersecurity risk management plans -- a significant expansion of federal data security obligations for ISPs.

### Planned Rulemakings That Were Never Completed

The Safeguarding Order contemplated three new rulemakings that would have imposed substantive broadband privacy and security obligations:
- A BIAS-specific CPNI implementing rulemaking under Section 222
- An information collection requirements rulemaking for BIAS providers
- A cybersecurity risk management plan rulemaking

None of these rulemakings reached final rules before the Sixth Circuit vacated the Safeguarding Order in January 2025.

### Maryland Privacy Laws: Context Note [HIGH confidence]

The Keller and Heckman telecom alert that flagged this development also covered Maryland's newly enacted privacy laws (MODPA and the Maryland Age-Appropriate Design Code Act, both signed May 9, 2024). The Maryland privacy laws are extensively covered in this knowledge base -- see [Related Reports](#related-reports) below. Notably, the Maryland AADC explicitly exempts "telecommunications services" and "broadband internet access services" from its requirements, a fact that intersects with the FCC's then-pending reclassification of broadband.

### Sixth Circuit Vacatur: *Ohio Telecom Association v. FCC* [HIGH confidence]

The Safeguarding Order was immediately challenged. On January 2, 2025, a unanimous three-judge panel of the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Sixth Circuit [set aside the Order in its entirety](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/ca6/24-3449/24-3449-2025-01-02.html) in *Ohio Telecom Ass'n v. FCC*, No. 24-3449. The court held that the FCC's classification of BIAS as a telecommunications service was inconsistent with the plain language of the Communications Act and that broadband remains an "information service." The decision relied heavily on the Supreme Court's 2024 ruling in *Loper Bright Enterprises v. Raimondo*, which ended Chevron deference to agency statutory interpretations, writing that "[a]pplying Loper Bright means we can end the FCC's vacillations."

The [Congressional Research Service analyzed](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/LSB11264) the decision as the first major telecommunications ruling after *Loper Bright*, noting that any future FCC effort to impose net neutrality or broadband privacy rules under Title II "would require clear congressional authorization." The [Gibson Dunn analysis](https://www.gibsondunn.com/sixth-circuit-strikes-down-fcc-net-neutrality-order/) noted that this decision also undermines the legal basis for any Section 222-based broadband privacy framework, restoring the pre-2024 status quo in which BIAS providers are subject only to the FTC's Section 5 authority.

### Current Status: FCC Data Breach Rules Separately Upheld

It is important to note that the Sixth Circuit's January 2025 decision applied only to the net neutrality / Title II reclassification order. Separately, the FCC adopted data breach reporting rules for telecommunications carriers in 2024 under Section 222. In a distinct proceeding (*Ohio Telecom Ass'n v. FCC*, [No. 24-3133](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/ca6/24-3133/24-3133-2025-08-13.html)), those data breach notification rules were [upheld by the Sixth Circuit on August 13, 2025](https://www.dwt.com/blogs/privacy--security-law-blog/2025/08/fcc-telecom-carrier-data-breach-order-upheld) as within the FCC's authority over voice carriers. The data breach rules do not currently apply to pure broadband providers. However, that ruling did not become final: petitioners filed a petition for en banc rehearing on September 29, 2025, and on October 7, 2025, the Sixth Circuit [held the case in abeyance](https://broadbandbreakfast.com/fcc-data-breach-order-under-review-agency-says/) at the FCC's own request while the agency internally reviews whether to maintain the Data Breach Order. As of April 2026, the data breach rules remain in abeyance and their ultimate fate depends on the outcome of that FCC review.

## Impact Assessment [MEDIUM confidence]

### Current Landscape: Broadband Privacy Under FTC Authority

Following the January 2025 vacatur, BIAS providers are once again regulated exclusively by the FTC for data privacy and security. The FTC enforces privacy obligations under its Section 5 unfair and deceptive practices authority but does not have rulemaking authority over common carriers. This creates an asymmetry: voice carriers remain subject to FCC Section 222 CPNI rules, while broadband providers -- even if offered by the same company -- are subject only to FTC oversight.

### ISP and Broadband Provider Implications

ISPs offering BIAS (cable, fiber, mobile broadband operators) are not currently required to:
- Obtain prior consent before using customer data for cross-service marketing
- Comply with CPNI-based data minimization obligations
- Implement FCC-mandated cybersecurity risk management plans for broadband networks

Privacy advocates have noted this regulatory gap. The [Electronic Privacy Information Center](https://epic.org/sixth-circuit-rejects-fcc-authority-over-isps/) and [Public Knowledge](https://publicknowledge.org/sixth-circuit-ruling-on-fcc-authority-threatens-consumer-protections-and-open-internet/) warned that the vacatur leaves broadband consumers with fewer enforceable privacy protections than voice customers.

### Legislative Gap and Congressional Action Required

The Sixth Circuit's ruling and the *Loper Bright* analysis mean that substantive broadband privacy rules at the federal level would require a congressional statute. Absent such legislation, the patchwork of state privacy laws -- including MODPA in Maryland and the California Consumer Privacy Act -- provide the primary consumer privacy protections applicable to ISPs in those states. State laws of general application covering controllers and processors of personal data may capture ISPs' data practices in states with comprehensive privacy laws.

### FTC as Backstop

The FTC retains authority to bring enforcement actions against ISPs for deceptive privacy practices under Section 5 and for violations of FTC privacy regulations applicable to non-common carriers. The FTC's 2021 ISP surveillance study documented extensive data collection practices by major broadband providers, and FTC enforcement remains the primary federal check on ISP data misuse.

## Action Items

- Broadband providers and ISPs should confirm their current privacy disclosures and practices are aligned with FTC Section 5 standards, as that is the operative federal privacy authority following the vacatur.
- Companies should monitor any FCC efforts to seek congressional authorization for broadband privacy regulation, or any new FCC rulemaking that identifies a non-Title II statutory basis for BIAS data protection rules.
- Compliance teams should assess applicability of state comprehensive privacy laws (including Maryland MODPA, California CCPA/CPRA, and the growing list of state laws effective 2025-2026) to their broadband data processing activities.
- Organizations relying on ISP data services should review their vendor contracts to understand what data access ISPs retain regarding their network traffic.
- Track any petition for certiorari or congressional response to *Ohio Telecom Ass'n v. FCC* that could alter the regulatory landscape.
