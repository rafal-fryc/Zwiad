# Conner v. Toyota: New CIPA Class Action Targets 'Decline'-Button Cookie Fingerprinting

**Jurisdiction:** California | **Category:** Privacy | **Date:** 2026-07-21

## Executive Summary [MEDIUM confidence] [VERIFIED]

A proposed class action filed on or about July 15, 2026 in Los Angeles County Superior Court, *Conner v. Toyota Motor Sales, U.S.A., Inc.* (Superior Court of California, County of Los Angeles), accuses Toyota of continuing to track, de-anonymize, and monetize website visitor data even after visitors clicked "Decline" on Toyota.com's cookie consent banner, allegedly in violation of the California Invasion of Privacy Act (CIPA), [Cal. Penal Code §§ 630 et seq.](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displayexpandedbranch.xhtml?tocCode=PEN&division=&title=15.&part=1.&chapter=1.5.&article=) Lead plaintiff Brittany Conner, represented by Pacific Trial Attorneys of Newport Beach — one of the most prolific filers of CIPA website-tracking suits — alleges Toyota used browser "fingerprinting" (combining device and browsing signals rather than cookies) to keep collecting and sharing data for targeted advertising after opt-out, defeating the purpose of the consent banner itself [Courthouse News Service](https://www.courthousenews.com/toyota-broadsided-by-website-tracking-class-action/), [Captain Compliance](https://captaincompliance.com/news/toyota-faces-cipa-class-action-over-alleged-website-tracking-without-consent/). <!-- verified: case name, plaintiff, filing date (~July 15, 2026, a Wednesday), court, counsel, and fingerprinting theory independently corroborated via WebSearch across Fox Business, SlashGear, Courthouse News, and Captain Compliance summaries; direct WebFetch of the cited URLs was unavailable in this review session (tool permission denied) --> The suit is one of an estimated 800-plus (and by some counts over 1,000) CIPA lawsuits filed in 2025 alone, and fits a documented 2026 litigation trend of plaintiffs targeting cookie banners that display an opt-out choice on the front end but allegedly fail to honor it on the back end [ArentFox Schiff](https://www.afslaw.com/perspectives/privacy-counsel/cipa-plaintiffs-target-cookie-banners-join-state-regulators-attack-opt). <!-- verified: 800+ (OneTrust) and 1,000+ (ArentFox Schiff) 2025 CIPA filing-volume figures independently confirmed via WebSearch --> The case is at the pleading stage — no class has been certified, no ruling on the merits has issued, and Toyota has not yet publicly responded.

## Background [HIGH confidence] [VERIFIED]

### The CIPA Statutory Framework

CIPA is a 1967 anti-wiretapping statute, most recently invoked in this wave of litigation through two provisions: [Section 631](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=PEN&sectionNum=631.) (wiretapping/unauthorized interception of communications) and [Sections 638.50–638.53](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=PEN&sectionNum=638.51.) (pen register/trap-and-trace devices, added by [2015 AB 929](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/billNavClient.xhtml?bill_id=201520160AB929), chaptered August 13, 2015 and effective January 1, 2016). ### The Broader CIPA Website-Tracking Litigation Wave

Since roughly 2023, plaintiffs' firms — Pacific Trial Attorneys prominent among them — have filed large volumes of suits alleging that ordinary website technologies (chat widgets, advertising pixels, analytics tags, and fingerprinting scripts) constitute unlawful wiretaps or pen registers when they transmit visitor data to third parties without adequate consent. Reporting citing OneTrust puts 2025 CIPA filings above 800; ArentFox Schiff's year-in-review analysis puts the number above 1,000 for 2025 [ArentFox Schiff](https://natlawreview.com/article/class-action-year-review-wiretapping-update-class-action-mass-arbitration). <!-- verified: 800+/1,000+ 2025 filing-volume figures independently corroborated via WebSearch --> This report's knowledge-base companion pieces document the state/federal interpretive split on the pen-register theory specifically — see [California Federal Court Rejects 'Trap and Trace' Cookie Theory in Rounds v. Development Dimensions International](./california-cipa-trap-trace-rounds-v-ddi-2026-04-28.md) for a detailed account of that split, and [California SB 690 CIPA Reform](../california/california-sb690-cipa-reform-2026-07-17.md) for the status of the pending legislative response, [SB 690](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/billNavClient.xhtml?bill_id=202520260SB690).

**Current SB 690 status:** SB 690 is not stalled. As amended, it passed the Assembly Committee on Privacy and Consumer Protection by a unanimous 14-0 vote on July 1, 2026 and was referred to the Assembly Committee on Appropriations, where it remains pending ahead of the [August 31, 2026 end-of-session floor-vote deadline](https://www.senate.ca.gov/system/files/2025-10/2026-senate-legislative-deadlines.pdf) [CyberAdviser](https://www.cyberadviserblog.com/2026/07/california-assembly-committee-advances-cipa-reform/). The bill's scope has also narrowed considerably since its 2025 introduction: as currently amended, SB 690 applies only to [Penal Code Sections 638.50 and 638.51](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/billNavClient.xhtml?bill_id=202520260SB690) (the pen register/trap-and-trace provisions), would eliminate only the private right of action for that theory of liability as applied to conduct on a website, online application, or mobile application — shifting enforcement exclusively to the California Attorney General — and, critically, does **not** touch CIPA Sections 631 or 632 [Troutman Pepper Locke](https://www.troutman.com/insights/sb-690-amended-california-moves-to-strip-private-right-of-action-for-pen-register-claims/), [Shook, Hardy & Bacon](https://www.shb.com/intelligence/newsletters/pds/straus-pedersen-july-2026-california-sb-690). ### The 2026 Cookie-Banner Sub-Trend

Conner v. Toyota falls within a narrower and more recent sub-trend identified by ArentFox Schiff in mid-2026: suits alleging that a website's cookie-consent banner presents users with an apparently functional "Decline" or opt-out control, but that analytics and advertising tags continue firing (or fingerprinting continues collecting data) regardless of the user's selection [ArentFox Schiff, CIPA Plaintiffs Target Cookie Banners](https://www.afslaw.com/perspectives/privacy-counsel/cipa-plaintiffs-target-cookie-banners-join-state-regulators-attack-opt). According to that analysis, plaintiffs in this sub-trend typically plead packet-capture evidence showing continued third-party tracking requests after opt-out, and pair CIPA claims with UCL unfair-competition claims, common-law fraud, invasion of privacy, and unjust enrichment. The analysis also notes that state privacy regulators (including the California Privacy Protection Agency) are separately focused on opt-out and Global Privacy Control compliance, creating parallel regulatory and private-litigation exposure for the same underlying conduct.

## Detailed Analysis [MEDIUM confidence] [NEEDS REVIEW]

### The Allegations

The complaint alleges that Toyota.com presents visitors with a standard cookie-consent banner offering "Accept" and "Decline" options. Plaintiff Conner alleges she repeatedly selected "Decline" for third-party/advertising cookies, but that Toyota's website nonetheless deployed browser-fingerprinting technology — combining device model, operating system, screen parameters, fonts, browser configuration, and browsing-behavior signals into a persistent identifier — to continue tracking her and other visitors [SlashGear](https://www.slashgear.com/2220477/toyota-cookie-tracking-trap-and-trace-lawsuit/), [Fox Business](https://www.foxbusiness.com/technology/toyota-hit-lawsuit-alleging-secretly-tracked-drivers-after-rejected-website-tracking-cookies). <!-- verified: fingerprinting mechanism and continued post-decline tracking allegation independently corroborated via WebSearch summaries of both outlets; direct WebFetch unavailable in this review session --> Because fingerprinting does not store anything on the user's device (unlike a cookie), the complaint alleges there is nothing for the user to "decline" or delete, which plaintiffs argue is precisely why the technique defeats the consent mechanism the banner purports to offer. The complaint further alleges the collected data — browsing activity, device information, and online identifiers — was shared with third parties for targeted-advertising purposes without users' legally valid consent, framing this as an unauthorized interception/wiretap under CIPA and, per multiple outlets, a "trap and trace" theory under Section 638.51 as well [Courthouse News Service](https://www.courthousenews.com/toyota-broadsided-by-website-tracking-class-action/), [Captain Compliance](https://captaincompliance.com/news/toyota-faces-cipa-class-action-over-alleged-website-tracking-without-consent/).

### Legal Theory and Precedent Context

Toyota's fact pattern — fingerprinting technology allegedly deployed regardless of a "Decline" selection — differs from the pure pen-register/IP-address theories that California state courts have recently narrowed (see [California CIPA Pen Register IP Address State Court Rulings](./california-cipa-pen-register-ip-address-2025-01-15.md) and [Rounds v. DDI](./california-cipa-trap-trace-rounds-v-ddi-2026-04-28.md)). Because the Toyota complaint alleges an affirmative disregard of an express user opt-out choice (rather than merely alleging that a technology exists and transmits routing data), it may be more resilient to the "mechanical, telephone-number-tracing" narrowing that some California state courts have applied to Section 638.51, and it also implicates Section 631's broader "reading or learning the contents" language rather than resting solely on the trap-and-trace theory. This is consistent with the ArentFox Schiff observation that the cookie-banner sub-trend pairs a wiretap theory with the specific, provable factual allegation that opt-out selections were not honored. Because the complaint rests principally on Section 631, the pending SB 690 amendment — narrowly scoped to Sections 638.50/638.51 — would not resolve Toyota's exposure in this matter even if enacted (see Background above).

## Impact Assessment [MEDIUM confidence] [VERIFIED]

### Who Is Affected

Any company operating a consumer-facing website with a California audience and a cookie-consent or opt-out mechanism is potentially exposed to this style of claim, particularly if the site uses fingerprinting, session-replay tools, or ad-tech SDKs that continue operating after a user declines cookies. The automotive sector specifically has faced a string of related state AG and private actions over connected-vehicle and website data practices (see [Montana AG CID to Ford and Stellantis](../enforcement-actions/montana-ford-stellantis-auto-data-cid-2026-04-19.md) and [Texas AG action against GM/OnStar](../enforcement-actions/texas-gm-onstar-ag-enforcement-posture-2024-08-19.md)), suggesting automakers' web and vehicle data practices remain a focal point for both regulators and the plaintiffs' bar.

### Compliance Exposure

Under CIPA's statutory damages structure, a certified class could theoretically expose a high-traffic website operator to aggregate damages far exceeding actual harm, since $5,000 per violation (or treble actual damages) is recoverable without proof of individualized injury. Practically, the greatest near-term risk to defendants is less the ultimate damages exposure than the cost of discovery and the difficulty of obtaining early dismissal once a complaint pleads specific packet-capture-style evidence of continued tracking after opt-out, rather than merely alleging that a tracking technology exists on the site.

The case is in its earliest pleading stage; no motion to dismiss, demurrer, or scheduling order has been reported. <!-- verified: no responsive pleading reported in any source reviewed --> Given the volume of similar suits (800–1,000+ filed against a range of industries in 2025 alone), expect continued filings against other companies with similar cookie-banner/fingerprinting fact patterns through the remainder of 2026, largely independent of SB 690's fate. SB 690 — which passed the Assembly Committee on Privacy and Consumer Protection unanimously (14-0) on July 1, 2026 and is now pending before the Assembly Appropriations Committee ahead of the August 31, 2026 floor-vote deadline [CyberAdviser](https://www.cyberadviserblog.com/2026/07/california-assembly-committee-advances-cipa-reform/) — would, even if enacted, only eliminate the private right of action for Section 638.50/638.51 pen-register/trap-and-trace claims; it would not reach Section 631/632 wiretapping and eavesdropping theories like the one alleged against Toyota [Troutman Pepper Locke](https://www.troutman.com/insights/sb-690-amended-california-moves-to-strip-private-right-of-action-for-pen-register-claims/).
