# AI Transcription Tools Face Growing Wiretap and Consent Scrutiny as Otter.ai Litigation Tests the Boundaries

**Jurisdiction:** Federal, California, Illinois, and other all-party consent states | **Category:** privacy | **Date:** 2026-07-22

## Executive Summary [MEDIUM confidence] [VERIFIED]

The rapid, largely unmanaged adoption of AI meeting transcription and notetaking tools -- Otter.ai, Fireflies, Microsoft Copilot, Zoom's built-in transcription service, and similar products -- is generating a new wave of privacy and wiretap-law exposure that most organizations have not yet governed. The originating item for this report is an IAPP Daily Dashboard entry relaying *Wall Street Journal* reporting that individuals are increasingly recording and transcribing meetings and conversations without obtaining consent from all participants, with UCLA law lecturer Alex Alben warning that unauthorized recordings by AI notetakers will likely generate personnel complaints and legal exposure going forward. This consumer/workplace trend is not merely anecdotal: it is now the subject of active federal litigation. A consolidated federal class action, *In re Otter.AI Privacy Litigation*, No. 5:25-cv-06911 (N.D. Cal.), alleges that Otter's "OtterPilot" AI notetaker joins meetings and records and transcribes all participants -- not just the host who invited it -- without the consent required under the federal Electronic Communications Privacy Act (ECPA), the federal Computer Fraud and Abuse Act (CFAA), California's two-party-consent wiretapping statute (CIPA, Penal Code §§ 631-632), and California's Comprehensive Computer Data Access and Fraud Act (CDAFA), among other theories described below. A related claim under Illinois' Biometric Information Privacy Act (BIPA) for voiceprint capture is pled separately by Illinois-resident plaintiffs in one of the four individual suits folded into the consolidated action, rather than as one of the seven causes of action asserted in the master consolidated complaint itself (see Detailed Analysis for the corrected breakdown). A ruling on Otter's motion to dismiss, argued May 20, 2026, remained pending as of this report's July 25, 2026 update; the court subsequently reset the matter for a combined motion hearing and initial case management conference on July 15, 2026, and no ruling had been publicly reported as of the most recent sources reviewed for this update. The case is being closely watched as a bellwether for the entire AI-notetaker industry, including Fireflies, Fathom, Granola, and the built-in AI features of Zoom and Microsoft Teams, because it will test whether host-only consent to add a bot is legally sufficient in the twelve U.S. jurisdictions that require all-party consent to record. <!-- verified -->

## Background: The Legal Framework for Recording Consent [LOW confidence] [NEEDS REVIEW]

Federal law under the Electronic Communications Privacy Act (ECPA) generally sets a one-party consent baseline: it is not unlawful to intercept a wire, oral, or electronic communication where the person intercepting is a party to the communication or where one party has given prior consent, unless the interception is for a criminal or tortious purpose, per the statutory summary reflected in [Cornell LII's overview of 18 U.S.C. § 2511](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/2511) and [FindLaw's codification](https://codes.findlaw.com/us/title-18-crimes-and-criminal-procedure/18-usc-sect-2511/). However, a substantial minority of states impose a stricter, all-party ("two-party") consent requirement. According to [Recording Law's 2026 state-by-state guide](https://www.recordinglaw.com/us-laws/ai-meeting-recording-laws/), twelve states require all-party consent for recording: California, Connecticut, Delaware, Florida, Illinois, Maryland, Massachusetts, Montana, New Hampshire, Oregon, Pennsylvania, and Washington. <!-- verified --> In these states, an AI notetaker that records a meeting based on the consent of only the host or organizer -- while other participants are unaware or have not affirmatively agreed -- can create both civil liability and, in some states, criminal exposure.

California's Invasion of Privacy Act (Penal Code §§ 631 and 632) is the most litigated of these statutes. Multiple independent secondary legal sources -- including [Shouse Law Group's explainer](https://www.shouselaw.com/ca/defense/penal-code/632/), [Esfandi Law Firm](https://www.esfandilawfirm.com/penal-code-632), and [EG Attorneys](https://www.egattorneys.com/eavesdropping-laws-penal-code-632-pc) -- describe Penal Code § 632 as criminalizing the intentional recording of a "confidential communication" (one in which a party has an objectively reasonable expectation that no one is secretly recording or listening in) without the consent of all parties, punishable as a misdemeanor or felony with fines up to $2,500 per violation and imprisonment. <!-- verified --> Illinois' Biometric Information Privacy Act (BIPA) adds a separate layer of exposure specific to voice biometrics: AI transcription tools that process speech to identify or distinguish speakers can create "voiceprints," which BIPA treats as biometric identifiers requiring advance written notice and consent before collection, according to [Goodwin's April 2026 client alert](https://www.goodwinlaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/04/alerts-practices-dpc-ai-transcription-tools-under-scrutiny).

## Detailed Analysis: The Otter.ai Litigation and the Industry-Wide Trend [MEDIUM confidence] [VERIFIED]

The clearest legal test case for this trend is *In re Otter.AI Privacy Litigation*, pending in the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California before Judge Eumi K. Lee. According to the case docket as summarized by [CourtListener](https://www.courtlistener.com/docket/71118721/brewer-v-otterai-inc/) and corroborated by [the National Law Review](https://natlawreview.com/article/take-note-new-wave-privacy-litigation-targets-ai-notetaker-otterai) and [Recording Law's case explainer](https://www.recordinglaw.com/news/otter-ai-wiretap-lawsuit-explained/), the litigation began with *Brewer v. Otter.ai Inc.*, filed August 15, 2025, by a California plaintiff who alleged his February 2025 sales call was recorded and transcribed because another call participant had Otter's "OtterPilot" feature running -- without Brewer's own consent. Three additional related suits followed -- including *Walker v. Otter.ai Inc.*, No. 5:25-cv-07187 (N.D. Cal., filed August 26, 2025) -- and Judge Lee consolidated all four into the lead case (No. 5:25-cv-06911) on October 22, 2025; a consolidated class action complaint was filed December 5, 2025.

**Correction to the causes of action (per reviewer verification):** The consolidated complaint asserts seven causes of action, corroborated across [the National Law Review's original analysis](https://natlawreview.com/article/take-note-new-wave-privacy-litigation-targets-ai-notetaker-otterai), [a follow-up National Law Review analysis of the complaint's specific claims](https://natlawreview.com/article/ai-notetaking-tools-under-fire-lessons-otterai-class-action-complaint), [Fisher Phillips' client alert on the litigation](https://www.fisherphillips.com/en/insights/insights/new-lawsuit-highlights-concerns-about-ai-notetakers), and [tldv.io's litigation tracker](https://tldv.io/blog/ai-meeting-recorder-lawsuits/): (1) violation of the federal Wiretap Act/ECPA; (2) violation of the federal Computer Fraud and Abuse Act (CFAA); (3) violation of California Penal Code § 631 (CIPA); (4) violation of California Penal Code § 632 (CIPA); (5) violation of California's Comprehensive Computer Data Access and Fraud Act (CDAFA); (6) common-law intrusion upon seclusion; and (7) common-law conversion. <!-- verified: independently confirmed via WebSearch corroboration of National Law Review, Mondaq, and Workplace Privacy Report coverage of the same seven causes of action --> **Illinois' Biometric Information Privacy Act (BIPA) is not one of these seven consolidated causes of action.** It is instead pled as a separate claim specific to the Illinois-resident plaintiffs (Jasper and Michael Walker) within *Walker v. The Walker plaintiffs allege that Otter's software captured their voiceprints during Zoom meetings without the advance written notice and consent BIPA requires, and seek to represent a class of individuals whose voiceprints Otter collected while they were located in Illinois (a presence-based class definition, distinct from an Illinois-residency-based class), per independent corroboration of the Walker complaint's class definition language across multiple secondary sources in this revision round. <!-- verified: class definition wording corrected in round-2 revision to match presence-based framing ("while located in Illinois") independently corroborated during reviewer re-verification --> This report's original draft mischaracterized BIPA as one of the seven consolidated causes of action and omitted CDAFA, intrusion upon seclusion, and conversion; that has been corrected here. The core theory across the consolidated action is that Otter obtains consent, at most, from the meeting host who installs or invites the OtterPilot bot, but not from the other call participants whose voices are recorded, transcribed, and -- the plaintiffs allege -- potentially used to help train Otter's underlying models. Otter has moved to dismiss; Judge Lee heard oral argument on the motion on May 20, 2026.

The Otter litigation is widely described by industry commentators as a bellwether for the broader category of "AI notetaker" products. [tldv.io's comparative analysis](https://tldv.io/blog/ai-meeting-recorder-lawsuits/) notes that Zoom's built-in AI Companion is generally regarded as having the strongest consent architecture among competitors -- participants cannot unmute until they affirmatively acknowledge a recording notice -- while bolt-on third-party bots like Otter and Fireflies, which join meetings as a separate silent participant added by the host, carry comparatively higher legal exposure because non-host participants may never see an equivalent notice. This is consistent with the trend identified in the underlying IAPP item for this finding and in [IAPP's related analysis, "The second wave of AI governance: The risks of ubiquitous transcription tools"](https://iapp.org/news/a/the-second-wave-of-ai-governance-the-risks-of-ubiquitous-transcription-tools), which argues that most organizations completed a "first wave" of AI governance focused on employees pasting sensitive data into generative AI prompts, but have not yet built governance structures for tools that passively capture what employees say in meetings without deliberate input -- specifically naming Otter, Fireflies, Microsoft Copilot, and Zoom's transcription service as ubiquitous but ungoverned in most workplaces.

The consumer/education angle raised in the underlying WSJ reporting is corroborated by a parallel, unrelated report from UCLA. [The Daily Bruin](https://dailybruin.com/2025/12/04/ucla-instructors-express-privacy-concerns-amid-rise-of-ai-notetaking-platforms-2) reported in December 2025 that UCLA instructors expressed concern over students' use of AI notetaking tools such as Otter, Krisp, and Notion in classrooms and recorded Zoom lectures, noting that California's two-party consent rule applies to this setting. UCLA law lecturer Alex Alben -- the same individual quoted in the WSJ/IAPP item underlying this finding -- told the Daily Bruin that both students and instructors should be affirmatively aware when a recording is occurring and should receive confirmation of how the recording will be used, and separately told the WSJ (as relayed via IAPP) that employers "need to be careful about technologies that are automatically recording, especially when it involves third parties." This corroboration across two independent reporting contexts (workplace meetings via WSJ/IAPP, and higher-education classrooms via the Daily Bruin) supports the MEDIUM confidence rating for this section, notwithstanding that the original WSJ article itself was not directly retrievable; the IAPP tracking URL provided in the finding (`info.iapp.org/...`) could not be fetched directly in this session, and its content was reconstructed from corroborating secondary coverage rather than the primary WSJ text. **[NEEDS REVIEW]** <!-- needs-human-review: primary WSJ article and IAPP tracking URL remain unfetched in this review round as well; corroboration is via secondary sources only -->

## Impact Assessment [MEDIUM confidence] [NEEDS REVIEW]

**Employers and enterprises** deploying or permitting AI meeting transcription tools face direct wiretap and biometric-privacy exposure in the twelve all-party consent states identified above, particularly where a tool is added by a meeting host without an affirmative, informed opt-in from every other participant. [Goodwin's April 2026 client alert](https://www.goodwinlaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/04/alerts-practices-dpc-ai-transcription-tools-under-scrutiny) frames this as a governance gap distinct from generative-AI chatbot risk: the risk here arises passively, from what the tool captures, rather than from what an employee deliberately inputs. Multi-jurisdictional meetings compound the risk, since [Recording Law's guidance](https://www.recordinglaw.com/us-laws/ai-meeting-recording-laws/) explains that when participants are in different states, the strictest applicable consent law generally governs the entire call -- meaning a single participant located in an all-party consent state can trigger all-party consent obligations for an otherwise one-party-consent meeting.

**AI notetaker vendors** (Otter.ai chief among them, but also Fireflies, Fathom, Granola, and similar products) face the immediate risk that the pending *In re Otter.AI Privacy Litigation* motion-to-dismiss ruling will set an influential precedent on whether host-only consent is legally sufficient, and on whether AI bots that join meetings as a distinct "participant" are properly analogized to a human eavesdropper for wiretap-law purposes. A ruling denying the motion to dismiss would likely accelerate copycat litigation against competing vendors (a related BIPA suit against Fireflies was already filed December 18, 2025) and increase pressure on all vendors to adopt Zoom-style hard consent gates (blocking unmute/participation until every attendee acknowledges the recording). Some vendors are already moving in this direction independent of a ruling: Otter reportedly added more visible bot-join notifications, and platform-level defenses are also emerging, with Google Meet and Microsoft Teams reportedly now flagging or routing third-party notetaker bots as suspected risks by default in 2026, per [tldv.io's analysis](https://tldv.io/blog/ai-meeting-recorder-lawsuits/) -- these platform-side and vendor-side changes are noted here as directionally relevant but have not been independently corroborated beyond a single tracker source, so they should not be treated as fully verified industry consensus. <!-- needs-human-review: independent WebSearch in this review round did not surface a second independent source confirming Google Meet/Teams default bot-flagging; treat as single-source and unverified -->

**Educational institutions** face a related but distinct exposure, illustrated by UCLA's internal concerns: students independently installing AI notetakers in recorded classes creates the same all-party consent problem as in the corporate context, without a clear institutional policy owner.

Because the underlying finding is a trend/commentary piece rather than a discrete legislative or regulatory action, there is no compliance deadline attached to this development. The practical timeline to watch is the outcome of Otter's motion to dismiss: argued May 20, 2026, with a combined motion hearing/initial case management conference subsequently set for July 15, 2026. As of this report's July 25, 2026 update, no ruling had been publicly reported following that conference, and this report could not independently re-confirm docket activity after July 15 due to a WebFetch tooling limitation in this session (see Recent Developments above); readers should check the docket directly for the current status. Unverified secondary reporting of possible settlement discussions in at least one underlying case should be treated as a separate, lower-confidence signal to monitor rather than a confirmed development.
