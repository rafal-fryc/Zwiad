# Class Action Wave Exposes Data Privacy Risk in Healthcare and Healthcare AI Acquisitions

**Jurisdiction:** Federal (litigation pending in Illinois federal and state courts; claims implicate multi-state genetic and health privacy statutes) | **Category:** Privacy | **Date:** July 20, 2026

## Executive Summary [MEDIUM confidence]

A wave of 2026 class-action lawsuits — most prominently *Farrier et al. v. Tempus AI, Inc.* (N.D. Ill., consolidated April 15, 2026) and *B.W. v. Invitae Corp. / Labcorp* (Cook County, Ill. Circuit Court) — alleges that healthcare and healthcare-AI acquirers obtained patients' genetic and health information as part of M&A transactions and then used or disclosed that data (including for AI model training and third-party licensing) without adequate patient notice or consent. Both suits center on the Illinois Genetic Information Privacy Act (GIPA), 410 ILCS 513/1 et seq., which requires written, use-specific consent before genetic data is disclosed and imposes statutory damages of $2,500 per negligent violation and $15,000 per willful/reckless violation. Commentators, including [Proskauer, as syndicated by the National Law Review](https://natlawreview.com/article/recent-class-actions-expose-data-privacy-risk-acquisitions-healthcare-and), argue these cases signal that data-driven healthcare and health-AI acquisitions carry latent privacy liability that survives closing — exposing acquirers to successor liability, statutory damages potentially reaching hundreds of millions of dollars in the aggregate, and heightened scrutiny under HIPAA and Section 5 of the FTC Act. The trend reinforces that pre-closing privacy diligence — confirming the legal basis for data transfer and continued use — is now a first-order M&A risk factor in this sector, not a peripheral compliance checkbox.

## Background [MEDIUM confidence]

Healthcare and healthcare-AI M&A activity has remained active through 2026, with acquirers paying premiums for targets holding large, proprietary clinical and genomic datasets that can be used to train or improve AI diagnostic and research tools, per [MobiHealthNews reporting on 2026 healthcare M&A trends](https://www.mobihealthnews.com/news/healthcare-ma-surges-2026-biopharma-medtech-drive-dealmaking). That same data — genetic test results, diagnoses, and other protected health information — is precisely the category of information subject to the strictest state and federal privacy protections, creating tension between the commercial rationale for these deals and the consent-based frameworks under which the underlying data was originally collected.

Genetic data carries a distinct legal profile from general health data. Illinois's GIPA (enacted 1998, most recently amended to extend to biomarker testing) was designed to prevent genetic discrimination by requiring that genetic testing information be treated as "confidential and privileged" and disclosed only to the tested individual or to persons the individual has specifically authorized **in writing**, per the [official Illinois Compiled Statutes text at ilga.gov](https://www.ilga.gov/legislation/ilcs/ilcs3.asp?ActID=1567&ChapterID=35). Because GIPA (like Illinois's better-known Biometric Information Privacy Act) creates a private right of action with statutory damages, it has become the preferred vehicle for plaintiffs' firms pursuing genetic-data claims nationally, including against employers and insurers, per [Lexology: Employers and Insurance Companies Continue to be Targeted with Deluge of Claims Under GIPA](https://natlawreview.com/article/employers-and-insurance-companies-continue-be-targeted-deluge-claims-under-illinois).

Two acquisitions completed in 2024-2025 are now the subject of the class actions driving this trend:

- **Invitae → Labcorp (2024):** Invitae Corporation, a genetic testing company, filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy on February 13, 2024. Laboratory Corporation of America Holdings (Labcorp) acquired substantially all of Invitae's assets for $239 million in a bankruptcy sale that closed August 5, 2024, per [Labcorp's own announcement](https://labcorp.com/invitae-receives-court-approval-sale-labcorp) and [PR Newswire](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/invitae-receives-court-approval-for-sale-to-labcorp-302138770.html).
- **Tempus AI → Ambry Genetics (February 2025):** Tempus AI, Inc., a Chicago-based healthcare AI company, acquired Ambry Genetics Corporation for approximately $600 million, adding a genetic-testing database reported at over one million tests to Tempus's clinical and molecular data library (which the company describes as including 45 million de-identified patient records), per [Fisher Phillips](https://www.jdsupra.com/legalnews/ai-company-s-acquisition-of-genetic-3569094/) and [HIPAA Journal](https://www.hipaajournal.com/tempus-ai-class-action-alawsuit-genetic-data-disclosures/).

This litigation trend follows, and is thematically related to, the separate California AG enforcement action against Chrome Holding Co. (formerly 23andMe) over its 2023 data breach — see Related Reports below — which similarly tests how genetic-data liability travels through corporate transactions (there, a bankruptcy asset sale rather than an operating acquisition).

## Detailed Analysis [MEDIUM confidence]

**Farrier et al. v. Tempus AI, Inc.** Multiple lawsuits filed against Tempus AI were consolidated into a single amended complaint, *Farrier et al. v. Tempus AI, Inc.*, No. 1:26-cv-04246, filed April 15, 2026 in the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of Illinois, per [docket records on Justia](https://dockets.justia.com/docket/illinois/ilndce/1:2026cv04246/498888) and [PacerMonitor](https://www.pacermonitor.com/public/case/64149379/Farrier_et_al_v_Tempus_AI,_Inc). Seven named plaintiffs from Illinois, California, New York, Michigan, Florida, Georgia, and West Virginia allege they provided genetic samples to Ambry Genetics for medical testing with an expectation of confidentiality, and that when Tempus AI acquired Ambry, their genetic data was transferred and then commercially exploited — used to train Tempus's AI tools and licensed to more than 70 third-party life-sciences partners in deals the complaint values at a combined $1.1 billion — without the plaintiffs' knowledge or consent, per [Fisher Phillips' summary of the complaint](https://www.jdsupra.com/legalnews/ai-company-s-acquisition-of-genetic-3569094/) and [Law.com's coverage](https://www.law.com/corpcounsel/2026/04/02/suits-against-tempus-ai-test-legal-lines-for-mining-genetic-data/?slreturn=20260724143632). The consolidated complaint asserts 21 counts spanning GIPA, the California Confidentiality of Medical Information Act, and consumer-protection statutes in Florida, Georgia, Michigan, New York, and West Virginia, alongside common-law claims for negligence, fraudulent concealment, unjust enrichment, conversion, breach of implied and express contract, breach of fiduciary duty, and intrusion-based invasion of privacy, per [HIPAA Journal](https://www.hipaajournal.com/tempus-ai-class-action-alawsuit-genetic-data-disclosures/) and [GenomeWeb](https://www.genomeweb.com/cancer/tempus-ai-sued-breach-genetic-information-privacy-act). As of this writing, these remain unproven allegations; Tempus AI has not yet had the opportunity to respond in a filed answer, per [Fisher Phillips](https://www.jdsupra.com/legalnews/ai-company-s-acquisition-of-genetic-3569094/).

**B.W. v. Invitae Corp. (and Labcorp).** A separate class action, filed by Illinois resident Julie Wiess on behalf of her minor son ("B.W.") in the Circuit Court of Cook County, Illinois, Chancery Division, alleges that Invitae unlawfully disclosed patients' protected genetic information to Labcorp in connection with the 2024 bankruptcy asset sale, without first obtaining the written consent GIPA requires, per [ClassAction.org](https://www.classaction.org/news/invitae-unlawfully-shared-patients-genetic-information-with-labcorp-class-action-lawsuit-claims) and [360Dx](https://www.360dx.com/business-news/labcorps-invitae-illegally-shared-genetic-data-upon-acquisition-class-action). The complaint seeks class certification on behalf of all Illinois residents whose genetic information was disclosed to Labcorp, and seeks GIPA's statutory damages — $15,000 per willful or reckless violation and $2,500 per negligent violation, or actual damages, whichever is greater — per [Law360's coverage of the complaint](https://www.law360.com/cybersecurity-privacy/articles/2489730). As of June 2026, no settlement had been reached in this matter, according to available reporting.

**Common legal theory across both suits.** In each case, plaintiffs frame the acquisition itself as the mechanism of the privacy violation: the target company allegedly transferred genetic data to the acquirer as part of the deal (or the acquirer subsequently used/licensed that data) without a fresh, transaction-specific written authorization from the data subjects, even though the data subjects' original consent to the target predated and did not contemplate the acquisition or downstream AI training use. This theory does not require proof of a data breach or unauthorized third-party intrusion — the "disclosure" at issue is the M&A transfer and subsequent commercial use itself, which is a materially different and arguably lower bar for plaintiffs than traditional data-breach litigation.

**Regulatory backdrop.** Beyond GIPA, commentary on this litigation trend notes that acquirers of healthcare and healthcare-AI targets face overlapping exposure under HIPAA (to the extent protected health information is at issue and Business Associate Agreement obligations were not properly assigned or renegotiated), Section 5 of the FTC Act (unfair or deceptive practices, where marketing promises about data confidentiality are not honored post-acquisition), and an expanding roster of state genetic-privacy statutes beyond Illinois — including in Oregon, New Hampshire, California (CMIA and GIPA), Alabama, Utah, and South Dakota — per the underlying [Proskauer/National Law Review analysis](https://natlawreview.com/article/recent-class-actions-expose-data-privacy-risk-acquisitions-healthcare-and) and a related [Mondaq analysis of genetic data and AI training litigation risk following acquisitions](https://www.mondaq.com/unitedstates/new-technology/1783368/genetic-data-and-artificial-intelligence-training-following-acquisitions-emerging-litigation-risk-and-a-rapidly-expanding-state-regulatory-landscape). *Official text for these additional state statutes was not independently re-verified for this memo; readers should confirm current statutory text directly with each state's official code before relying on specific provisions.*

## Impact Assessment [MEDIUM confidence]

**Who is affected:** Any company acquiring a target that holds genetic, biometric, or other sensitive health data — particularly healthcare AI companies seeking training data, genetic-testing labs, and digital health platforms — faces potential successor liability for the target's data-handling practices and independent liability for its own post-acquisition use of that data. This extends to private equity sponsors and strategic acquirers alike, and applies whether the deal is a going-concern acquisition (Tempus/Ambry) or a distressed/bankruptcy asset sale (Labcorp/Invitae, and by extension the TTAM/23andMe situation covered in a related report below).

**Compliance implications:** The consent obtained by a target company at the time of original data collection generally will not, on these plaintiffs' theory, extend to a new commercial use by an unrelated acquirer — particularly a use as novel as AI model training or bulk third-party licensing. Acquirers should not assume that data assets transfer "as-is" with full downstream usage rights simply because the deal documents purport to convey "all data and data-related assets."

**Enforcement and litigation outlook:** Both cases are in early stages (complaint/consolidation stage as of mid-2026) with no adjudicated liability findings yet. GIPA's per-violation statutory damages structure ($2,500–$15,000), multiplied across potentially large classes (Tempus's data library is described as covering tens of millions of records; the Invitae/Labcorp class covers "tens of thousands" of Illinois residents), creates outsized aggregate exposure even in the absence of provable actual harm — the same dynamic that has made Illinois's Biometric Information Privacy Act (BIPA) a major driver of large settlements in prior years. Expect plaintiffs' firms to bring similar suits against other 2025-2026 healthcare-AI acquisitions with large genomic or clinical datasets, and expect state AGs to watch these private suits as a template for their own enforcement (as California's AG has already done in the analogous 23andMe/Chrome Holding matter).

## Action Items

- **M&A and privacy counsel** advising on healthcare or healthcare-AI acquisitions should treat genetic and health data as requiring transaction-specific consent analysis, not blanket "data assets transfer with the deal" assumptions — confirm whether target-company consumer consents and privacy notices affirmatively authorize transfer to an acquirer and any new use (including AI training).
- **Acquirers of genetic-testing or clinical-data companies** should conduct a state-by-state genetic/health privacy statute inventory (GIPA, CMIA, and the growing list of state genetic privacy acts) before closing, and budget for remediation (re-consent campaigns, data segregation, or exclusion of high-risk datasets from AI training pipelines) as a deal cost.
- **In-house privacy and compliance teams** at companies that have already closed similar acquisitions should audit whether post-closing data use (AI training, third-party licensing) exceeds the scope of consents obtained by the target pre-acquisition, and consider proactive remediation before litigation exposure crystallizes.
- **Monitor** the docket in *Farrier et al. v. Tempus AI, Inc.*, No. 1:26-cv-04246 (N.D. Ill.), and *B.W. v. Invitae Corp.* (Cook County, Ill. Circuit Court, Chancery Division) for motions to dismiss rulings, which will test whether the "acquisition-as-disclosure" theory survives early pleading challenges — a ruling either way will significantly shape future deal risk allocation in this sector.
- **Track** state legislative activity expanding genetic-privacy statutes (recent enactments in Utah and South Dakota, per secondary reporting) as the regulatory landscape underlying this litigation trend continues to broaden.
