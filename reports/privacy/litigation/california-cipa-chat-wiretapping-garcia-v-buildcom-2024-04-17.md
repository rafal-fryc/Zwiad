# Federal Court Dismisses CIPA Wiretapping Class Action Over Website Chat Feature in Garcia v. Build.com

**Jurisdiction:** California, Federal | **Category:** Privacy | **Date:** 2024-04-17

## Executive Summary [HIGH confidence]

In *Garcia v. Build.com, Inc.*, No. 22-cv-01985-DMS-KSC (S.D. Cal. Mar. 29, 2024), Chief U.S. District Judge Dana M. Sabraw dismissed a putative class action alleging that the online home improvement retailer Build.com violated California's Invasion of Privacy Act (CIPA), [Cal. Penal Code Section 631](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?sectionNum=631.&lawCode=PEN), through its website chat feature. The court held that the plaintiff's amended complaint failed to plausibly allege the existence of a third-party eavesdropper or that any third-party vendor committed a predicate CIPA violation that Build.com aided and abetted. This decision is part of a broader judicial trend rejecting CIPA wiretapping claims targeting website chat functionalities, reinforcing the principle that a party to a conversation cannot be liable for eavesdropping on its own communications under Section 631.

## Background [HIGH confidence]

### California's Invasion of Privacy Act and Section 631

California Penal Code [Section 631](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?sectionNum=631.&lawCode=PEN) is the state's primary anti-wiretapping statute, enacted during the Cold War era. It prohibits, among other things: (1) intentionally tapping or making unauthorized connections to any telegraph or telephone wire; (2) willfully reading or attempting to read the contents of any communication while it is in transit; (3) using information obtained through unlawful interception; and (4) aiding and abetting any of these prohibited acts. Section 631(a) includes four distinct clauses, each addressing a different prohibited activity.

The statute provides a private right of action under [Section 637.2](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?sectionNum=637.2.&lawCode=PEN), allowing injured persons to recover the greater of $5,000 per violation or three times actual damages, making it attractive for class action litigation.

### The CIPA Website Chat Litigation Wave

Beginning in approximately 2022, plaintiffs' attorneys filed a [surge of putative class actions](https://callaborlaw.com/blog/tsunami-of-cipa-class-actions-storming-california-businesses) targeting businesses that use third-party chat software on their websites. The central theory in these cases is that when a website deploys a chat feature powered by a third-party vendor (such as LivePerson, Salesforce, or similar providers), that vendor functions as a "third-party eavesdropper" intercepting communications between the website visitor and the business. Under this theory, the website operator is liable for aiding and abetting the vendor's alleged wiretapping.

This litigation wave has produced [hundreds of cases across California state and federal courts](https://www.dailyjournal.com/articles/380927-the-california-invasion-of-plaintiffs-act-a-cold-war-era-law-is-heating-up-court-dockets), generating significant defense costs and settlement pressure even in cases with questionable legal merit. However, by 2023 and 2024, courts increasingly began [rejecting these claims](https://www.insideclassactions.com/2023/05/09/a-closer-look-courts-reject-california-wiretap-claims-based-on-website-chat-features/) on multiple grounds.

## Detailed Analysis [HIGH confidence]

### Procedural History

The plaintiff, Garcia, filed the original complaint in 2022, alleging two claims under CIPA: a Section 631 wiretapping claim and a Section 632.7 claim related to recording confidential communications. Garcia alleged she visited Build.com's website on her smartphone, engaged with the site's chat feature, and disclosed personally identifiable information during the chat conversation. She contended that Build.com allowed unnamed third-party service providers to embed "chat technology code" that "secretly intercept[ed] in real time, eavesdrop[ped] upon, and store[d] transcripts of consumers' chat communications" ([Covington, Inside Class Actions](https://www.insideclassactions.com/2024/04/15/federal-court-dismisses-class-action-asserting-california-wiretapping-claim-based-on-website-chat-feature/)).

In July 2023, Judge Sabraw granted Build.com's first motion to dismiss ([Document 17, S.D. Cal. 2023](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/district-courts/california/casdce/3:2022cv01985/748883/17/)). The court dismissed with prejudice the Section 631 claim to the extent it was based on Clauses 1 through 3 of Section 631(a), holding that Build.com, as a party to the chat conversation, "cannot be liable for eavesdropping on its own conversation." The court dismissed without prejudice only the narrow Clause 4 aiding-and-abetting theory, giving the plaintiff leave to amend.

### The March 2024 Dismissal

Garcia filed a First Amended Complaint that identified WhosOn and Salesforce as the specific third-party service providers allegedly eavesdropping on her chat communications. On March 29, 2024, Judge Sabraw [granted Build.com's second motion to dismiss](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/district-courts/california/casdce/3:2022cv01985/748883/37/) the amended complaint, finding two critical deficiencies:

1. **Failure to plausibly allege a third-party eavesdropper.** Despite now naming specific vendors, the amended allegations remained conclusory. The court found that the plaintiff's assertions amounted to speculative and "naked assertion[s], devoid of further factual enhancement" that could not plausibly allege that WhosOn or Salesforce actually functioned as unauthorized third-party eavesdroppers on the chat communications.

2. **Failure to plausibly allege a predicate violation.** Even if a third party were involved, the plaintiff failed to plausibly allege that such third party committed any predicate violation of Section 631(a) that Build.com could have aided and abetted. Without an underlying wiretapping violation by a third party, the aiding-and-abetting theory under Clause 4 necessarily fails.

The court dismissed the Clause 4 claim without prejudice and gave the plaintiff 14 days to file a Second Amended Complaint. When no further amended complaint was filed, the case was dismissed entirely ([National Law Review](https://natlawreview.com/article/big-privacy-news-ca-court-dismisses-cipa-claims-against-buildcom)).

### Key Legal Principles

The *Garcia v. Build.com* decision rests on well-established principles that courts have increasingly applied to the CIPA chat litigation wave:

**The Party Exception.** Under CIPA Section 631, only a third party -- someone who is not a participant in the communication -- can be liable for eavesdropping. As the [Ninth Circuit has affirmed](https://www.crowell.com/en/insights/client-alerts/ninth-circuit-affirms-that-cipa-only-applies-to-third-party-eavesdropping), "a party to a conversation cannot be liable under section 631 for 'eavesdropping' on its own conversation" because eavesdropping, by definition, requires secret listening by someone other than the parties to the communication.

**The Vendor-as-Extension Theory.** Where a third-party software provider merely facilitates a chat on behalf of the website operator, courts have held that the vendor "operate[s] like an extension of the defendant" rather than as an independent third-party eavesdropper. This characterization defeats the foundational premise of the wiretapping theory ([Gibbons Law Alert](https://www.gibbonslawalert.com/2024/09/03/chatbot-or-not-california-federal-courts-limit-cipa-applicability/)).

**Pleading Specificity.** Courts have demanded more than conclusory allegations that a third-party vendor "intercepted" communications. Plaintiffs must plausibly allege that the vendor actually accessed message content while in transit, which is a factual showing most plaintiffs have been unable to make.

## Impact Assessment [MEDIUM confidence]

### Implications for the CIPA Chat Litigation Wave

The *Garcia* dismissal reinforces a growing consensus among federal courts in California that the wiretapping theory underlying CIPA chat class actions faces substantial legal barriers. Combined with related decisions -- including the [Beyond Yoga summary judgment](https://www.insideclassactions.com/2026/02/09/california-court-dismisses-wiretapping-claims-regarding-retailers-website-chat-feature-on-summary-judgment/) (where a California state court dismissed similar claims because encrypted chat messages could not be "intercepted" in transit) -- the case law increasingly disfavors plaintiffs in this space.

However, the litigation wave has not been entirely extinguished. As one analysis notes, [plaintiffs' attorneys have pivoted](https://ktslaw.com/en/Blog/classaction/2024/2/California-Online-Tracking-Lawsuits-Shift-Away-from-Wiretap-Theory-Toward-Pen-Register-Theory) from the traditional wiretap theory under Section 631(a) toward claims under the pen register and trap-and-trace provisions of the statute, which do not require proving that message content was intercepted. This shift suggests the underlying litigation risk has not disappeared but has evolved.

### Affected Entities

- **Online retailers and e-commerce businesses** using third-party chat vendors: The decision provides a strong defense framework, but businesses should document their chat vendor relationships to support the "extension of the party" defense.
- **Third-party chat software providers** (LivePerson, Salesforce, Zendesk, and similar): These vendors benefit from the judicial reluctance to characterize them as independent eavesdroppers, though they may face direct claims in future cases.
- **Plaintiffs' class action firms**: The wiretap theory faces increasing headwinds, but the pen register pivot and evolving factual theories keep the door open for future litigation.

### Broader Context

The CIPA chat litigation wave is one front in a larger trend of privacy class actions based on website analytics and tracking technologies. Related areas include session replay litigation (addressed in [*Smidga v. Spirit Airlines*, W.D. Pa.](reports/privacy/litigation/pennsylvania-smidga-spirit-airlines-standing-2024-04-17.md)), pixel tracking claims, and cookie consent lawsuits. Courts across jurisdictions are grappling with how Cold War-era wiretapping statutes apply to modern web technologies.

## Action Items

- **Document chat vendor relationships.** Online retailers should maintain clear records showing that third-party chat vendors operate as agents or extensions of the business, not as independent interceptors of communications. Service agreements should explicitly authorize the vendor to process communications on the business's behalf.
- **Implement encryption.** Ensure chat communications are encrypted in transit. The *Beyond Yoga* decision demonstrates that encryption provides a strong factual defense against interception claims.
- **Monitor pen register theory developments.** The pivot from wiretap claims to pen register claims under Section 631 represents the next frontier. Businesses should track developments in this area, as the legal standards differ.
- **Review chat disclosure practices.** Consider adding clear disclosures to chat interfaces stating that communications are processed by a third-party vendor on the business's behalf. While not legally required for the party exception defense, such disclosures may reduce litigation risk.
- **Watch for Ninth Circuit guidance.** Appellate decisions interpreting Section 631 in the chat context will be determinative. The [Ninth Circuit's affirmation](https://www.crowell.com/en/insights/client-alerts/ninth-circuit-affirms-that-cipa-only-applies-to-third-party-eavesdropping) that CIPA only applies to third-party eavesdropping is favorable, but the court has not yet addressed the pen register theory in this context.
