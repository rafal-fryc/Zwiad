# Seventh Circuit Finds Access or Disclosure Exclusion Bars CGL Coverage for BIPA Claims (Thermoflex v. Mitsui)

**Jurisdiction:** Illinois (Seventh Circuit) | **Category:** Privacy | **Date:** May 17, 2024

## Executive Summary [MEDIUM confidence]

On May 17, 2024, the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit issued companion decisions in two related appeals — [*Thermoflex Waukegan, LLC v. Mitsui Sumitomo Insurance USA, Inc.*, No. 23-1521](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/ca7/23-1521/23-1521-2024-05-17.html) and [*Thermoflex Waukegan, LLC v. Mitsui Sumitomo Insurance USA, Inc.*, No. 23-1578](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/ca7/23-1578/23-1578-2024-05-17.html) — together producing a significant and mixed insurance coverage ruling for companies facing class actions under the Illinois Biometric Information Privacy Act (BIPA). In No. 23-1578, the court held that the standard commercial general liability (CGL) policy exclusion for "access to or disclosure of confidential or personal information" bars coverage — including the duty to defend — for BIPA claims brought under GL and excess policies issued by Citizens Insurance Company of America and Harleysville Lake States Insurance Company. In doing so the Seventh Circuit reversed the district court, which had found coverage, and adopted the minority position among courts that have considered the issue. In No. 23-1521, the court simultaneously delivered a partial win for the policyholder: it affirmed that the umbrella carrier, Mitsui Sumitomo Insurance USA, Inc., owed a duty to defend once the underlying GL policy limits were exhausted, rejecting three other exclusions — for statutory violations, data breach liability, and employment-related practices — that Mitsui had argued barred umbrella coverage. The decisions matter because the "access or disclosure" exclusion is a standard form found in most modern CGL policies, and if routinely enforced against BIPA claims, it will substantially curtail the insurance coverage available to BIPA defendants in the Seventh Circuit.

## Background [HIGH confidence]

### BIPA and Its Insurance Coverage Landscape

Illinois enacted the [Biometric Information Privacy Act (BIPA), 740 ILCS 14/](https://www.ilga.gov/Legislation/ILCS/Articles?ActID=3004&ChapterID=57&Print=True) in 2008 as the first state biometric privacy law to include a private right of action. BIPA requires private entities to obtain informed written consent before collecting biometric identifiers (fingerprints, retina or iris scans, voiceprints, handprints, and facial geometry), establish a publicly available retention and destruction schedule, and refrain from profiting from or improperly disclosing biometric data. Violations carry statutory damages of $1,000 per negligent violation and $5,000 per intentional or reckless violation, plus attorneys' fees.

BIPA class action litigation expanded rapidly following the Illinois Supreme Court's 2019 ruling in [*Rosenbach v. Six Flags Entertainment Corp.*](https://law.justia.com/cases/illinois/supreme-court/2019/123186.html), which held that a statutory violation alone — without proof of actual harm — confers standing. The wave of class action filings that followed raised the question of whether companies' existing CGL insurance policies would cover BIPA settlement costs and defense fees.

The controlling Seventh Circuit precedent prior to *Thermoflex* was the Illinois Supreme Court's 2021 ruling in [*West Bend Mutual Insurance Co. v. Krishna Schaumburg Tan, Inc.*, 2021 IL 125978](https://perkinscoie.com/insights/update/illinois-supreme-court-affirms-bipa-lawsuits-are-covered-gl-policies), which held that CGL policies' "personal and advertising injury" coverage extends to BIPA claims (on the theory that disclosing biometric data to a third-party vendor constitutes "publication" of private material) and that the policies' "Recording and Distribution of Material or Information in Violation of Statutes" exclusion (the "Statutory Violation" exclusion) did not bar coverage for BIPA because BIPA regulates data dissemination, not the method of communication, making it unlike the TCPA and CAN-SPAM statutes enumerated in that exclusion. *Krishna* established BIPA coverage as a strong policyholder right.

Following *Krishna*, several federal district courts grappled with a different exclusion increasingly inserted by insurers into CGL policies: the "Access or Disclosure of Confidential or Personal Information" exclusion. Two Illinois federal district courts found this exclusion ambiguous and resolved it in favor of coverage. The Seventh Circuit in *Thermoflex* reversed that trend.

### The Thermoflex Waukegan Facts

Thermoflex Waukegan, LLC, an automotive parts manufacturer, required its hourly employees to clock in and out using handprint scans. The handprint data was processed through a third-party vendor. A class of workers sued Thermoflex in Illinois state court alleging violations of BIPA Sections 15(a), (b), and (d): failure to maintain a publicly available retention policy, collection without informed consent, and unlawful disclosure to the vendor.

Thermoflex sought defense coverage from two sets of insurers. The first set — Citizens Insurance Company of America and Harleysville Lake States Insurance Company — provided GL and excess coverage. The second — Mitsui Sumitomo Insurance USA, Inc. — provided umbrella coverage. Each set of insurers filed a separate declaratory judgment action in the Northern District of Illinois seeking a ruling that they owed no defense or indemnification, generating two distinct district court proceedings.

The **GL and excess case** (No. 1:2020-cv-05980, later 7th Circuit No. 23-1578) was presided over by Judge John F. Kness of the Northern District of Illinois. In a March 1, 2022 opinion, [Judge Kness ruled in favor of Thermoflex](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/district-courts/illinois/ilndce/1:2020cv05980/392070/52/), holding that Citizens and Harleysville owed a duty to defend because none of the asserted exclusions — including the Access or Disclosure Exclusion — unambiguously barred coverage for BIPA claims. The Seventh Circuit reversed that ruling in No. 23-1578, holding that the Access or Disclosure Exclusion unambiguously barred GL and excess coverage.

The **Mitsui umbrella case** (later 7th Circuit No. 23-1521) was presided over by Judge Lee of the Northern District of Illinois (subsequently elevated to the Seventh Circuit). The district court held that Mitsui owed a duty to defend under its umbrella policy because none of the three asserted exclusions — Statutory Violation, Data Breach Liability, and Employment-Related Practices — applied to BIPA claims. The Seventh Circuit affirmed this ruling, confirming that the umbrella carrier owed coverage once the GL policy limits were exhausted.

## Detailed Analysis [MEDIUM confidence]

### The Access or Disclosure Exclusion

The GL and excess policies issued by Citizens and Harleysville contained an exclusion titled "Access or Disclosure of Confidential or Personal Information," which provided that the insurance did not apply to damages arising out of any access to or disclosure of "any person's or organization's confidential or personal information, including patents, trade secrets, processing methods, customer lists, financial information, credit card information, health information or **any other type of nonpublic information**." (Emphasis added to the operative catchall language.)

Thermoflex argued the exclusion was ambiguous as applied to biometric identifiers — that handprints and fingerprints are not obviously "confidential or personal information" because they are not the types of financial or records-based information listed in the exclusion's examples. The Seventh Circuit rejected that argument.

The panel held that BIPA itself resolves any purported ambiguity: BIPA expressly identifies biometric identifiers as confidential, stating that "biometric identifiers and information are unlike other unique identifiers that are used to access finances or other sensitive information" precisely because they cannot be changed if compromised — making them inherently sensitive. The court reasoned that if BIPA itself deems biometric data confidential, then the exclusion's reference to "confidential or personal information" plainly encompasses biometric identifiers such as handprints. The ordinary meaning of "confidential or personal information" also includes biometric identifiers that can be used for identity theft, and the catchall phrase "any other type of nonpublic information" sweeps in any information BIPA treats as confidential.

In reaching this conclusion the Seventh Circuit reversed Judge Kness's district court ruling (which had found the exclusion ambiguous) and [disagreed with the two district courts that had found the exclusion ambiguous](https://perkinscoie.com/insights/update/seventh-circuit-ruling-insurance-coverage-biometric-privacy-class-actions-strays). The Seventh Circuit's analysis is notable for using the underlying statute's own characterization of the data at issue to resolve an ambiguity in the insurance policy — an approach that could be replicated by courts analyzing coverage exclusions for other privacy statutes.

### Other Exclusions: Statutory Violation and Data Breach Liability

The Seventh Circuit also addressed the GL/excess policy's "Recording and Distribution of Material or Information in Violation of Statutes" (Statutory Violation) exclusion and the "Data Breach Liability" exclusion, finding neither barred coverage for BIPA claims.

On the **Statutory Violation exclusion**, the court followed its own prior holding in [*Citizens Insurance Co. of America v. Wynndalco Enterprises, LLC*, 87 F.4th 827 (7th Cir. 2023)](https://www.fmglaw.com/insurance-4/the-courts-clash-again-the-seventh-circuit-refuses-to-accede-to-illinois-appellate-courts-determination-of-insurance-coverage-for-bipa-claims/) and the Illinois Supreme Court's *Krishna* decision: the exclusion is limited to statutes that regulate the *method of communicating* (like the TCPA and CAN-SPAM), not statutes like BIPA that regulate the collection and use of information. Including BIPA in the same category as communications statutes via the catchall clause would, in the Seventh Circuit's view, render the personal and advertising injury coverage illusory. This holding actually benefited Thermoflex and policyholders generally.

On the **Data Breach Liability exclusion**, the Seventh Circuit agreed with the district court that the exclusion did not apply: BIPA claims arise from the collection and use of biometric data without consent — not from a "breach" of security systems or an unauthorized access event. The facts alleged did not involve a data breach in the traditional sense of a security incident.

### The Umbrella Policy: Mitsui Owes a Defense

The Mitsui umbrella policy did not contain an Access or Disclosure Exclusion. The policy's three asserted exclusions — Statutory Violation, Data Breach Liability, and Employment-Related Practices — were analyzed by the Seventh Circuit on the same reasoning applied to the GL policy, and none were found to bar coverage. The court affirmed the district court's holding that Mitsui owed Thermoflex a duty to defend once the limits of the underlying GL policy (plus deductibles) were exhausted. This aspect of the ruling was favorable to policyholders with umbrella coverage lacking the Access or Disclosure Exclusion.

### The Court Conflict: Federal vs. Illinois State Courts

*Thermoflex* sits within a larger and unresolved conflict between the Seventh Circuit and the Illinois Appellate Court over BIPA insurance coverage. The Illinois Appellate Court's First District held in [*National Fire Insurance Co. of Hartford v. Visual Pak Co.*, Case No. 1-22-1160 (Ill. App. Ct. Dec. 19, 2023)](https://www.hunton.com/hunton-insurance-recovery-blog/illinois-court-says-7th-circuit-fumbled-illinois-law-in-bipa-decision0) that the Statutory Violation exclusion's catchall language *does* bar coverage for BIPA, explicitly rejecting the Seventh Circuit's *Wynndalco* decision as "not an accurate reflection of Illinois law." The Illinois Supreme Court declined a petition for leave to appeal the *Visual Pak* decision, leaving the federal-state split unresolved.

This creates a venue problem for litigants: an Illinois company facing a BIPA class action may have coverage under the Seventh Circuit's reading of its GL policy (no Statutory Violation bar) but no coverage under the Illinois Appellate Court's reading. Because BIPA coverage disputes are often litigated in federal court via diversity jurisdiction, the Seventh Circuit's interpretation tends to govern — but insurer-filed declaratory judgment actions in Illinois state court could produce a different result. [Freeman Mathis & Gary analysts](https://www.fmglaw.com/insurance/clash-of-the-courts-how-the-illinois-appellate-courts-discord-with-the-seventh-circuit-continues-to-shape-the-evolving-landscape-of-insurance-coverage-for-bipa-claims/) have described this as a "clash of courts" that will continue to shape BIPA coverage litigation until the Illinois Supreme Court definitively rules on the Statutory Violation exclusion question — or on the Access or Disclosure exclusion.

### Interaction with BIPA's 2024 Damages Amendment

The *Thermoflex* ruling's practical significance must be read alongside Illinois Senate Bill 2979, signed by Governor Pritzker in August 2024 as [Public Act 103-0769](https://www.ilga.gov/Legislation/publicacts/view/103-0769), which amended BIPA's Section 20 to cap recovery at one statutory award per person (not per scan). The Seventh Circuit subsequently held in *Clay v. Union Pacific Railroad Co.* (2026) that this per-person cap applies retroactively to pending cases. The net effect of both developments on BIPA defendants is nuanced:

- **Reduced overall exposure** from the SB 2979 damages cap means individual BIPA settlements are lower, reducing (but not eliminating) the importance of insurance coverage for any single case.
- **Heightened significance of coverage denials** for defendants still facing per-class-member exposure ($5,000 × potentially thousands of class members), where the Access or Disclosure Exclusion now presents a significant barrier to GL coverage under the Seventh Circuit's reading.
- **Umbrella policies without the Access or Disclosure Exclusion** remain a viable coverage source, making policy review critical.
- **Future underwriting**: Insurers now have Seventh Circuit precedent to include — and enforce — the Access or Disclosure Exclusion in all new and renewed CGL policies to avoid BIPA obligations. [Willis Towers Watson analysts](https://www.wtwco.com/en-us/insights/2024/10/illinois-amends-bipa-a-new-limit-on-damages-for-biometric-privacy-violations) note that insurance market practices for BIPA are "far from uniform," with some markets carrying explicit BIPA exclusions and others relying on broad unlawful-collection language.

## Impact Assessment [MEDIUM confidence]

### For BIPA Defendants (Policyholders)

Companies facing BIPA class actions that carry standard CGL policies with the Access or Disclosure Exclusion can no longer rely on the prior pro-policyholder trend in federal court. The Seventh Circuit has now sanctioned insurers' use of that exclusion to deny both defense and indemnification. Key implications:

- **CGL/excess policies with the Access or Disclosure Exclusion**: Coverage is barred under the Seventh Circuit's reading. Policyholders with such policies should expect denials.
- **Umbrella policies**: Must be reviewed separately. If the umbrella lacks the Access or Disclosure Exclusion, *Thermoflex* suggests coverage remains available, at least once GL limits are exhausted.
- **State court vs. federal court forum**: Illinois Appellate Court *Visual Pak* precedent and Seventh Circuit *Thermoflex* precedent conflict on the Statutory Violation exclusion; they converge in denying coverage, though on different grounds.
- **Settlement leverage**: A BIPA defendant denied GL coverage faces the full cost of settlements from its own balance sheet, removing a key negotiating tool and potentially forcing lower settlements.

### For Insurers

The Seventh Circuit's adoption of the minority view that the Access or Disclosure Exclusion bars BIPA coverage is a major win for insurers. It:

- **Validates coverage denials** that were legally uncertain after prior district court decisions finding the exclusion ambiguous.
- **Provides a template** for denying future BIPA coverage claims using the same exclusion language.
- **Incentivizes adding the Access or Disclosure Exclusion** to all new CGL and excess policies where it is not already present.
- As noted by [Perkins Coie](https://perkinscoie.com/insights/update/seventh-circuit-ruling-insurance-coverage-biometric-privacy-class-actions-strays), it will "now require Illinois Supreme Court intervention for policyholders to obtain the BIPA coverage they bargained for."

### Broader Biometric Privacy Insurance Landscape

The *Thermoflex* ruling is specific to the Seventh Circuit and to Illinois BIPA, but its logic could migrate to other jurisdictions with biometric privacy statutes. Texas, Washington, and other states have enacted biometric data laws, and the argument that the statute's own labeling of biometric data as "confidential" settles the exclusion question could be adopted by other courts. [Hunton Andrews Kurth](https://www.hunton.com/insights/publications/the-insurance-implications-of-illinois-bipa-amendments) has tracked these developments as part of the broader question of BIPA's insurance implications post-SB 2979.

The HeplerBroom firm has noted that the ruling "furthers mixed signals on insurance coverage" and that practitioners must analyze each policy's specific exclusion language, as the precise wording of the Access or Disclosure Exclusion varies among insurers. [HeplerBroom](https://heplerbroom.com/blog/bipa-ruling-furthers-mixed-signals-on-insurance-coverage/) also observed that the decision underscores the importance of umbrella coverage as a backstop.

## Action Items

- **Audit all CGL, excess, and umbrella policies** for the presence and exact language of "Access or Disclosure of Confidential or Personal Information" exclusions before the next BIPA exposure arises. Under *Thermoflex*, policies with this exclusion will likely not provide a defense in the Seventh Circuit.
- **Review umbrella policy terms specifically**: If the umbrella policy lacks an Access or Disclosure Exclusion, it may remain a viable coverage source once GL limits are exhausted. Confirm this with coverage counsel.
- **Update litigation reserves** in any pending BIPA matter to reflect the possibility — now supported by Seventh Circuit precedent — that GL and excess coverage may be unavailable and the company must self-fund defense and settlement costs.
- **Engage coverage counsel immediately** upon receipt of a BIPA complaint to: (a) tender the defense to all applicable carriers; (b) document any denial; and (c) assess whether to file a declaratory judgment action in Illinois state court (where *Visual Pak* may produce a different result on the Statutory Violation exclusion) or federal court.
- **Negotiate favorable policy terms at renewal**: Seek endorsements or manuscript policy language that explicitly covers BIPA claims, or negotiate deletion of the Access or Disclosure Exclusion as applied to biometric data; or purchase dedicated biometric/privacy liability insurance.
- **Track the Illinois Supreme Court**: The unresolved tension between *Thermoflex* and *Visual Pak* on the Statutory Violation exclusion, and the open question of how the Illinois Supreme Court would rule on the Access or Disclosure Exclusion, means the law may shift further. Monitor any Illinois Supreme Court BIPA or biometric coverage grants of leave to appeal.
- **Advise on BIPA compliance urgency**: Companies without robust BIPA compliance programs (written policies, consent workflows, retention schedules) now have both liability exposure and constrained insurance coverage — a compounding risk that makes proactive compliance more valuable.
