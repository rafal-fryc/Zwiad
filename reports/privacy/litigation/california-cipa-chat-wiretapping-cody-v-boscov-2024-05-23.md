# Central District of California Dismisses CIPA Wiretapping and Eavesdropping Claims Against Boscov's Over Website Live Chat Feature

**Jurisdiction:** California, Federal | **Category:** Privacy | **Date:** 2024-05-23

## Executive Summary [HIGH confidence]

In *Annette Cody v. Boscov's, Inc.*, No. 8:22-cv-01434 (C.D. Cal. May 6, 2024), Judge Sunshine S. Sykes of the United States District Court for the Central District of California dismissed a putative class action alleging that Boscov's, Inc. — a regional department store chain — violated California's Invasion of Privacy Act (CIPA) through its website live chat feature. The plaintiff alleged that Boscov's aided and abetted two third-party vendors, Webex and Kustomer, that intercepted customer chat communications in violation of [CIPA Section 631](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?sectionNum=631.&lawCode=PEN) (wiretapping) and [CIPA Section 632.7](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?sectionNum=632.7.&lawCode=PEN) (eavesdropping on cellular and cordless communications). The court dismissed both claims, holding that (1) accessing a website using a smartphone's web browser does not constitute a "telephonic communication" under Section 631, (2) the alleged code did not intercept communications "while in transit" as required by the statute, and (3) the plaintiff failed to adequately plead that she used a qualifying device under Section 632.7. This decision reinforces a growing federal judicial consensus rejecting CIPA-based wiretapping class actions targeting website chat functionality.

## Background [HIGH confidence]

### California's Invasion of Privacy Act: Sections 631 and 632.7

The [California Invasion of Privacy Act](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?sectionNum=630.&lawCode=PEN), codified at Cal. Penal Code §§ 630–638.55, is the state's comprehensive wiretapping and eavesdropping statute. Originally enacted in 1967 during the Cold War era to address government surveillance, it has become a potent tool for private class action litigation in the digital age because of its statutory damages provision.

[Section 631(a)](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?sectionNum=631.&lawCode=PEN) prohibits four categories of conduct: (1) intentionally tapping telegraph or telephone wires; (2) willfully attempting to learn the contents of any communication while it is "in transit or passing over any wire, line or cable, or is being sent from, or received at any place within this state"; (3) using information obtained through such interception; and (4) aiding, abetting, agreeing with, employing, or conspiring with any person to do any of the above. The statute provides a private right of action under [Section 637.2](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?sectionNum=637.2.&lawCode=PEN), allowing recovery of the greater of $5,000 per violation or three times actual damages — making it a magnet for class action litigation.

[Section 632.7](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?sectionNum=632.7.&lawCode=PEN) prohibits intentionally intercepting or recording communications "transmitted between two cellular radio telephones, a cellular radio telephone and a landline telephone, two cordless telephones, a cordless telephone and a landline telephone, or a cordless telephone and a cellular radio telephone," without consent. The statute is limited to communications involving cellular or cordless devices — an express requirement that the *Cody* court found critical.

### The Website Chat Class Action Wave

Beginning around 2022, plaintiffs' attorneys filed a [wave of CIPA class actions](https://www.insideclassactions.com/2023/05/09/a-closer-look-courts-reject-california-wiretap-claims-based-on-website-chat-features/) targeting businesses that deploy third-party live chat software on their websites. The central theory: when a website uses third-party vendors (such as Webex, Kustomer, LivePerson, Salesforce, Zendesk, or similar services) to power a chat feature, those vendors operate as "third-party eavesdroppers" intercepting communications between website visitors and the business. Under this theory, the website operator is liable for aiding and abetting the vendors' alleged CIPA violations.

The [Daily Journal described the resulting litigation volume](https://www.dailyjournal.com/articles/380927-the-california-invasion-of-plaintiffs-act-a-cold-war-era-law-is-heating-up-court-dockets) as "heating up court dockets" across California, with hundreds of cases generating substantial defense costs and settlement pressure. However, by 2023 and 2024, courts in both the Northern and Southern Districts of California began dismissing these claims on multiple grounds. *Cody v. Boscov's* adds the Central District to this trend.

### The Retail Litigation Center's Involvement

The Retail Industry Leaders Association's [Retail Litigation Center filed an amicus brief](https://www.rila.org/retail-litigation-center/cody-v-boscovs-inc) in *Cody v. Boscov's*, arguing that CIPA's three separate sections — Section 631, Section 632, and Section 632.7 — address distinct conduct and were each enacted for specific purposes that do not encompass routine commercial use of third-party chat platforms. The court's ultimate ruling aligned with this structural reading of CIPA.

## Detailed Analysis [HIGH confidence]

### Procedural History

Plaintiff Annette Cody filed suit against Boscov's, Inc. in 2022, alleging class-wide violations of CIPA Sections 631(a) and 632.7 based on her use of Boscov's website chat feature from her smartphone. Cody alleged that Boscov's aided and abetted two third-party vendors — Webex and Kustomer — that together intercepted chat inquiries in real time. Specifically, she alleged that Boscov's chat function contained code that rerouted customer inquiries to computer servers owned and controlled by Kustomer, and that Webex also provided tools enabling Boscov's to record and maintain information about customer conversations, making both companies unauthorized eavesdroppers on her communications.

The case proceeded through the pleading stage with Boscov's moving to dismiss. An earlier ruling granted dismissal with leave to amend on some claims, and the May 6, 2024 decision addressed the amended complaint, resulting in final dismissal.

### Section 631 Holdings: Two Independent Grounds for Dismissal

Judge Sykes dismissed Cody's Section 631(a) claims on two independent legal grounds, either of which would have been sufficient.

**Ground 1: Web Browser Use Is Not a "Telephonic Communication."** Section 631(a) prohibits the interception of communications "sent from, or received at any place within this state" over a "wire, line or cable." Courts interpreting this provision have debated whether internet-based communications qualify. Judge Sykes held that [using a cell phone's web browser to access a website chat feature does not constitute a "telephonic communication"](https://natlawreview.com/article/court-rejects-claims-websites-live-chat-feature-violates-californias-prohibitions) within the meaning of Section 631. A smartphone's web browser function is fundamentally different from its phone function; the statute's wire-based communication requirement was not designed to encompass browser-based internet chat sessions.

This holding distinguishes *Cody* from cases where courts have found Section 631 potentially applicable to communications over telephone-connected channels. By anchoring the analysis to the statutory text requiring "telephonic" or wire-based communication, the court erected a high threshold that most website chat interactions will fail to meet.

**Ground 2: The "In Transit" Requirement.** Even accepting that some wire-based communication occurred, the court held that Cody failed to adequately allege that the code from Webex and Kustomer intercepted communications ["while in transit," as opposed to storing them after they were received by Boscov's](https://privacylaw.proskauer.com/2024/05/articles/california/court-rejects-claims-that-websites-live-chat-feature-violates-californias-prohibitions-on-wiretapping-and-eavesdropping/). The complaint's allegations — that both vendors' code "intercepted" chat messages — were insufficient to differentiate between contemporaneous interception during transmission (which may implicate Section 631) and routine post-receipt storage or processing (which does not). Courts have consistently held that the "in transit" element requires the plaintiff to plausibly allege acquisition during transmission, not access to data already received and stored. Cody's pleadings could not satisfy this standard.

This "in transit" analysis remains active at the appellate level, but the Ninth Circuit's 2025 signals are split rather than uniformly pro-defendant. The [Mayer Brown analysis of two back-to-back June 2025 Ninth Circuit memoranda](https://www.mayerbrown.com/en/insights/publications/2025/06/the-ninth-circuit-briefly-speaks-on-cipa-section-631) illustrates the divergence: on June 18, 2025, the court affirmed dismissal of Section 631 claims in *Thomas v. Papa John's International* on party-exception grounds (the vendor was deemed an extension of the defendant, not a third-party eavesdropper); but just two days later, in *Mikulsky v. Bloomingdale's, LLC* (June 20, 2025), the Ninth Circuit reversed dismissal and held that the plaintiff had adequately pled a Section 631 violation by specifically alleging real-time capture of the *contents* of her communications — not merely metadata or post-receipt storage. The contemporaneous-interception standard is therefore not a categorical bar: complaints that specifically and plausibly allege real-time content interception during transmission can survive dismissal, as *Mikulsky* demonstrates, while those that allege only generic "interception" without distinguishing in-transit capture from post-receipt access will fail, as *Cody* and *Thomas* show.

### Section 632.7 Holdings: Failure to Allege a Qualifying Device

Section 632.7 is expressly limited to communications involving cellular radio telephones, cordless telephones, or landline telephones in certain combinations. The court dismissed Cody's Section 632.7 claim because she failed to allege whether she used a smartphone — a qualifying device — or another type of device to access Boscov's website. Without a factual allegation that the communication was "transmitted between" one of the qualifying device combinations, the statutory predicate for Section 632.7 liability was absent.

Notably, the court [rejected Boscov's alternative argument](https://natlawreview.com/article/court-rejects-claims-websites-live-chat-feature-violates-californias-prohibitions) that Section 632.7 necessarily fails because the defendant (Boscov's as a business) was not using a qualifying telephone device. The court read the California Supreme Court's precedent as indicating that Section 632.7 prohibits the "intentional interception or recording of a communication involving a cellular phone or a cordless phone" — so only one party's device need be a qualifying instrument. But because Cody failed to allege the specific nature of her own device, the claim failed on pleading grounds.

The court granted leave to amend the Section 632.7 claim, potentially allowing Cody to replead with specific device allegations, though this path ultimately led to no further viable complaint.

### The Vendor-as-Extension Framework

The *Cody* decision also engaged with the established principle that a party to a conversation cannot be liable for eavesdropping on its own communications under Section 631 — the so-called "party exception." Courts, including in the [Ninth Circuit's *Thomas v. Papa John's International* affirmance](https://www.crowell.com/en/insights/client-alerts/ninth-circuit-affirms-that-cipa-only-applies-to-third-party-eavesdropping), have consistently held that CIPA Section 631 "only applies to third-party eavesdropping" by someone not party to the communication. Where third-party vendors merely facilitate a chat on behalf of the website operator, courts characterize those vendors as "operating like an extension of the defendant" rather than as independent eavesdroppers.

Consistent with this framework, the court found that even where the roles of both Webex and Kustomer were asserted, Cody's allegations were insufficient to establish that either vendor was an independent third-party eavesdropper rather than a functional extension of Boscov's chat infrastructure. The combination of the "in transit" deficiency and the "extension of defendant" framework closed both prongs of the Section 631 theory simultaneously.

## Impact Assessment [MEDIUM confidence]

### Significance for the CIPA Chat Litigation Wave

*Cody v. Boscov's* adds an important data point to the emerging judicial consensus across California's federal district courts that CIPA Section 631 wiretapping claims based on website chat features face substantial pleading obstacles. Key sister-district decisions reaching similar conclusions include *Garcia v. Build.com, Inc.*, No. 22-cv-01985 (S.D. Cal. Mar. 29, 2024) ([earlier report](reports/privacy/litigation/california-cipa-chat-wiretapping-garcia-v-buildcom-2024-04-17.md)), and various Northern District cases dismissing similar claims.

The *Cody* decision is particularly significant because it articulates the "telephonic communication" bar as a threshold issue — a ground that, if accepted broadly, would defeat Section 631 claims at the outset rather than requiring detailed analysis of the "in transit" or "party exception" elements. This threshold argument provides defendants with an additional, earlier basis for dismissal that prior decisions did not squarely address.

### The Pen Register Pivot and Continuing Litigation Risk

The judicial skepticism toward Section 631 wiretapping theories has [driven plaintiffs' attorneys to pivot toward alternative CIPA theories](https://www.klgates.com/Pen-Register-and-Trap-and-Trace-Claims-The-Latest-Wave-of-CIPA-Litigation-3-4-2024), particularly claims under [CIPA Section 638.51](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?sectionNum=638.51.&lawCode=PEN), which prohibits installation or use of "pen register or trap and trace devices" without a court order. Unlike Section 631, pen register claims do not require proving interception of message *content*; they target devices that record addressing, routing, or dialing information — a description that plaintiffs argue encompasses web analytics pixels, tracking cookies, and chat widget metadata.

As [Holland & Knight's February 2026 analysis](https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/02/uncertainty-continues-in-california-on-cipa-section-63851-claims) notes, there remains significant uncertainty about the Section 638.51 theory, with California state courts and federal courts reaching divergent conclusions. Federal courts have largely found that website pixels may qualify as pen registers, while California state courts have more often applied the statute narrowly. This split creates ongoing unpredictability for website operators.

### Affected Industries and Compliance Implications

The *Cody* decision most directly benefits:

- **Retail and e-commerce businesses** deploying third-party live chat or customer service platforms (Webex, Kustomer, Salesforce Service Cloud, Zendesk, LivePerson, Intercom, and similar vendors). The court's "telephonic communication" and "in transit" holdings substantially narrow the viable theory of liability.
- **Chat software vendors** (Webex, Kustomer, and their competitors): The decision's characterization of vendors as functional extensions of their business clients, rather than independent eavesdroppers, supports a structural defense available in future cases.
- **Legal defense teams**: Two independent dismissal grounds — neither dependent on the party exception — give defendants additional early dismissal arguments at the Rule 12(b)(6) stage.

The decision does not eliminate CIPA risk for website operators. The Section 632.7 claim was dismissed with leave to amend, and a properly pled amended complaint identifying a qualifying device could survive. More significantly, the pen register theory under Section 638.51 remains a live risk that the *Cody* holding does not address.

## Action Items

- **Document device-agnostic chat access.** The Section 632.7 holding turned on the plaintiff's failure to allege device type. Website operators should not assume this gap will persist in future pleadings — plaintiffs' counsel will routinely allege smartphone use going forward, making Section 632.7 claims harder to dismiss on this ground.
- **Audit chat vendor contractual relationships.** Ensure service agreements with live chat vendors (Webex, Kustomer, Salesforce, Zendesk, and similar) explicitly authorize the vendor to process communications on the business's behalf and characterize the vendor as the business's agent or service provider. This supports the "extension of defendant" defense.
- **Evaluate pen register exposure separately.** The *Cody* holding does not protect against Section 638.51 pen register claims based on web analytics pixels, tracking scripts, or chat widget metadata. Conduct a separate assessment of these technologies' compliance posture.
- **Implement chat encryption.** Encrypting chat communications in transit strengthens the "in transit" defense: encrypted data cannot be "intercepted" in any meaningful sense while it is secure. The *Beyond Yoga* summary judgment decision (C.D. Cal.) demonstrates the practical value of this argument.
- **Monitor Ninth Circuit developments.** The Ninth Circuit's 2025 memoranda in *Thomas v. Papa John's* and *Mikulsky v. Bloomingdale's* send split signals: the in-transit standard is not a categorical bar, and complaints that specifically allege real-time content interception can survive. Future published opinions will control across all California federal district courts and may resolve the current uncertainty.
- **Consider adding chat consent disclosures.** Proactive disclosure that chat communications are processed by named third-party vendors on the business's behalf, combined with a consent mechanism, provides a complete statutory defense under CIPA's all-party consent framework and may reduce litigation risk regardless of which theory plaintiffs pursue.
