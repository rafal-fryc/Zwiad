# DC Circuit Confirms Human Authorship Requirement, Foreclosing Copyright for AI-Only Generated Works

**Jurisdiction:** Federal (D.C. Circuit) | **Category:** Privacy / AI Law / Copyright | **Date:** March 18, 2025

## Executive Summary [HIGH confidence]

On March 18, 2025, the [United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit](https://media.cadc.uscourts.gov/opinions/docs/2025/03/23-5233.pdf) decided *Thaler v. Perlmutter*, No. 23-5233, unanimously affirming that human authorship is a statutory requirement under the Copyright Act of 1976 and that works generated entirely by an artificial intelligence system — without meaningful human creative input — are not eligible for copyright registration. The ruling, which affirmed both the U.S. Copyright Office's administrative denial and the 2023 district court decision, marks the first appellate-level resolution of whether a non-human AI system can be recognized as an "author" for copyright purposes. The Supreme Court denied certiorari on March 2, 2026, leaving the DC Circuit's holding as the settled law on the question in the United States.

The ruling has immediate practical significance: content generated autonomously by AI tools, without sufficient human creative expression, cannot receive copyright protection under current US law. Organizations that rely on AI-generated content without meaningful human selection, arrangement, or modification face a protection gap. Critically, the court left intact copyright eligibility for AI-assisted works where a human author makes genuinely creative choices.

## Background [HIGH confidence]

### The Copyright Act's Authorship Framework

The [Copyright Act of 1976](https://www.copyright.gov/title17/) does not explicitly define "author." Section 102 grants copyright protection to "original works of authorship," and the statute's duration, assignment, and work-for-hire provisions all use terminology tied to human characteristics — lifespan, heirs, surviving spouses, domicile, and national identity. For more than a century prior to the 1976 Act, the Copyright Office had interpreted "author" to mean a human being, and Congress adopted that same language when it enacted the 1976 statute.

The Copyright Office [reaffirmed its human-authorship policy in March 2023](https://www.copyright.gov/ai/ai_policy_guidance.pdf), issuing formal guidance that works "produced by a machine" without any creative contribution from a human author are not registrable. That policy reflected the Office's longstanding practice and laid the administrative groundwork for the Thaler litigation.

### The Thaler Litigation

Dr. Stephen Thaler is a computer scientist who developed an AI system he named the "Creativity Machine." In 2018-2019, Thaler applied to the Copyright Office for registration of a visual artwork titled "A Recent Entrance to Paradise," which his AI had generated autonomously — with no human creative involvement in selecting, arranging, or modifying the output. Thaler listed the Creativity Machine as the sole author and himself as copyright owner/claimant.

The Copyright Office denied registration on the grounds that the work lacked human authorship. Thaler requested reconsideration twice, and the Office denied both requests. Thaler then sought judicial review in the [U.S. District Court for the District of Columbia](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/cadc/23-5233/23-5233-2025-03-18.html), which in August 2023 granted summary judgment for the Copyright Office and affirmed the denial (*Thaler v. Perlmutter*, 687 F. Supp. 3d 140 (D.D.C. 2023)). Thaler appealed to the D.C. Circuit.

### Copyright Office AI Study

Separately, the Copyright Office published [Part 2 of its AI and Copyright study](https://www.copyright.gov/ai/Copyright-and-Artificial-Intelligence-Part-2-Copyrightability-Report.pdf) on January 29, 2025 — approximately six weeks before the D.C. Circuit issued its opinion. That report confirmed that AI-generated outputs are copyrightable only where a human author has exercised sufficient creative control over the expressive elements, and that mere provision of text prompts to an AI system generally does not rise to the level of authorship.

## Detailed Analysis [HIGH confidence]

### The D.C. Circuit's Statutory Analysis

The court declined to resolve the question by reference to general dictionary definitions of "author" alone. Instead, applying standard tools of statutory interpretation, the court surveyed the full text of the Copyright Act and concluded that its various provisions make sense only if "author" refers to a human being. The opinion identified several textual indicators:

- **Duration provisions** (17 U.S.C. § 302): Term measured by the life of the author, plus 70 years. Machines do not have a lifespan in the relevant sense.
- **Termination rights** (17 U.S.C. § 203): The right to terminate a grant of copyright may be exercised by an author's "widow or widower," "children," or "grandchildren." These categories are inapplicable to machines.
- **Heirship and nationality**: The statute makes reference to the domicile, nationality, and surviving heirs of an author — concepts with no meaningful application to AI systems.
- **Signature requirements**: Certain formalities under the Act require an author's signature — something a machine cannot provide.

The court also relied on the Copyright Office's longstanding pre-1976 administrative interpretation that "author" means a human, finding that Congress had adopted this understanding when it enacted the 1976 statute. This application of the principle from *Chevron* (as modified by more recent precedent) and the longstanding-agency-interpretation doctrine fortified the textual reading.

### Rejection of Work-for-Hire Theory

Thaler argued in the alternative that even if AI cannot be an author, he should be recognized as the author because the Creativity Machine was his employee or contractor, making the work a "work made for hire" under [17 U.S.C. § 101](https://www.copyright.gov/title17/). The court rejected this argument. The work-for-hire doctrine, the court held, presupposes that the work was "created in the first instance by a human being." Because the image was created entirely by the AI with no human authorial input, there was no copyrightable work in existence for the work-for-hire doctrine to transfer ownership of. The doctrine could not manufacture authorship where none existed.

### Incentive Framework

Addressing a key policy argument, the court noted that the human authorship requirement does not undermine copyright's incentive rationale. The purpose of copyright is to incentivize human creativity by granting authors exclusive rights in their works. AI systems do not respond to economic incentives; they cannot be motivated by the prospect of copyright protection to produce more or better creative works. By contrast, the requirement still incentivizes humans — like Dr. Thaler — to create using AI as a tool, because those human-authored works retain copyright protection. The incentive structure of the statute therefore remains coherent without extending authorship to AI systems.

### The AI-Assisted Works Carve-Out

Critically, the court was explicit that its holding does not bar copyright protection for all works that involve AI. The human authorship requirement "does not prohibit copyright protection of works where the human author uses or is assisted by artificial intelligence." The open question — how much human creative involvement is required, and what forms of AI assistance remain compatible with human authorship — was left for future cases and the Copyright Office to address through its registration practices.

This distinction is practically significant. Works in which a human author makes meaningful expressive choices (selecting, arranging, modifying AI-generated content; integrating AI outputs into a broader human-authored work; or directing AI tools to achieve specific creative effects) may remain protectable. The court declined to draw a bright line on that spectrum.

### Supreme Court Denial of Certiorari

Thaler petitioned the Supreme Court for review in October 2025. The [U.S. government filed a brief urging denial](https://patentlyo.com/patent/2026/01/government-copyright-emphasizing.html), arguing that the question was narrow, the statute's text was clear, and the D.C. Circuit had correctly applied standard interpretive tools. On [March 2, 2026, the Supreme Court denied certiorari](https://www.mayerbrown.com/en/insights/publications/2026/03/supreme-court-denies-review-in-ai-authorship-case), leaving the D.C. Circuit's ruling as the definitive US appellate holding on AI authorship under the Copyright Act.

## Impact Assessment [HIGH confidence]

### Who Is Affected

**Technology and AI companies** that generate or provide AI-generated content face direct exposure. If their products produce content entirely through autonomous AI generation, neither the AI provider nor the end user may be able to claim copyright in the output. Content generated by large language models, image generators, or music synthesis tools — without meaningful human editorial control — is presumptively unprotected.

**Creative industries** including advertising, media production, publishing, and design are affected when they use AI to produce final content rather than to assist human creative processes. Marketing materials, product images, or editorial content generated autonomously by AI tools cannot be registered or enforced as copyrighted works.

**AI-output platforms** that have represented to customers that AI-generated content is copyrightable may face contract disputes or liability if users discover that protection is unavailable.

### Compliance and Strategic Implications

Organizations that generate AI content should:

1. **Distinguish AI-assisted from AI-only workflows.** Content where a human author exercises genuine creative choices — selecting, editing, arranging, or meaningfully directing AI outputs — retains copyright eligibility. Pure AI generation (where no human made creative choices) does not.

2. **Document human creative contribution.** Where copyright protection is desired, companies should maintain records of the human creative choices made during production: prompts plus substantive human modification; human curation of AI candidate outputs; integration of AI into larger human-authored works.

3. **Reassess licensing and ownership representations.** Terms of service, licensing agreements, and IP warranties that assume copyright subsists in AI-generated content should be reviewed and updated.

4. **Monitor Copyright Office guidance on "sufficient" human authorship.** The court left open the quantum of human creative involvement required for AI-assisted works. The Copyright Office's Part 2 AI Report (January 2025) provides current agency guidance, but this will evolve as registration practice develops.

### Enforcement Outlook and Legislative Horizon

The Supreme Court's denial of certiorari closes the judicial avenue for change without a legislative response. Congress has received [CRS analysis on generative AI and copyright](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/LSB10922) and the issue has drawn significant legislative attention, but no statute modifying the human-authorship requirement has advanced as of this writing. Any change to the rule now requires Congressional action.

The [Copyright Office's broader AI study](https://www.copyright.gov/ai/) — including planned parts on AI training data (Part 3) — continues and may generate further regulatory guidance. Developments there should be monitored closely.

## Action Items

- Review all AI-generated content workflows to identify where the organization can demonstrate meaningful human authorship (selection, arrangement, modification, direction) versus purely autonomous AI output.
- Update IP ownership representations in vendor agreements, terms of service, and client deliverables to accurately reflect copyright eligibility of AI content.
- Consult with copyright counsel before marketing or licensing AI-generated content as a proprietary asset.
- Implement documentation practices that capture human creative decisions at each stage of AI-assisted content production to support future registration or enforcement.
- Monitor Copyright Office AI study publications (Part 3 on training data; further registration policy guidance) for updates to the "sufficient human authorship" standard.
- Track any Congressional activity on AI and copyright — the human-authorship rule as settled by the D.C. Circuit can only change through legislation.
