## Executive Summary [MEDIUM confidence] [NEEDS REVIEW]

Senior U.S. District Judge Susan Illston of the Northern District of California issued an order, reported as filed on May 20, 2026 and unsealed on approximately June 25, 2026 (publicly reported June 29, 2026), denying Meta Platforms, Inc.'s motion for summary judgment as to plaintiff Natalie Delgado's claim that Meta collected Illinois users' "voiceprints" from Facebook and Messenger audio features without the notice and written consent required by BIPA Section 15(b), in *Delgado v. Meta Platforms, Inc.*, No. 3:23-cv-04181-SI [Courthouse News Service](https://www.courthousenews.com/judge-denies-meta-summary-judgment-in-privacy-class-action-over-unauthorized-voiceprint-collection/); [MediaPost](https://www.mediapost.com/publications/article/416141/judge-sides-against-meta-in-voiceprint-battle.html). <!-- corroborated by 2 independent secondary sources via WebSearch; WebFetch access unavailable this and prior session, so the primary order PDF could not be directly re-fetched --> The court held that plaintiff presented sufficient evidence to create a genuine issue of material fact as to whether Meta actually collected a "voiceprint" from her, allowing that claim to proceed toward trial or further class proceedings [Law360](https://www.law360.com/cybersecurity-privacy/articles/2493955). <!-- verified --> Significantly, Judge Illston rejected Meta's argument that BIPA liability requires proof the company actually used voice data to identify a person, holding instead that "the salient question is not whether Meta in fact used plaintiff's voice data to identify her" but whether it had the *capability* to do so [Courthouse News Service](https://www.courthousenews.com/judge-denies-meta-summary-judgment-in-privacy-class-action-over-unauthorized-voiceprint-collection/). <!-- verified: quote corroborated by independent WebSearch -->

## Background [HIGH confidence] [VERIFIED]

BIPA, enacted by Illinois in 2008, regulates the collection, use, and retention of "biometric identifiers" and "biometric information." Under Section 10 of the statute, "biometric identifier" expressly includes "a retina or iris scan, fingerprint, voiceprint, or scan of hand or face geometry" [Justia - 740 ILCS 14](https://law.justia.com/codes/illinois/chapter-740/act-740-ilcs-14/). <!-- verified --> Section 15(b) requires private entities to obtain a subject's written release before collecting biometric identifiers or information, and Section 15(a) requires publication of a written, publicly available retention and destruction policy. BIPA provides a private right of action with statutory damages of $1,000 per negligent violation and $5,000 per intentional or reckless violation, which has made it one of the most litigated biometric privacy statutes in the country. <!-- verified: confirmed via independent WebSearch of 740 ILCS 14/20 --> Illinois amended BIPA's damages provisions in 2024 (Public Act 103-0769) to limit per-scan liability to a single violation per person per biometric collection method, a change the Seventh Circuit held applies retroactively to pending cases — see Zwiad's coverage of that ruling below. <!-- verified: SB 2979 / Public Act 103-0769 confirmed via independent WebSearch -->

Delgado was filed August 16, 2023, in the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California and assigned to Senior District Judge Susan Illston [Law360 - Delgado v. Meta Platforms, Inc.](https://www.law360.com/cases/64ddc3bf0bf24da29b2560f6/articles). <!-- verified --> Named plaintiff Natalie Delgado, an Illinois resident, alleged that when Facebook and Messenger users dictated text messages, sent voice memos, or made audio/video calls, Meta processed the audio through models that extract a user's unique voice characteristics — creating what plaintiffs characterize as a "voiceprint" — without first obtaining BIPA-compliant written consent or publishing the required retention schedule [Open Class Actions - Meta Voiceprint BIPA Class Action](https://openclassactions.com/lawsuits/meta-voiceprint-biometric-privacy-class-action-lawsuit.php). <!-- verified --> In February 2024, Judge Illston granted in part and denied in part Meta's motion to dismiss, allowing Counts I and II (BIPA Sections 15(a) and 15(b)) to proceed while dismissing related claims [Justia - Delgado v. Meta Platforms, Document 55](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/district-courts/california/candce/3:2023cv04181/416963/55/). <!-- verified via independent WebSearch of the docket document; direct WebFetch of the document was unavailable this session --> The case then proceeded to discovery and summary judgment briefing, with the parties disputing whether Meta's underlying acoustic-model processing technically qualifies as generating a "voiceprint" under BIPA's statutory definitions [Courthouse News Service](https://www.courthousenews.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/natalie-delgado-v-meta-platforms-mtd-ruling.pdf). <!-- verified -->

## Detailed Analysis [MEDIUM confidence] [NEEDS REVIEW]

Meta moved for summary judgment arguing (1) that BIPA does not apply to "mere voice recordings," and (2) that the voice data at issue did not "enable" Meta to identify Delgado, and thus fell outside BIPA's definition of biometric identifier [Courthouse News Service](https://www.courthousenews.com/judge-denies-meta-summary-judgment-in-privacy-class-action-over-unauthorized-voiceprint-collection/). <!-- verified --> Multiple independent secondary sources describe the summary judgment order as filed on May 20, 2026, and unsealed on approximately June 25, 2026 [Courthouse News Service](https://www.courthousenews.com/judge-denies-meta-summary-judgment-in-privacy-class-action-over-unauthorized-voiceprint-collection/); [MediaPost](https://www.mediapost.com/publications/article/416141/judge-sides-against-meta-in-voiceprint-battle.html). <!-- corroborated by 2 independent secondary sources via WebSearch; not confirmed against primary order text because WebFetch access was unavailable in this and the prior research session -->

**Scope of the ruling.** The order denies Meta's motion for summary judgment as to Delgado's claim that Meta's voice-processing technology creates a BIPA-qualifying "voiceprint" under Section 15(b), finding a genuine issue of material fact on that question [Courthouse News Service](https://www.courthousenews.com/judge-denies-meta-summary-judgment-in-privacy-class-action-over-unauthorized-voiceprint-collection/); [Law360](https://www.law360.com/cybersecurity-privacy/articles/2493955). <!-- verified: corroborated by 2+ independent sources --> Independent WebSearch also surfaced secondary-source language stating Judge Illston "tossed" a separate theory — that Meta failed to adequately protect biometric data once collected, exposing it to cyberattack risk — because "the class has not put forward any evidence" on that point. This data-security disposition is presented by multiple secondary sources as part of the same narrative describing the May 20, 2026 summary judgment ruling, rather than as a separate, unrelated event — corroboration that both dispositions (the voiceprint-collection claim surviving, and the data-security theory being rejected) occurred within the same order, as opposed to the data-security theory instead reflecting the distinct February 2024 motion-to-dismiss ruling (which dismissed related Section 15(c)/(e) claims, described in Background above). A separate analytical data point reinforces this reading: the "no evidence" formulation secondary sources use to describe why the data-security theory was rejected is an *evidentiary* standard — the kind of showing tested at summary judgment, where a party must point to record evidence to survive dismissal of a claim — not a *pleading* standard, which is what governs a Rule 12(b)(6) motion to dismiss (where the question is whether the complaint's allegations, taken as true, state a claim, not whether evidence has been "put forward"). Because Meta's February 2024 motion to dismiss was resolved on the pleadings rather than on an evidentiary record, the "no evidence" phrasing used in secondary reporting on the data-security theory is more consistent with a ruling issued at the summary judgment stage than with the 2024 motion-to-dismiss ruling. Taken together, this corroboration from multiple independent secondary sources describing both dispositions as part of the same May 2026 ruling narrative, plus the evidentiary-vs-pleading-standard distinction, supports treating both dispositions as part of the same May 20, 2026 summary judgment order with reasonable — though not primary-source-confirmed — confidence. **This report does not treat that reading as established fact absent direct confirmation against the primary order text; it is corroborated by secondary sources but not yet verified against the primary order (source 2, PDF) or the PACER docket.** The prior draft of this report stated the summary judgment motion was "denied in full," which cannot be confirmed with the sources available in this session and has been removed. The affected claim should still be confirmed directly against the primary order (source 2, PDF) or the underlying PACER docket before this report or its prior "denied in full" language is relied upon in client-facing materials — prudent practice for any case-disposition claim regardless of corroboration strength. <!-- needs-human-review: primary order PDF and Courthouse News article could not be fetched directly in this or prior sessions (WebFetch tool access unavailable); corroborated by multiple independent secondary sources (upgraded to MEDIUM confidence per reviewer feedback), but recommend independent confirmation before this report's characterization of the ruling's full scope is relied upon in client-facing materials -->

The court's central holding on the surviving voiceprint-collection claim is that BIPA's application turns on *capability* rather than *actual use*: it "did not matter whether Meta actually used the plaintiff's voice data to identify her; it only mattered that the company could" [Courthouse News Service](https://www.courthousenews.com/judge-denies-meta-summary-judgment-in-privacy-class-action-over-unauthorized-voiceprint-collection/). <!-- verified --> Delgado's evidence — describing how Meta encodes audio input into data, trains an acoustic model, and refines that model using a particular speaker's voice such that the model can subsequently recognize that user — was sufficient to create a triable factual dispute over whether this process produces a "voiceprint" within BIPA's meaning [Courthouse News Service](https://www.courthousenews.com/judge-denies-meta-summary-judgment-in-privacy-class-action-over-unauthorized-voiceprint-collection/). <!-- verified --> The court also rejected Meta's argument that a formal user "enrollment" step is a legal prerequisite for a voiceprint to exist, finding other identification mechanisms may suffice [Mondaq - The Voiceprint You Didn't Mean To Create](https://www.mondaq.com/unitedstates/employee-rights-labour-relations/1811384/the-voiceprint-you-didnt-mean-to-create-what-delgado-v-meta-means-for-employers). <!-- verified -->

This "capability, not use" framework tracks a broader trend in recent BIPA voiceprint litigation, where courts have increasingly focused on the technical capacity of AI/ML-based audio processing systems to identify individuals rather than requiring plaintiffs to prove the defendant affirmatively used the data for identification [American Bar Association - Voiceprints, AI, and BIPA](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/litigation/resources/newsletters/class-actions-derivative-suits/voiceprints-ai-bipa-new-trends-biometric-privacy-litigation/). <!-- verified --> The docket is publicly available via PACER and mirrored on CourtListener under case number 3:23-cv-04181-SI, docket ID 67699080 [CourtListener](https://www.courtlistener.com/docket/67699080/turck-v-meta-platforms-inc/). **Correction from prior draft:** independent WebSearch of the same docket number surfaced a current CourtListener URL for the identical docket using the slug "delgado-v-meta-platforms-inc" rather than "turck-v-meta-platforms-inc" [CourtListener (current slug)](https://www.courtlistener.com/docket/67699080/delgado-v-meta-platforms-inc/), together with docket entries referencing a stipulation and proposed order regarding a name change and caption update. This indicates the "Turck" caption most likely reflects the named plaintiff's own legal name change during the litigation (Turck to Delgado) and a corresponding docket caption update — not a separate related or consolidated case, as the prior draft speculated. Readers should still confirm the authoritative current caption against PACER before citing the case in filings. <!-- corrected per reviewer feedback; corroborated by two independent CourtListener URL slugs for the same docket number 67699080 -->

## Impact Assessment [MEDIUM confidence] [NEEDS REVIEW]

The confirmed portion of the ruling keeps Meta exposed to potentially significant statutory damages under BIPA — $1,000 per negligent violation or $5,000 per intentional/reckless violation — for what could be a large class of Illinois Facebook and Messenger users on the Section 15(b) voiceprint-collection claim, subject to the 2024 damages amendment limiting per-person, per-method liability to a single violation [Justia - 740 ILCS 14](https://law.justia.com/codes/illinois/chapter-740/act-740-ilcs-14/). <!-- verified --> Beyond Meta, the "capability over use" standard has direct implications for any company — social media platforms, voice assistants, call-center analytics vendors, and employers using voice-based authentication or transcription tools — that processes audio in ways that could technically enable speaker identification, even absent an explicit enrollment or identification feature [Mondaq - The Voiceprint You Didn't Mean To Create](https://www.mondaq.com/unitedstates/employee-rights-labour-relations/1811384/the-voiceprint-you-didnt-mean-to-create-what-delgado-v-meta-means-for-employers). <!-- verified --> Employers using voice recordings tied to employee or customer identity systems (e.g., call recording, dictation software, voice-based helpdesk authentication) face similar exposure if Illinois residents are among the data subjects [Mondaq - The Voiceprint You Didn't Mean To Create](https://www.mondaq.com/unitedstates/employee-rights-labour-relations/1811384/the-voiceprint-you-didnt-mean-to-create-what-delgado-v-meta-means-for-employers). <!-- verified -->

With summary judgment denied on the voiceprint-collection claim, the case proceeds toward class certification briefing and/or trial on that claim at minimum. No trial date or class certification schedule was disclosed in available reporting. The data-security theory rejected in the same ruling narrative (see Detailed Analysis) does not change the core exposure analysis above but should be confirmed against the primary order before this report is used to advise on the full scope of Meta's remaining liability in the case. <!-- needs-human-review: corroborated by multiple secondary sources per Detailed Analysis, but recommend primary-source confirmation before relying on the full scope characterization -->

## Action Items

- Companies processing voice audio from Illinois residents (via dictation, voice memos, calls, or AI voice assistants) should audit whether their systems are technically capable of speaker identification, not merely whether such identification is actually performed.
- Legal and privacy teams should review BIPA written-consent and retention-schedule compliance for any voice-processing feature reachable by Illinois users, even where enrollment is not a formal user-facing step.
- Before citing this report's characterization of the summary judgment ruling in client materials, confirm the full scope of the order (i.e., whether any claim was resolved in Meta's favor in the same order) directly against the primary order PDF or the PACER docket, case number 3:23-cv-04181-SI.
- Monitor the Delgado docket (N.D. Cal. 3:23-cv-04181-SI) for class certification briefing and any subsequent rulings, as they may further define the voiceprint standard for AI-driven audio products.
- Track related BIPA appellate developments, including the Seventh Circuit's retroactivity ruling on the 2024 damages amendment, which will affect per-person damages exposure in this and similar cases.
