# California Court Allows Punitive Damages and Attorney Fees in CIPA Pen Register Case, Then Partially Reverses

**Jurisdiction:** California | **Category:** Privacy | **Date:** 2024-07-24

## Executive Summary [MEDIUM confidence]

In *Monica Sanchez v. Unite Eurotherapy, Inc.*, Case No. 24STCV02282 (Los Angeles Superior Court), Judge Rolf Treu issued a tentative ruling in July 2024 denying a motion to strike the plaintiff's claims for punitive damages and attorney fees under the California Invasion of Privacy Act (CIPA). The court held that the complaint's allegations that the defendant secretly deployed spyware on website visitors' devices were sufficient, at the pleading stage, to sustain requests for both punitive damages under Civil Code Section 3294 and attorney fees under Code of Civil Procedure Section 1021.5. The judge subsequently reversed the tentative ruling on punitive damages — finding the complaint's allegations insufficiently specific — but maintained the denial on attorney fees, allowing the fee claim to proceed. The ruling adds to a growing, contested body of California case law on CIPA's pen register provisions and signals that even where courts are skeptical of punitive damages in CIPA cases, attorney fees under the private attorney general doctrine remain a viable recovery theory, materially increasing defendants' litigation exposure.

## Background [MEDIUM confidence]

### California's Invasion of Privacy Act and the Pen Register Wave

California enacted the Invasion of Privacy Act (CIPA) in 1967 as an anti-wiretapping statute. [Cal. Penal Code § 638.51](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=PEN&sectionNum=638.51.) — added by AB 929 in 2015 — makes it unlawful to install or use a "pen register" or "trap and trace device" without prior court order or consent. A "pen register" is defined under [§ 638.50(b)](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=PEN&sectionNum=638.50.) as a device or process that records dialing, routing, addressing, or signaling information, but not the contents of a communication.

Beginning in late 2022 and accelerating through 2023–2024, plaintiff's firms began adapting the pen register provisions to commercial websites, arguing that third-party advertising pixels, analytics tags, and session-replay trackers qualify as "processes" that capture "routing" or "addressing" information — specifically, user IP addresses and browsing behavior. [K&L Gates](https://www.klgates.com/Pen-Register-and-Trap-and-Trace-Claims-The-Latest-Wave-of-CIPA-Litigation-3-4-2024) documented the scale of this litigation wave in a March 2024 analysis, noting that a single prominent plaintiff's firm had filed over 120 such lawsuits and that hundreds of class actions were being pursued against California-facing businesses across the industry.

CIPA Section 638.51 provides a private right of action with statutory damages of $5,000 per violation, or three times the plaintiff's actual damages, whichever is greater. Defendants in the wave of litigation argued that the statutory penalty constitutes the plaintiff's exclusive remedy, foreclosing any additional award of punitive damages under Civil Code Section 3294 or fee-shifting under the private attorney general statute.

### The Plaintiff and the Theory

On January 30, 2024, Monica Sanchez — described in litigation documents as a "consumer privacy advocate" who works as a professional "tester" to identify companies that violate California privacy law — filed a complaint against Unite Eurotherapy, Inc., a California professional hair care company. Sanchez alleged that the defendant's publicly accessible website "secretly deployed spyware" on the devices of consumers who visited the site, constituting unlawful installation of a pen register under [CIPA § 638.51](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=PEN&sectionNum=638.51.).

The complaint included requests for: (1) statutory damages under CIPA; (2) punitive damages, predicated on allegations that the defendant's conduct was malicious, oppressive, or taken in willful and conscious disregard of plaintiffs' rights (satisfying Civil Code § 3294 standards); and (3) attorney fees under the private attorney general statute, Code of Civil Procedure § 1021.5.

## Detailed Analysis [MEDIUM confidence]

### The Motion to Strike

On March 7, 2024, Defendant Unite Eurotherapy filed a motion to strike, challenging two paragraphs of the complaint: (1) paragraph 30, which alleged "oppression" and "malice" as predicates for punitive damages; and (2) paragraph 31, which asserted entitlement to punitive damages and attorney fees. The defendant advanced two core arguments:

**First**, that the complaint's allegations supporting punitive damages were impermissibly conclusory. Under California law, punitive damage claims must be pleaded with specificity; general assertions of bad intent unsupported by factual detail are subject to being stricken. The defendant contended the complaint's language amounted to nothing more than labels and legal conclusions.

**Second**, that the $5,000-per-violation statutory remedy under [CIPA § 638.51](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=PEN&sectionNum=638.51.) constitutes the plaintiff's exclusive remedy, precluding any separate claim for punitive damages. This argument, if accepted, would significantly reduce defendants' exposure in CIPA pen register cases and undermine the economics of bringing individual (as opposed to class) actions.

### The Tentative Ruling — Denial on Both Grounds

Judge Treu's tentative ruling denied the motion to strike in its entirety. As summarized by [Thompson Hine LLP](https://www.thompsonhine.com/insights/new-ruling-on-punitive-damages-and-attorney-fees-in-california-invasion-of-privacy-act-cipa-case/), the court found on punitive damages that:

- Under binding California precedent, a plaintiff may plead both statutory penalties and punitive damages at the pleading stage without being required to elect between remedies.
- The complaint "states sufficient facts" to support the punitive damages request, as it alleged the defendant secretly accessed the plaintiff's device and installed tracking software — conduct the court characterized as potentially "vile or contemptible" and undertaken "with willful and conscious disregard to the privacy rights of their website visitors."

On attorney fees, the court denied the motion to strike because Code of Civil Procedure § 1021.5 authorizes fee awards in proceedings that enforce "an important right affecting the public interest." The court found that plaintiff's asserted rights under CIPA satisfy this threshold, noting that a significant benefit had potentially been conferred on the general public (or a large class of persons) and that the necessity and financial burden of private enforcement justified the award if the plaintiff ultimately prevailed.

### The Reversal on Punitive Damages

As subsequently reported by [Thompson Hine LLP](https://www.thompsonhine.com/insights/judge-reverses-ruling-on-punitive-damages-in-california-invasion-of-privacy-act-cipa-case/), Judge Treu reversed his tentative ruling on punitive damages — a procedurally unusual step in California Superior Court motion practice. The court granted the motion to strike the punitive damages claim, concluding that the complaint's factual allegations were ultimately insufficient to satisfy the specificity standard. The court gave the plaintiff 20 days leave to amend to replead the punitive damages allegations with greater specificity.

Critically, Judge Treu **did not** reverse on the attorney fees issue. The denial of the motion to strike the attorney fees claim was maintained, allowing the Section 1021.5 claim to proceed.

### Conflict in the Case Law on Exclusive Remedy

The *Sanchez* ruling joins a divided body of California authority on whether CIPA's statutory damages are the exclusive remedy. As [Thompson Hine LLP](https://www.thompsonhine.com/insights/cipa-litigation-update-from-punitive-damages-to-failing-to-state-a-claim/) documented in a subsequent litigation update, California courts have split on this question:

- In *Rodriguez v. Indium Software, Inc.*, a California Superior Court judge denied a motion to strike punitive damages in a CIPA case, finding the statutory scheme did not foreclose supplemental damages.
- In *Levings v. Choice Hotels*, however, a court reached the opposite conclusion, granting a motion to strike and holding that the plaintiff was limited to CIPA's statutory damages.

This split means the exclusive-remedy question remains live and unsettled across California state courts. Federal district courts interpreting CIPA have similarly trended toward dismissal of pen register claims on various grounds — including failure to allege that the technology constitutes a "process" within the statutory definition and challenges to plaintiff standing — while California state courts have been more receptive to plaintiffs' theories.

### The "Tester" Plaintiff Concern

The *Sanchez* complaint's characterization of the plaintiff as a professional "tester" — someone whose business it is to visit websites for the purpose of identifying CIPA violations — raises a separate but related question about standing and the legitimacy of the litigation model. Courts have increasingly scrutinized whether tester plaintiffs suffer a sufficiently concrete injury to maintain claims under CIPA § 637.2's statutory standing requirement. In *Rodriguez v. Fountain9, Inc.*, 2024 WL 3886811 (Cal. Super. July 9, 2024), a Los Angeles Superior Court sustained the defendant's demurrer because the plaintiff failed to allege a concrete injury-in-fact — a bare allegation of IP address collection was held to be "abstract and hypothetical" and insufficient to confer statutory standing under CIPA § 637.2. The injury-in-fact question was not squarely resolved in *Sanchez* at the motion-to-strike stage, but it remains a potential grounds for later dismissal.

## Impact Assessment [MEDIUM confidence]

### Heightened Settlement Pressure

The survival of the attorney fees claim under CCP § 1021.5 is the most practically significant aspect of the *Sanchez* rulings. Even where a defendant successfully strikes punitive damages allegations, the prospect of a fee-shifting award under the private attorney general doctrine substantially increases litigation costs and settlement leverage for plaintiffs. Because CIPA pen register claims generate per-violation statutory damages of $5,000 — which in a class context can aggregate to enormous figures — layering attorney fees eligibility on top of that baseline compounds exposure significantly.

### Implications for Business Defendants

Any California-facing business that operates a website using third-party analytics, advertising pixels, session-replay tools, or other JavaScript-based tracking technologies is a potential target of CIPA pen register claims. The *Sanchez* ruling reinforces that:

1. Defendants cannot rely solely on the "exclusive remedy" argument to eliminate punitive damage risk at the pleading stage in all California courts.
2. Attorney fees under Section 1021.5 are available even in individual (non-class) CIPA actions if the case advances past a motion to strike, which materially increases plaintiff-side incentives and defendant-side risk.
3. The "tester plaintiff" problem — that serial CIPA litigants may not suffer genuine harm — is a viable defense theory grounded in CIPA § 637.2's statutory standing requirement, but one that requires factual development beyond the pleading stage.

### Broader CIPA Pen Register Landscape

As of mid-2024, California state and federal courts remained divided on core questions about CIPA pen register claims: (1) whether common website technologies qualify as "pen registers" or "trap and trace devices"; (2) whether plaintiffs must allege a concrete injury-in-fact beyond the bare statutory violation to satisfy CIPA § 637.2's standing requirement; (3) whether the $5,000 per-violation remedy is exclusive; and (4) whether punitive damages and attorney fees are available. The [American Bar Association](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/business_law/resources/business-law-today/2024-august/californias-invasion-privacy-act/) characterized CIPA as "a new frontier for website tracking litigation" with fundamentally unsettled law. The *Sanchez* tentative ruling and subsequent reversal exemplify this uncertainty — with a single judge reaching opposite conclusions on punitive damages within weeks.

## Action Items

- **Audit website tracking technology immediately.** Identify all third-party pixels, session-replay tools, analytics tags, and advertising scripts deployed on California-facing websites. Determine whether any constitute "pen registers" or "trap and trace devices" under CIPA § 638.51.
- **Implement a consent mechanism for tracking.** CIPA § 638.51(b) provides a defense where the person being tracked has given consent. Review whether existing cookie consent or privacy notice mechanisms are legally sufficient to establish CIPA consent.
- **Do not assume exclusive-remedy as a complete defense.** The case law split on whether CIPA's statutory damages preclude punitive damages and attorney fees means defendants must plan for the possibility that both additional remedies survive motions to strike in California Superior Court.
- **Assess concrete injury and statutory standing arguments.** CIPA § 637.2 requires plaintiffs to allege a concrete injury-in-fact beyond the bare statutory violation. Both in state and federal court, evaluate whether the plaintiff's alleged harm is sufficient to confer standing, particularly where the plaintiff is a professional tester with no particularized injury from tracking.
- **Monitor amendments to the complaint.** Given that Judge Treu granted leave to amend on punitive damages with a 20-day window, the *Sanchez* litigation continues to develop. Track whether the amended complaint survives a renewed motion to strike.
- **Review insurance coverage.** Evaluate whether cyber liability or directors-and-officers policies respond to CIPA pen register claims, including the punitive damages component to the extent state law does not bar insuring such awards.
