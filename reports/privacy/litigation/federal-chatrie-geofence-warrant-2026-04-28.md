# Supreme Court Hears Arguments in Chatrie v. United States on Constitutionality of Geofence Warrants

**Jurisdiction:** Federal | **Category:** Privacy | **Date:** 2026-04-28

## Summary [HIGH confidence]

On April 27, 2026, the U.S. Supreme Court heard approximately two hours of oral arguments in [*Chatrie v. United States*](https://www.scotusblog.com/cases/chatrie-v-united-states/), No. 25-112 — the first case in which the Court will directly decide whether law enforcement's use of geofence warrants violates the Fourth Amendment's prohibition on unreasonable searches. The justices appeared closely divided, with some skeptical of broad privacy claims and others troubled by the mass collection of location data from innocent bystanders. A decision is expected by the end of the Court's current term, likely June or July 2026, and will set a binding nationwide standard governing geofence warrant practice for law enforcement and data obligations for technology companies.

## Key Facts [HIGH confidence]

- The case arose from a 2019 armed robbery of a credit union in Midlothian, Virginia. Investigators obtained a [geofence warrant](https://www.scotusblog.com/2026/04/justices-appear-mixed-on-whether-geofence-warrant-violated-the-fourth-amendment-/) directing Google to disclose location data for all devices within 150 meters of the bank during the 30 minutes before and after the robbery, yielding 19 accounts, then narrowing to 9 accounts over a two-hour window, and finally resolving to 3 accounts — one of which was Okello Chatrie's. ([SCOTUSblog](https://www.scotusblog.com/2026/04/justices-appear-mixed-on-whether-geofence-warrant-violated-the-fourth-amendment-/))

- The Supreme Court granted certiorari on January 16, 2026, docketing the case as No. 25-112. The decision below is [136 F.4th 100](https://www.ca4.uscourts.gov/opinions/224489.p.pdf), issued by the en banc Fourth Circuit in April 2025. ([Supreme Court docket](https://www.supremecourt.gov/search.aspx?filename=%2Fdocket%2Fdocketfiles%2Fhtml%2Fpublic%2F25-112.html))

- The en banc Fourth Circuit split 7–7 on whether a Fourth Amendment "search" occurred, but 14 of 15 judges voted to affirm denial of suppression — most on the grounds that officers acted in good faith reliance on the warrant. ([Venable LLP](https://www.venable.com/insights/publications/2025/05/update-fourth-circuit-dodges-constitutionality))

- Chatrie's counsel, Adam G. Unikowsky of Jenner & Block, argued that the geofence warrant is an unconstitutional general warrant — a dragnet that sweeps in the location data of hundreds of innocent people without particularized probable cause. ([SCOTUSblog](https://www.scotusblog.com/2026/04/justices-appear-mixed-on-whether-geofence-warrant-violated-the-fourth-amendment-/))

- The government, represented by Deputy Solicitor General Eric J. Feigin, argued users voluntarily shared their location with Google by enabling Location History, invoking the third-party doctrine. ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/04/28/scotus-chatrie-geofence-search-warrant-ruling-arguments/))

- Justice Amy Coney Barrett expressed skepticism about Chatrie's privacy claim, noting the warrant covered only "a few hours" during which Chatrie was in "a public location" where "nobody has a reasonable expectation of privacy in their publicly observable movements." ([SCOTUSblog](https://www.scotusblog.com/2026/04/justices-appear-mixed-on-whether-geofence-warrant-violated-the-fourth-amendment-/))

- Justice Sonia Sotomayor pressed the government on consent, noting the trial court found Chatrie had not "necessarily" consented to share his data, and questioned whether consent given "at midnight to try to get your phone to work correctly" constitutes meaningful consent to government access. ([SCOTUSblog](https://www.scotusblog.com/2026/04/justices-appear-mixed-on-whether-geofence-warrant-violated-the-fourth-amendment-/))

- Chief Justice Roberts asked whether the government's logic would allow law enforcement to identify every person attending a church or political rally via the same technique — a question the government answered by noting individuals can disable Location History. ([NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/04/27/nx-s1-5800863/supreme-court-weighs-geofence-warrants))

- Twenty-nine amicus briefs were filed across the ideological spectrum from technology companies, trade associations, civil liberties organizations, and law enforcement groups. ([Brookings Institution](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/supreme-court-weighs-constitutionality-of-geofence-warrants/))

- Google separately announced in 2023 that it would begin storing Location History locally on user devices and reduce default retention periods — changes that could reduce future geofence warrant volume but do not moot the Chatrie case. ([Brookings Institution](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/supreme-court-agrees-to-hear-a-fourth-amendment-case-regarding-geofence-warrants/))

- At peak usage (2020–2023), geofence warrants constituted over 25% of all warrants Google received from U.S. law enforcement. Google reported a 1,500% increase in geofence requests from 2017 to 2018 alone. ([Congress.gov CRS Report LSB11274](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/LSB11274))

## Legal Background [HIGH confidence]

**Geofence warrants and the third-party doctrine.** Traditional geofence warrants compel technology companies that collect user location data — principally through opt-in features like Google's Location History — to disclose which devices were within a defined geographic perimeter during a specified time window. Law enforcement then iteratively narrows the pool from all devices in the area to identified individuals.

**Carpenter v. United States (2018).** The Court's most recent significant Fourth Amendment decision on digital location data, [*Carpenter v. United States*](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/17pdf/16-402_new3.pdf), 585 U.S. 296 (2018), held that the third-party doctrine does not apply to seven days or more of cell-site location information (CSLI) and that obtaining such records requires a warrant. *Carpenter* left expressly open whether other forms of digital location data — including data voluntarily contributed to app platforms — receive the same protection. *Chatrie* squarely presents that open question for Google's opt-in Location History product.

**Circuit split.** The Fourth Circuit's en banc decision directly conflicts with a Fifth Circuit ruling that declared geofence warrants unconstitutional, creating the circuit split that prompted the Supreme Court to grant certiorari. ([DLG Learning Center analysis](https://dlglearningcenter.com/dividing-lines-the-fourth-circuits-take-on-geofence-warrants-in-united-states-v-chatrie-amid-growing-circuit-split/))

**Question presented.** The official question presented, as stated in the [Supreme Court's case page](https://www.supremecourt.gov/qp/25-00112qp.pdf): whether the execution of the geofence warrant in this case violated the Fourth Amendment of the United States Constitution.

## Action Items

- **Technology companies holding location data:** Audit how you collect, store, and share precise location data. Develop (or refresh) standard operating procedures for responding to geofence warrants, including legal review protocols and user-notification policies. Do not wait for the decision — the Court's ruling may require rapid policy changes.

- **Law enforcement agencies:** Consult legal counsel before issuing new geofence warrants pending the decision. Prepare contingency protocols for a ruling that imposes additional particularity or probable-cause requirements beyond current practice.

- **Privacy counsel and compliance officers:** Monitor the docket at [SCOTUS docket 25-112](https://www.supremecourt.gov/search.aspx?filename=%2Fdocket%2Fdocketfiles%2Fhtml%2Fpublic%2F25-112.html) for the opinion, expected by end of June or July 2026. Brief executives and product teams on potential scope changes to location data features. Consider whether opt-in consent language for location services is sufficiently granular and informed to withstand post-*Chatrie* scrutiny.

- **Organizations collecting or processing location data on behalf of clients:** Review contracts for indemnification or compliance obligations that could be triggered by a ruling that expands Fourth Amendment protections. Assess exposure from location data brokers in your supply chain.

- **Keyword and reverse-warrant users:** Note that the *Chatrie* decision may also signal the Court's approach to reverse-keyword warrants and other data-dragnet investigative techniques. A broad ruling limiting geofence warrants could have direct implications for these related practices.
