# Inbox Overload: Massachusetts Lawsuits Target Online Retailers' Email Collection at Checkout

**Jurisdiction:** Massachusetts | **Category:** Privacy | **Date:** 2024-08-15

## Executive Summary [MEDIUM confidence]

A coordinated wave of class-action lawsuits filed in mid-2024 targets online retailers for allegedly requiring customers to provide email addresses during credit card checkouts in violation of Massachusetts General Laws Chapter 93, Section 105 — the Consumer Privacy in Commercial Transactions Act ("CPICTA"). At least four initial complaints were filed in Massachusetts Superior Court in the summer of 2024, naming defendants including Macy's, Inc., GameStop Corp., Bloomingdale's Inc., and BG Retail, LLC (Famous Footwear's parent). The central legal theory is that an email address constitutes "personal identification information" (PII) under a statute enacted in 1991 — before email existed as a consumer phenomenon — and that requiring it during an online credit card transaction is therefore unlawful. The litigation carries significant financial exposure: the statute provides statutory damages of $25 per violation with potential trebling for knowing violations, and class members are Massachusetts consumers who made credit card purchases on the affected websites. As of late 2024 and into 2025, at least one federal court denied a motion to dismiss, allowing the claims to proceed and signaling that plaintiffs' novel theory has legs.

## Background [HIGH confidence]

Massachusetts General Laws Chapter 93, Section 105 (["CPICTA"](https://malegislature.gov/Laws/GeneralLaws/PartI/TitleXV/Chapter93/Section105)) was enacted in 1991, contemporaneous with similar point-of-sale data collection statutes in other states such as California's Song-Beverly Credit Card Act. The statute prohibits any business that accepts a credit card from writing, causing to be written, or requiring a cardholder to write "personal identification information" (PII) on a credit card transaction form — unless that information is required by the card issuer.

The statute expressly identifies a cardholder's "address or telephone number" as examples of PII, but does not mention email addresses. When the law was passed, consumer email was not in widespread use, and the Massachusetts legislature has not amended Section 105 in over 30 years to incorporate email as a covered data element. This gap between the statutory text and modern e-commerce practices became the fault line that plaintiffs' counsel exploited in the 2024 litigation.

The threshold judicial precedent for expanding CPICTA's scope came with [*Tyler v. Michaels Stores, Inc.*, SJC-11145 (Mass. 2013)](https://law.justia.com/cases/massachusetts/supreme-court/2013/sjc-11145.html). The Massachusetts Supreme Judicial Court (SJC) held in *Tyler* that (1) ZIP codes constitute personal identification information under the statute; (2) a plaintiff may bring an action absent identity fraud — meaning mere collection of PII without downstream harm is actionable; and (3) the phrase "credit card transaction form" applies equally to electronic and paper forms. The SJC reasoned that ZIP codes, combined with a customer's name, allow a merchant to identify the consumer's full address or telephone number through publicly available databases — the very information Section 105 expressly protects. This interpretive method — looking not just at the data collected but at what it can enable — forms the blueprint for the email-address theory: an email address uniquely identifies a person and enables direct marketing contact without the consumer's consent.

Prior waves of CPICTA class actions targeted ZIP code collection and establishment of stored loyalty profiles that captured PII without disclosure. The 2024 email-collection cases represent a third generation of Section 105 litigation, with a new plaintiff-side theory that email addresses are "personal identification information" within the statute's non-exhaustive definition.

## Detailed Analysis [MEDIUM confidence]

### The Statutory Framework

[Massachusetts G.L. c. 93, § 105(a)](https://malegislature.gov/Laws/GeneralLaws/PartI/TitleXV/Chapter93/Section105) provides in relevant part:

> No person, firm, partnership, corporation or other business entity that accepts a credit card for a business transaction shall write, cause to be written or require that a credit card holder write personal identification information, not required by the credit card issuer, on the credit card transaction form.

The statute defines "personal identification information" to include, but not be limited to, a credit card holder's address or telephone number. The non-exhaustive "shall include, but shall not be limited to" language is what creates space for plaintiffs to argue that email addresses fall within the statute's scope by analogy to telephone numbers and addresses, both of which enable direct personal contact.

Any violation is deemed an unfair and deceptive trade practice under [Massachusetts G.L. c. 93A](https://malegislature.gov/Laws/GeneralLaws/PartI/TitleXV/Chapter93A), §§ 2 and 9. This linkage is important: it triggers the Chapter 93A private right of action and its fee-shifting and treble-damages provisions, in addition to the $25 per-violation statutory damages floor under Section 105 itself.

The statute does provide safe-harbor exceptions permitting collection of PII that is "necessary for the completion of a transaction" such as shipping, delivery, installation, or a warranty. Defendants have argued that email addresses collected for order confirmation fall within this exception. Plaintiffs counter that post-purchase marketing emails are not necessary for completion of the transaction and that the defendants' actual use of the data (sending unsolicited promotional emails) demonstrates the information was not genuinely required by the transaction.

### The 2024 Lawsuits

According to reporting by [Womble Bond Dickinson on JDSupra (August 15, 2024)](https://www.jdsupra.com/legalnews/inbox-overload-massachusetts-lawsuits-2392145/) and [National Law Review](https://natlawreview.com/article/inbox-overload-massachusetts-lawsuits-target-online-retailers-email-collection), two plaintiff-side law firms working in coordination filed at least four class action complaints in Massachusetts Superior Court, Suffolk County, in the summer of 2024:

- **Temesgen v. Macy's, Inc.**, Mass. Super. Ct., No. 2484-CV-01680 (filed June 25, 2024)
- **Magnuson v. GameStop Corp.**, Mass. Super. Ct., No. 2484-CV-02058
- **DeFelippis v. Bloomingdale's Inc.**, Mass. Super. Ct., No. 2484-CV-02059
- **Carr v. BG Retail, LLC (Famous Footwear)**, Mass. Super. Ct., No. 2484-CV-02060

In each case, the named plaintiff alleges that during online checkout they were required to provide an email address to complete a credit card purchase. After the purchase, the plaintiff received unsolicited marketing emails — sometimes even without clicking an optional consent checkbox during checkout. Each plaintiff seeks to represent a class of all Massachusetts consumers who made a credit card purchase on the retailer's website, and seeks statutory damages, injunctive relief, and attorney's fees and costs under Chapters 93, § 105 and 93A.

The *Carr* case was later removed to federal court and refiled as **Carr v. BG Retail, LLC & Caleres, Inc.**, No. 24-12387 (D. Mass.).

### The Federal Court Ruling in *Carr v. BG Retail*

In a significant development for the litigation wave, the United States District Court for the District of Massachusetts [denied the defendants' motion to dismiss](https://natlawreview.com/article/federal-court-allows-chapter-93a-claims-based-unwanted-marketing-emails-proceed) in *Carr v. BG Retail*. The court found that the plaintiff's allegations of concrete harm — including nuisance from marketing emails, wasted time, displacement of important messages, and consumption of email storage — were sufficiently objective and plausible at the pleading stage to establish Article III standing.

The court also allowed the Chapter 93A claims to proceed. Defendants had argued that their checkout page disclosed that providing an email address constituted consent to receive marketing communications, but the court held that whether the combined presentation had a "tendency to deceive" within the meaning of Chapter 93A was too fact-intensive to resolve on a motion to dismiss.

This ruling is the most consequential litigation development in the 2024 wave: it signals that at the pleading stage, plaintiffs' theory that email addresses are PII under CPICTA and that their use for marketing constitutes an unfair and deceptive practice is viable.

### Contested Legal Questions

The 2024 cases present several unresolved legal questions:

1. **Is an email address "personal identification information" under Section 105?** The statute's list is non-exhaustive and predates email. Defendants argue that because the Legislature has not added email addresses in 30+ years, the omission is deliberate. Plaintiffs argue that *Tyler v. Michaels* established an expansive, functional interpretation focused on whether data enables contact with or identification of the consumer — which email clearly does. [Blank Rome's analysis](https://www.blankrome.com/publications/checkout-challenged-navigating-new-wave-e-mail-address-lawsuits-massachusetts) identifies this as the pivotal unresolved statutory interpretation question.

2. **Does "required" mean technically required or effectively required?** Retailers often make the email field mandatory in their checkout UX even if the underlying transaction could theoretically proceed without it. Courts will likely need to assess whether practical coercion — you cannot click "Place Order" without filling in the email field — constitutes "requiring" under the statute.

3. **Does the transaction-completion exception apply?** Defendants argue that order confirmation emails are necessary for completion of the transaction. Courts will assess whether "necessary" is satisfied by a legitimate business need or only by a strict technical necessity imposed by the card issuer.

4. **What is the class-wide damages exposure?** At $25 per violation — each individual purchase being one violation — multiplied by thousands or millions of online transactions, aggregate damages could be enormous even before trebling. One commentator noted that the statute's damages math makes even a small percentage of Massachusetts consumers producing astronomical aggregate liability for high-volume online retailers.

### Comparison with CAN-SPAM

Defendants may argue that compliance with the federal CAN-SPAM Act — which permits commercial emails until the recipient opts out — preempts or satisfies state obligations. Courts are likely to reject this defense: CAN-SPAM expressly does not preempt state laws that are "not specifically directed at electronic mail" and Section 105 is a point-of-sale data collection statute, not an anti-spam law. The [*Carr* court's refusal to dismiss the claims](https://natlawreview.com/article/federal-court-allows-chapter-93a-claims-based-unwanted-marketing-emails-proceed) is consistent with this analysis.

## Impact Assessment [MEDIUM confidence]

### Affected Businesses

Any online retailer that:
- Accepts credit cards for transactions;
- Has Massachusetts-resident customers; and
- Collects email addresses in its checkout flow as a mandatory or effectively mandatory field

is potentially exposed to CPICTA liability. This is not a niche category — it describes the standard e-commerce checkout flow used by the overwhelming majority of online retailers.

Brick-and-mortar retailers with online storefronts are equally at risk, as *Tyler v. Michaels* already confirmed that "credit card transaction forms" include electronic forms.

### Damages Exposure

The financial exposure under CPICTA's damages structure is potentially severe for high-volume retailers:

- **Statutory damages:** $25 per violation (each transaction = one violation)
- **Treble damages:** Up to $75 per violation for knowing or willful violations
- **Attorney's fees and costs:** Chapter 93A's fee-shifting provision creates strong plaintiff-side economic incentives
- **Injunctive relief:** Courts may order cessation of the email-collection practice at checkout

For a retailer processing 100,000 Massachusetts credit card transactions annually, the base statutory damages exposure is $2.5 million per year; trebled exposure is $7.5 million per year. Class certification would aggregate claims across all class members. This damages arithmetic explains the coordinated, multi-defendant litigation strategy observed in 2024.

### Compliance Timeline

There is no pending legislation or regulatory deadline driving compliance. The risk is immediate: any pending Massachusetts credit card transaction involving a mandatory email field is a potential violation already accruing liability. The statute does not require advance notice or regulatory enforcement action before a private plaintiff may sue.

### Enforcement Outlook

The plaintiff-side bar's coordinated filing strategy in 2024, combined with the *Carr* court's denial of the motion to dismiss, suggests that CPICTA email-collection litigation will continue and likely expand. Additional defendants may be targeted as firms map the Massachusetts consumer base of online retailers. The SJC has not yet definitively ruled on whether email addresses are PII under Section 105; that question is likely to arise either on certification, summary judgment, or eventual SJC review of a dispositive ruling.

## Action Items

- **Audit checkout flows immediately.** Identify any credit card checkout page that makes email address entry mandatory (rather than optional) for Massachusetts consumers. This is the primary risk scenario.
- **Make email optional or segregate the field.** If an email address is genuinely required for order confirmation or shipping notification, consider making it optional and not placing it on the credit card transaction form itself — or ensure clear consent to marketing is obtained separately, before or during account creation, not at checkout.
- **Do not rely on CAN-SPAM compliance.** Federal opt-out email compliance does not satisfy Massachusetts CPICTA, which operates at the point of data collection, not downstream consent management.
- **Review disclosure language.** Even if the checkout page discloses that providing an email constitutes consent to marketing, the *Carr* court found this insufficient at the pleading stage. Disclosure language must be clear, conspicuous, and not buried in terms of service.
- **Monitor court developments.** The *Carr* case and the Massachusetts Superior Court cases are the leading indicators. Any SJC ruling on whether email qualifies as PII under Section 105 will be binding precedent. Track docket activity for Magnuson v. GameStop (No. 2484-CV-02058), DeFelippis v. Bloomingdale's (No. 2484-CV-02059), Temesgen v. Macy's (No. 2484-CV-01680), and Carr v. BG Retail (D. Mass. No. 24-12387).
- **Consult Massachusetts-specific privacy counsel.** This is a Massachusetts-specific risk that is not fully mitigated by general privacy compliance programs. Retailers should seek counsel experienced with Chapter 93, § 105 and Chapter 93A litigation.
