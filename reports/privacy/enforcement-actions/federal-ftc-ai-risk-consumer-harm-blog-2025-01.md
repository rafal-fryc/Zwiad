# FTC Staff Blog Outlines Four AI Risk Factors for Companies — and Is Subsequently Removed Under Trump Administration

**Jurisdiction:** Federal | **Category:** Privacy / AI | **Date:** January 3, 2025

## Executive Summary [MEDIUM confidence]

On January 3, 2025, FTC staff published a blog post titled "AI and the Risk of Consumer Harm" on the agency's Office of Technology blog, outlining four factors companies should consider when developing or deploying AI products to avoid violating federal consumer protection laws. The post, authored by seven FTC staff members across the Office of Technology and the Bureau of Consumer Protection's Division of Advertising Practices, emphasized that existing statutes — including Section 5 of the FTC Act — apply fully to AI-related products and services, with no AI exemption. The blog was not formal guidance but signaled the FTC's enforcement priorities and analytical framework at the close of the Lina Khan chairmanship. By October 2025, the Trump-era FTC had removed the post from the agency website, along with other Khan-era AI guidance materials — a development that introduces uncertainty about whether the four factors remain operative as an informal enforcement roadmap.

## Background [MEDIUM confidence]

The January 2025 blog post came at the end of a period of active FTC AI oversight under Chair Lina Khan (2021–January 2025). The FTC had signaled its AI enforcement posture through a series of policy statements, business guidance documents, and enforcement sweeps in the years prior.

The most significant precursor was **Operation AI Comply**, announced [September 25, 2024](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/09/ftc-announces-crackdown-deceptive-ai-claims-schemes), in which the FTC brought five simultaneous enforcement actions against companies that had allegedly used AI claims to deceive consumers. Targets included DoNotPay (settled for $193,000 over claims of being an "AI lawyer"), Ascend Ecom and Ecommerce Empire Builders (challenged for fabricated AI-powered income guarantees), FBA Machine (alleged $15.9 million consumer harm), and Rytr (challenged for generating fake AI reviews). These actions established that Section 5 prohibitions on unfair or deceptive acts apply to AI products as to any other commercial offering.

The January 3, 2025 blog post — published just weeks before Chair Khan's departure — distilled the FTC's accumulated enforcement experience with AI into a practitioner-facing framework, organized around four categories of risk.

## Detailed Analysis [MEDIUM confidence]

### The Four Factors

The FTC blog identified four areas that companies developing or deploying AI must address. Staff explicitly noted that these four factors are "not a comprehensive overview of what companies should be considering when they design, build, test, and deploy their own products" — but rather the areas the FTC has most actively monitored and where enforcement exposure is highest.

**1. Pre-Deployment and Ongoing Due Diligence**

Companies must assess and mitigate potential downstream harm both before and during deployment. This encompasses evaluating the impact of AI-powered decisions on consumers — particularly in contexts such as credit, employment, housing, and healthcare — where automated or AI-assisted decisions can produce discriminatory or inaccurate outcomes. The FTC had previously emphasized that companies offering AI models must test for, detect, and address bias and accuracy failures before releasing products to market.

**2. Detecting and Removing Harmful AI-Generated Content**

Companies must take preventive steps to detect and remove AI-generated deepfakes, non-consensual intimate imagery (NCII), and child sexual abuse material (CSAM). The FTC tied this factor to its broader enforcement agenda on AI-enabled fraud and impersonation, noting that the proliferation of generative AI tools has dramatically lowered the barrier to creating such content.

**3. Avoiding Deceptive Claims About AI Capabilities**

Building directly on Operation AI Comply, the blog warned against false or exaggerated claims about AI products — whether overstating accuracy, intelligence, automation level, or income-generating potential. The FTC had taken action under Section 5 against "AI washing" — the practice of attaching AI branding to products that do not use the described technology — in addition to outright performance misrepresentations.

**4. Data Security and Privacy**

AI models — especially large generative AI systems — are trained on and process significant volumes of personal data, potentially including highly sensitive categories such as health, financial, or biometric information. The FTC reiterated its longstanding data security and privacy enforcement record and signaled that AI data practices would be evaluated against existing standards (Safeguards Rule, Health Breach Notification Rule, and Section 5 data security precedents).

### Legal Basis

The blog post identified the primary statutory authority as [Section 5 of the FTC Act](https://www.ftc.gov/legal-library/browse/statutes/federal-trade-commission-act), which prohibits "unfair or deceptive acts or practices in or affecting commerce." The FTC has long used Section 5 as a technology-neutral consumer protection tool; the blog reaffirmed that no AI-specific legislation is required for the FTC to act against harmful AI products.

The post also referenced the FTC's authority under sector-specific statutes — including the Health Breach Notification Rule — and its established data security enforcement framework, under which companies have faced multimillion-dollar settlements for failing to implement reasonable security measures.

### Authorship and Status

The post was authored by Michael Atleson, Shoshana Wodinsky, Stephanie Nguyen, Dan Salsburg, Leah Frazier, Mark Suter, and Sam Levine — staff from across the Office of Technology and the Division of Advertising Practices. It was published on January 3, 2025, the final weeks of the Khan FTC.

By October 2025, the post had been removed from the FTC website. [TechCrunch reported](https://techcrunch.com/2025/10/20/ftc-removes-lina-khan-era-posts-about-ai-risks-and-open-source/) that the Trump-era FTC quietly deleted multiple Khan-era AI guidance materials, including "AI and the Risk of Consumer Harm," "On Open-Weights Foundation Models" (July 2024), and "Consumers Are Voicing Concerns About AI" (October 2023). The FTC provided no public explanation. Critics noted the removals may implicate the Federal Records Act and the Open Government Data Act. An archived copy of the original post is preserved in [a PDF mirror](https://data.aclum.org/storage/2025/01/FTC_www_ftc_gov_policy_advocacy-research_tech-at-ftc_2025_01_ai-risk-consumer-harm.pdf).

## Impact Assessment [MEDIUM confidence]

### Who Is Affected

The blog's four-factor framework applied broadly to any company that:
- Develops AI models, AI-powered products, or AI-enabled services for consumer-facing applications;
- Deploys third-party AI tools in ways that affect consumers (e.g., AI-powered customer service, credit decisioning, or targeted advertising);
- Makes marketing claims referencing AI, machine learning, or algorithmic capabilities;
- Handles personal data in the course of AI product development or operation.

This covers a wide swath of the commercial AI market — from large-platform generative AI providers to specialized vertical-AI startups in fintech, healthtech, legaltech, and adtech.

### Enforcement Outlook and Post-Removal Uncertainty

The removal of the blog post does not extinguish FTC enforcement authority under Section 5 of the FTC Act. The statutory prohibition on unfair or deceptive acts and practices remains in full force regardless of agency website content. However, the removal creates ambiguity about how the current FTC Commission weighs these four specific risk categories.

Significantly, the FTC under the Trump administration has shown mixed signals on AI enforcement. The agency set aside its Rytr consent order in late 2025, citing the White House AI Action Plan's emphasis on not unduly burdening AI innovation. At the same time, FTC enforcement actions in 2025 continued in areas such as AI-washing, consumer harm from AI-generated content, and data security failures.

Companies should not interpret the blog's removal as regulatory clearance. The four factors map directly to established Section 5 doctrines and prior enforcement actions — removal of the explanatory blog post does not repeal the underlying legal standards.

### Compliance Considerations

For companies in the AI supply chain, the four factors remain useful as an internal risk framework regardless of the current FTC's posture, because:
1. State attorneys general are increasingly active in AI enforcement using analogous state consumer protection statutes;
2. The four factors align with risk categories addressed in emerging state AI laws (e.g., Colorado SB 24-205, Texas HB 149);
3. International regulators (EU AI Act, UK AI frameworks) address substantially overlapping risk categories.

## Action Items

- Conduct an internal AI product audit mapped against the four FTC factors: (1) pre-deployment due diligence, (2) harmful content detection and removal, (3) accuracy and capability claims review, and (4) data security and privacy compliance.
- Review all external-facing AI marketing and product claims against FTC "AI washing" precedents from Operation AI Comply; ensure every AI capability claim is substantiated.
- Implement a documented AI risk assessment process for any new AI deployment that includes consumer harm testing before launch.
- Establish or audit data governance processes for AI training data and inference-time personal data processing, with particular attention to GLBA Safeguards Rule and HIPAA requirements for applicable industries.
- Monitor FTC enforcement activity under the current Commission for signals on which of the four factors remain active enforcement priorities; track state AG enforcement as an additional barometer.
- Preserve the archived PDF of the original FTC blog post as a compliance reference document given its removal from the FTC website.
