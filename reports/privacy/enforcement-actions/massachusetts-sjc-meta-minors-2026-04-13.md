# Massachusetts SJC Clears AG Campbell's Lawsuit Against Meta Over Addictive Design Features for Minors

**Jurisdiction:** Massachusetts | **Category:** Privacy | **Date:** 2026-04-13

## Summary [HIGH confidence]

On April 10, 2026, the Massachusetts Supreme Judicial Court (SJC) issued a first-of-its-kind ruling in [*Commonwealth v. Meta Platforms, Inc.*](https://law.justia.com/cases/massachusetts/supreme-court/2026/sjc-13747.html) (SJC-13747) holding that Section 230 of the federal Communications Decency Act does not shield Meta from a state consumer-protection lawsuit alleging that Instagram's design features are addictive and harmful to minors. The ruling allows Massachusetts Attorney General Andrea Joy Campbell's 2023 lawsuit to proceed on its merits, preserving both Chapter 93A consumer-protection claims and public-nuisance claims. This is the first state supreme court ruling in the country to hold that Section 230 does not categorically immunize a platform's product-design and marketing decisions.

## Key Facts [HIGH confidence]

- The [SJC opinion](https://www.mass.gov/doc/commonwealth-v-meta-platforms-inc-sjc-m13747/download), authored by Justice Dalila Argaez Wendlandt, affirmed denial of Meta's motion to dismiss on Section 230 grounds in *Commonwealth v. Meta Platforms, Inc.*, SJC-13747, decided April 10, 2026
- The claims target Meta's design choices — push notifications, infinite scroll, "likes," ephemeral/time-limited content, and algorithmic recommendations — alleged to exploit developmental vulnerabilities of adolescent users, per [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/04/10/meta-must-face-youth-addiction-lawsuit-by-massachusetts-court-rules.html)
- The court held that claims based on Meta's own conduct (product design and safety misrepresentations) do not require treating Meta as a "publisher or speaker" of third-party content under [Section 230(c)(1), 47 U.S.C. § 230](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/47/230)
- Campbell's 2023 complaint was filed alongside parallel actions by 40+ other state AGs; at least 34 states are pursuing similar cases against Meta in federal court and at least 9 states are pursuing cases in state court, per [Commonwealth Beacon](https://commonwealthbeacon.org/courts/in-first-ruling-of-its-kind-mass-high-court-says-meta-not-shielded-from-lawsuits-over-addictive-features/)
- Instagram had over 300,000 daily active Massachusetts users aged 13–17, cited as the basis for parens patriae standing
- On April 8–9, 2026, the Massachusetts House passed a youth-technology bill 129–25 banning under-14s from social media; the bill now heads to the Senate, per [WGBH](https://www.wgbh.org/news/politics/2026-04-08/mass-house-passes-bill-to-ban-kids-under-14-from-social-media)
- On April 14-15, 2026, Governor Maura Healey filed a separate, more targeted bill that would require platforms to implement default settings for users under 18, including a two-hour daily time limit, per [WBUR](https://www.wbur.org/news/2026/04/15/maura-healey-social-media-bill-massachusetts)

## The SJC Ruling [HIGH confidence]

The decision, authored by Justice Dalila Argaez Wendlandt, affirmed a lower-court ruling denying Meta's motion to dismiss on Section 230 grounds. The SJC is the first state high court to rule that Section 230 does not categorically bar state-law claims premised on an online platform's own design choices — as distinct from claims seeking to impose liability for third-party content.

The court emphasized that the Commonwealth's claims "do not seek to impose liability on Meta for information provided by third parties." The claims allege "harm stemming from Meta's own conduct either by designing a social media platform that capitalizes on the developmental vulnerabilities of children or by affirmatively misleading consumers about the safety of the Instagram platform."

AG Campbell called the ruling a "major step in holding these companies accountable for practices that have fueled the youth mental health crisis."

## AG's Legal Claims [HIGH confidence]

Campbell's complaint, originally filed in 2023 alongside parallel actions by 40+ other state attorneys general, advances two principal theories:

1. **Chapter 93A (Unfair and Deceptive Trade Practices).** [M.G.L. c. 93A, § 2](https://malegislature.gov/Laws/GeneralLaws/PartI/TitleXV/Chapter93a/Section2) prohibits "unfair or deceptive acts or practices in the conduct of any trade or commerce." The AG alleges Meta (a) deployed manipulative design features — push notifications, infinite scroll, "likes," ephemeral/time-limited content, and recommendation algorithms — engineered to exploit developmental vulnerabilities of adolescent users, and (b) affirmatively misrepresented the safety of Instagram and Facebook for minors despite internal research documenting harms.

2. **Public Nuisance.** The Commonwealth asserts that Meta's platform design has materially contributed to the adolescent mental-health crisis, constituting an unreasonable interference with public health and welfare. The SJC allowed this claim to proceed alongside the 93A claim.

The complaint cites Instagram's Massachusetts footprint — over 300,000 daily active users aged 13–17 — as the basis for parens patriae standing.

## Section 230 Defense [HIGH confidence]

Meta argued that [Section 230(c)(1) of the Communications Decency Act, 47 U.S.C. § 230](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/47/230), immunized it because the alleged harms ultimately flow from users' consumption of third-party content delivered through the platform. Meta invoked the familiar line of authority treating algorithmic curation and recommendation as protected "publisher" activity.

The SJC rejected this framing. The court held that claims targeting a platform's own features and its own statements about safety do not require the platform to be treated as a "publisher or speaker" of third-party content. Because the duty Meta allegedly breached — not to design a dangerously addictive product; not to misrepresent product safety — is independent of any duty arising from third-party speech, Section 230 does not preempt the Commonwealth's claims.

This is the first state-supreme-court-level holding on this question and reinforces an emerging consensus that Section 230 protects hosting decisions, not product-design and marketing decisions. The decision is likely to be cited in the other 34+ state AG actions now pending in federal court.

## Multi-State AG Litigation Context [HIGH confidence]

The Massachusetts ruling did not emerge in isolation. As of April 2026:

- **40+ state AGs** filed parallel actions against Meta in 2023, originally coordinated through a federal MDL proceeding (MDL 3047) in the Northern District of California
- **At least 34 states** continue to pursue Meta in federal court under MDL 3047 on similar product-design theories
- **At least 9 states** (including Massachusetts) filed in their own state courts to pursue state-specific consumer-protection theories, often to avoid the Section 230 federal-preemption argument in a less favorable federal forum
- The federal MDL 3047 court in California [previously denied Meta's motion to dismiss](https://reports/privacy/childrens-privacy/california-meta-coppa-mdl3047-ruling-2024-10-22.md) on similar grounds in 2024, reinforcing the multistate trend
- Iowa AG Brenna Bird filed a new state-court action the same week as the Massachusetts SJC decision, underscoring the momentum this ruling provides to the state AG coalition

The Massachusetts ruling is particularly significant because it is the first state high court decision — as opposed to a trial court or federal district court ruling — to reach this conclusion on Section 230, giving it persuasive weight in every other pending state and federal forum.

## Separate Legislation: Youth Online Safety Bills [HIGH confidence]

Three distinct legislative proposals are currently in play at the Massachusetts State House:

**House Bill (passed 129–25, April 8–9, 2026):**
- Under 14: Prohibited from creating or maintaining social media accounts; platforms must terminate existing non-compliant accounts
- Ages 14–15: Require verifiable parental consent
- Age 16+: No social-media age restrictions
- School cellphones: "Bell-to-bell" ban on student cellphone use in public K–12 schools
- Effective: October 1, 2026 if enacted
- The bill's definition of "social media" is reportedly broad enough to sweep in services such as YouTube, Wikipedia, and Roblox, per [Axios Boston](https://www.axios.com/local/boston/2026/04/09/massachusetts-house-social-media-age-verification-youth-technology-bill-ban-privacy)

**Senate Bill:** The Senate passed a narrower bell-to-bell cellphone ban only in July 2025. The two chambers will need to reconcile their divergent approaches on social media before sending a bill to the Governor.

**Governor Healey's Bill (filed April 14–15, 2026):** Rather than an outright ban, Healey's proposal would require platforms to implement special default settings for users under 18, including a two-hour daily time limit and disabled push notifications by default, per [WBUR](https://www.wbur.org/news/2026/04/15/maura-healey-social-media-bill-massachusetts). Healey characterized it as "not a ban" but a structural safety floor. The Governor's bill was described as "complementary" to both the House and Senate versions.

Comparable age-based social media restrictions in Florida, Utah, Ohio, and Arkansas have faced First Amendment litigation from NetChoice, and similar challenges are anticipated if Massachusetts enacts a categorical ban.

## Action Items

- **Audit addictive design features now.** The SJC ruling makes Massachusetts product-design liability claims viable. Identify and document the business rationale for every engagement-optimization feature (notification frequency, scroll behavior, recommendation algorithms, engagement metrics) targeted at under-18 users.
- **Review youth safety representations.** Chapter 93A's deception prong targets public statements about platform safety. Review marketing materials, parent-facing content, help-center pages, and investor disclosures for potential misrepresentation exposure.
- **Implement legal holds.** Internal studies of youth engagement, mental health impacts, and addictiveness are central evidence in these cases. Ensure litigation holds are in place for all 40+ state AG actions, not just Massachusetts.
- **Prepare for age assurance.** If the Massachusetts House bill becomes law in substantially its current form, compliant age-verification and parental-consent workflows must be operational by October 1, 2026. Begin vendor assessment now.
- **Monitor the Senate-House conference.** The House bill and Senate bill must be reconciled before reaching the Governor. Track conference committee proceedings and Governor Healey's separate proposal for the final statutory landscape.
- **Cross-state coordination.** A Massachusetts judgment or significant settlement will have precedential and practical ripple effects across all 40+ parallel state actions. National legal strategy should be coordinated accordingly.
