# Oregon OCPA Six-Month Enforcement Report: 110 Complaints, 21 Cure Notices, and a Roadmap to Stricter Enforcement

**Jurisdiction:** Oregon | **Category:** Privacy | **Date:** 2025-03-17

## Executive Summary [HIGH confidence]

Oregon Attorney General Dan Rayfield released the [first six-month enforcement report on the Oregon Consumer Privacy Act (OCPA)](https://www.doj.state.or.us/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/OCPA-Six-Month-Enforcement-Report.pdf) in early March 2025, covering the period from the law's effective date of July 1, 2024 through December 31, 2024. The report documents 110 consumer complaints — significantly more than other comparably sized states — and 21 closed enforcement matters, all resolved through cure notices. The most-complained-about industry is data brokers, particularly background-check websites, and the most frequently denied consumer right is the right to delete personal data. The report signals that the Oregon DOJ's Privacy Unit is operating with measurable effectiveness under the current notice-and-cure regime, and explicitly previews a shift to more aggressive, direct enforcement after the 30-day cure provision sunsets on January 1, 2026. Businesses doing business in Oregon should treat this report as a compliance audit checklist.

## Background and Statutory Framework [HIGH confidence]

The Oregon Consumer Privacy Act, codified at [ORS 646A.570–646A.589](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_646a.570), was enacted as Oregon SB 619, signed into law by Governor Tina Kotek on July 18, 2023, and took effect on July 1, 2024. It applies to any entity that:

- Conducts business in Oregon or provides products or services to Oregon residents, AND
- During a calendar year, controls or processes personal data of **100,000 or more consumers** (excluding data processed solely to complete a payment transaction), OR
- Controls or processes personal data of **25,000 or more consumers** while deriving **25% or more of annual gross revenue** from selling personal data.

The OCPA grants Oregon consumers six core rights:

1. The right to **confirm** whether a controller is processing their personal data and which categories are being processed.
2. The right to obtain a **list of specific third parties** to which personal data has been disclosed — a provision that distinguishes the OCPA from virtually all peer state privacy laws, which require only categories of third parties, not names.
3. The right to **obtain a portable copy** of personal data in a readily usable format.
4. The right to **correct** inaccuracies in personal data.
5. The right to **delete** personal data about the consumer.
6. The right to **opt out** of processing for targeted advertising, sale of personal data, and profiling for decisions with legal or similarly significant effects.

Enforcement authority is vested exclusively in the Oregon Attorney General. There is no private right of action. The OCPA additionally covers **sensitive data** more broadly than most peer state laws, extending protections to national origin, transgender or nonbinary status, and victim-of-crime status, in addition to categories such as racial and ethnic origin, health data, sexual orientation, and precise geolocation.

Critically, the OCPA contains a **notice-and-cure provision** through January 1, 2026: before initiating a formal enforcement action, the AG must provide a written notice of violation and allow the company 30 days to cure identified deficiencies. This provision does not apply after January 1, 2026.

## The Six-Month Enforcement Report: Key Findings [HIGH confidence]

The [official six-month enforcement report](https://www.doj.state.or.us/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/OCPA-Six-Month-Enforcement-Report.pdf) is available directly from the Oregon DOJ. The [Oregon AG's press release](https://www.doj.state.or.us/media-home/news-media-releases/attorney-general-rayfield-releases-6-month-report-on-oregon-consumer-privacy-act/) confirms the following key findings.

### Complaint Volume

The Privacy Unit received **110 consumer complaints** in the first six months. This volume is notable when compared to peer states: Connecticut, for example, received only approximately 30 complaints in the first six months following its privacy law's effective date. AG Rayfield cited this as evidence that Oregonians are both aware of their privacy rights and willing to exercise them.

The majority of complaints arrived via the Privacy Unit's online "Privacy Complaint Portal," which allows consumers to report suspected OCPA violations directly.

### Top Complaint Categories by Industry

The dominant complaint categories by industry were:

1. **Data brokers** — specifically background-check websites that compile and sell personal information including names, addresses, phone numbers, and criminal history records.
2. **Social media and technology platforms** — companies collecting, sharing, and monetizing user behavioral data.

### Most Frequently Violated Consumer Rights

The report identifies the most frequently denied consumer rights as:

1. **Right to delete** — the most requested and most frequently denied right, with consumers commonly receiving non-responses or inadequate responses to deletion requests.
2. **Right to obtain a copy of personal data** — specifically, many companies failed to include "back-end" data such as marketing profiles, inferred behavioral segments, or shopping patterns in their copy responses.
3. **Right to a list of specific third-party recipients** — companies frequently listed only categories of recipients rather than specific named entities, which does not satisfy Oregon's uniquely stringent disclosure requirement.

### Enforcement Actions: 21 Cure Notices Issued and Closed

The Privacy Unit initiated and closed **21 enforcement matters** during the six-month period. All were resolved through cure notices — none required civil penalty proceedings. The report characterizes the business response to cure notices as generally cooperative: most companies updated their privacy notices and improved their consumer rights mechanisms promptly after receiving DOJ contact.

### Common Compliance Deficiencies Identified in Cure Notices [HIGH confidence]

The report enumerates recurring deficiencies that led to cure notices:

- **Failure to disclose consumer rights** — some businesses had no privacy notice or failed to inform consumers of any OCPA rights whatsoever.
- **Inadequate third-party disclosures** — privacy notices listed only categories of third parties rather than the specific entities to which personal data was sold, as Oregon law requires.
- **Confusing or exclusionary privacy notices** — notices that acknowledged state-specific consumer rights by listing certain states (e.g., California, Colorado, Connecticut) but omitted Oregon explicitly, thereby creating ambiguity about whether Oregon rights apply.
- **Inadequate or burdensome rights mechanisms** — failure to provide a clear, functional, accessible link for consumers to exercise opt-out or other rights; mechanisms that were broken, incomplete, or designed to discourage requests.
- **Omission of back-end data in data access and deletion responses** — companies responding to access or deletion requests limited their responses to data consumers had directly provided (e.g., account registration data), excluding inferred or derived data profiles.

## Future Enforcement Priorities and Upcoming Changes [HIGH confidence]

### Sunset of Notice-and-Cure Provision: January 1, 2026

The single most significant development previewed in the report is the **sunset of the OCPA's 30-day cure requirement on January 1, 2026**. After that date, the Oregon AG may proceed directly to formal enforcement action — including civil penalty proceedings — without providing advance notice or any opportunity to cure. The report states explicitly that when the cure provision sunsets, the Privacy Unit intends to focus enforcement attention on **universal opt-out mechanism (UOOM)** compliance.

The OCPA requires controllers to honor consumer opt-out signals transmitted through universal opt-out mechanisms (sometimes called Global Privacy Control or GPC). The Privacy Unit did not enforce UOOM requirements during the cure period, but compliance with these signals will become a priority enforcement target in 2026.

### Nonprofit Extension: July 1, 2025

As of July 1, 2025, the OCPA's enforcement coverage extends to qualifying **nonprofit entities**. The Privacy Unit indicated in the report that it had already begun outreach to Oregon nonprofits and was developing nonprofit-specific compliance guidance and FAQs.

### Concurrent Legislative Development: HB 2008

Separately from the six-month enforcement report, Oregon enacted significant new restrictions on certain data practices through **HB 2008**, which was introduced in the 2025 Oregon legislative session on **February 27, 2025** and signed into law on **June 3, 2025**. These restrictions are not part of the original OCPA framework described in the enforcement report; they represent a distinct legislative development from the 2025 session.

HB 2008 added the following prohibitions, effective **January 1, 2026**:

- **Ban on selling precise geolocation data**: Controllers are prohibited from selling "precise geolocation data," defined as data capable of identifying an individual or device within a **1,750-foot radius**.
- **Ban on selling children's data**: Controllers are prohibited from selling the personal data of individuals under age 16.
- **Ban on using children's data for targeted advertising and certain profiling**: Using data of individuals under 16 for targeted advertising, or profiling in connection with decisions of legal or similarly significant effect, is prohibited.

Businesses affected by the geolocation and children's data restrictions should note that these obligations arise from HB 2008 — a separate 2025 legislative enactment — and not from the six-month enforcement report or the original OCPA. Compliance timelines and obligations should be tracked under HB 2008 independently.

## Analysis: What This Report Signals for Businesses [MEDIUM confidence]

Analysis from [Hunton Andrews Kurth](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-information-security-law/key-findings-from-oregons-consumer-privacy-act-report), [White & Case](https://www.whitecase.com/insight-alert/oregon-releases-report-first-six-months-its-oregon-consumer-privacy-act), [Wyrick Robbins](https://practicalprivacy.wyrick.com/blog/blazing-a-trail-in-oregon-the-ags-six-month-report-on-ocpa-complaints-and-enforcement), and [Fisher Phillips](https://www.fisherphillips.com/en/news-insights/consumer-privacy-complaints-pouring-in-from-oregonians-how-your-business-can-avoid-top-compliance-issues-noted-by-states-ag.html) reaches consistent conclusions.

**Oregon is a meaningful enforcement jurisdiction.** The 110 complaints — more than triple Connecticut's first-half complaint volume — demonstrate consumer engagement and AG capacity. The Privacy Unit is functioning and actively investigating. Organizations that assumed Oregon enforcement would be minimal should revise that assumption.

**The data broker sector faces concentrated risk.** Background-check websites and data aggregators were the primary complaint target. These businesses should audit their Oregon consumers' rights portals immediately, ensure deletion requests are processed promptly, and verify that their third-party disclosure lists specifically name recipient entities rather than merely describing categories.

**Third-party specificity is an Oregon-only compliance burden.** No other state consumer privacy law currently requires controllers to name specific third parties to which personal data has been disclosed, as opposed to describing categories. This creates a unique disclosure obligation that cannot be satisfied by copying disclosures from California or Colorado compliance programs.

**The 2026 enforcement shift is material.** Beginning January 1, 2026, the AG can proceed directly to civil penalty litigation without advance notice. [ORS 646A.589](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_646a.589) authorizes civil penalties of up to **$7,500 per violation**. Organizations that currently rely on the cure window as a compliance safety net will lose that protection. The report signals universal opt-out as the first enforcement priority post-cure sunset.

**Back-end data cannot be excluded from rights responses.** The Privacy Unit's finding that companies routinely omit inferred and derived data from access and deletion responses is a clear enforcement signal. Controllers must audit their data inventories and ensure that all personal data — including marketing profiles, behavioral segments, and inferred attributes — is within scope of their rights-response processes.

## Compliance Action Items

The following action items are drawn from the Privacy Unit's identified deficiency patterns:

1. **Audit your privacy notice** to confirm it explicitly lists Oregon consumer rights and is not limited to California or other named states.
2. **Review third-party disclosure practices**: ensure your privacy notice or rights-request responses name the specific third-party entities (not merely categories) to which personal data has been sold.
3. **Test your rights-request portal**: confirm it is functional, accessible, and provides a clear opt-out mechanism without undue friction.
4. **Expand data inventory scope**: ensure that marketing profiles, behavioral data, inferred data segments, and derived attributes are covered in your data map and treated as in-scope for access and deletion requests.
5. **Prepare for universal opt-out enforcement**: implement technical capability to honor Global Privacy Control signals before January 1, 2026.
6. **Assess children's data practices (HB 2008)**: if you operate platforms that collect data from under-16 users, implement controls to prevent sale of that data and use for targeted advertising by January 1, 2026, per HB 2008.
7. **Assess geolocation data practices (HB 2008)**: if precise geolocation data (within 1,750-foot radius) is sold, cease or restructure that activity before January 1, 2026, per HB 2008.
8. **Nonprofits**: if your organization is a qualifying nonprofit, begin OCPA compliance planning; enforcement against nonprofits commenced July 1, 2025.
