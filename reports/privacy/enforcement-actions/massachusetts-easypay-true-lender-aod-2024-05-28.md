# Massachusetts AG Forces EasyPay Finance from State in 'True Lender' / Rent-a-Bank Settlement

**Jurisdiction:** Massachusetts | **Category:** Privacy / Consumer Finance | **Date:** 2024-05-28

## Executive Summary [HIGH confidence]

On May 21, 2024, Massachusetts Attorney General Andrea Joy Campbell entered into an [Assurance of Discontinuance (AOD)](https://www.mass.gov/news/attorney-general-campbell-reaches-settlement-with-easypay-over-alleged-rent-a-bank-scheme-secures-restitution-and-ceases-lending-in-the-commonwealth) with EasyPay Finance, a California-based consumer installment lender. The AG alleged that EasyPay, acting through a partnership with Utah-chartered Transportation Alliance Bank (TAB Bank), was the "true lender" of loans it arranged for Massachusetts consumers — and that the partnership was a "rent-a-bank" scheme designed to circumvent the Commonwealth's 20% annual interest rate cap under [M.G.L. c. 271, § 49](https://malegislature.gov/Laws/GeneralLaws/PartIV/TitleI/Chapter271/Section49). EasyPay issued loans with an average APR exceeding 100% and charged rates as high as 189% — roughly five to nine times the state maximum. Under the settlement, EasyPay must pay $625,000 in restitution to harmed Massachusetts borrowers, request deletion of adverse credit-bureau tradelines for affected loans, cease all loan collection activity on existing loans in the state, and permanently exit the Massachusetts market. The action signals a maturing state-level enforcement posture on fintech-bank partnership lending, especially in the absence of a federal true lender standard following Congress's repeal of the OCC's 2020 True Lender Rule.

## Background [HIGH confidence]

### Massachusetts Interest Rate Limits and Usury Law

Massachusetts imposes one of the more restrictive usury regimes in the country. [M.G.L. c. 271, § 49](https://malegislature.gov/Laws/GeneralLaws/PartIV/TitleI/Chapter271/Section49) — the criminal usury statute — makes it a felony to knowingly charge interest and expenses exceeding 20% per annum on a loan, punishable by up to 10 years in state prison or a fine of up to $10,000, or both. Separately, [M.G.L. c. 140, § 96](https://www.mass.gov/info-details/mass-general-laws-c140-ss-96) regulates the "small loan business" and requires a license from the Commissioner of Banks for any person who makes loans of $6,000 or less at rates exceeding 12% per annum.

The Chapter 271 cap is subject to narrow exceptions, including for lenders regulated by state or federal banking regulators. This exception — which permits federally chartered and state-chartered banks to "export" their home state's interest rate under federal banking law — is the structural vulnerability that rent-a-bank arrangements exploit. By nominally partnering with a bank chartered in a state with no interest rate cap (such as Utah), a non-bank lender can argue that the bank's regulatory exemption shields the program from state usury limits.

### The "True Lender" Problem in Fintech-Bank Partnerships

The "true lender" doctrine is the legal framework courts and regulators use to determine whether a bank or its non-bank fintech partner is the actual lender in a loan transaction — and therefore which entity's regulatory charter governs the applicable interest rate. The question is substantive, not merely formal: who actually bears the credit risk, who funds the loan, who controls underwriting and servicing, and who retains the economic benefit of the lending program?

The doctrine became central to consumer finance regulation in the 2010s as marketplace and installment lenders proliferated. In 2020, the [OCC finalized a True Lender Rule](https://www.whitecase.com/insight-alert/occ-finalizes-true-lender-rule-attempting-end-uncertainty-around-bank-fintech) that would have created a simple bright-line test: if a national bank was named in the loan agreement or funded the loan at origination, the bank was the true lender. The rule was intended to provide certainty for fintech-bank partnerships. However, critics argued it would enable evasion of state usury laws at scale. Congress invoked the [Congressional Review Act to repeal the OCC rule on June 30, 2021](https://www.occ.gov/news-issuances/news-releases/2021/nr-occ-2021-69.html) — signed by President Biden — and the CRA's "no substantially similar rule" prohibition bars the OCC from reissuing the same standard, leaving a federal regulatory vacuum.

Following the OCC rule's repeal, enforcement authority effectively shifted to state attorneys general, who have employed common-law "predominant economic interest" tests, state consumer protection statutes, and usury laws to challenge partnerships they view as evasive.

### EasyPay Finance and TAB Bank

EasyPay Finance is a Vista, California-based consumer installment lender that markets point-of-sale financing for auto repair, veterinary, and other essential consumer services — often targeting borrowers with limited access to prime credit. EasyPay operated in Massachusetts through a partnership with [Transportation Alliance Bank (TAB Bank)](https://www.abc4.com/news/local-news/utah-bank-flagged-for-issuing-189-interest-loans-through-auto-repair-shops/), a Utah-chartered, FDIC-supervised industrial bank headquartered in Ogden, Utah. Utah has no statutory interest rate ceiling for consumer loans, allowing TAB Bank to nominally "export" rates that would otherwise be illegal in Massachusetts.

EasyPay had been operating in Massachusetts since at least 2018. Prior to the May 2024 AOD, EasyPay had also settled enforcement actions with [Colorado and the District of Columbia](https://www.nclc.org/resources/high-cost-rent-a-bank-loan-watch-list/) for similar rent-a-bank lending practices, and Iowa had required TAB Bank to cease facilitating EasyPay loans in that state.

## Detailed Analysis [HIGH confidence]

### The Attorney General's True Lender Theory

The core of AG Campbell's enforcement theory is that EasyPay — not TAB Bank — was the "true lender" of the loans originated in its name. The AG's office applied a "predominant economic interest" test, examining the economic substance of the EasyPay-TAB Bank relationship rather than the formal loan documentation. Four structural factors supported the AG's position:

1. **90% Participation Interest.** EasyPay held a 90% participation interest in each loan originated by TAB Bank. This meant EasyPay received the economic benefit of 90 cents on every dollar of interest collected — transferring nearly all loan revenue to the non-bank party.

2. **Risk of Non-Performance.** EasyPay bore substantially all credit risk. If a borrower defaulted, EasyPay — not TAB Bank — absorbed the loss. The bank's exposure to loan performance was minimal, further undermining any claim that TAB Bank was the genuine lender.

3. **Marketing, Customer Service, and Origination.** EasyPay controlled all customer-facing operations: it marketed the product to merchants and consumers, collected applications, handled customer service inquiries, and managed servicing on existing accounts. TAB Bank had no independent customer relationship.

4. **Underwriting Model.** EasyPay designed and operated the credit underwriting model. TAB Bank did not independently evaluate creditworthiness using its own criteria. The bank functionally approved whatever EasyPay's algorithm approved.

Under the predominant economic interest test articulated in [*Madden v. Midland Funding*](https://natlawreview.com/article/massachusetts-ag-forces-fintech-state-part-true-lender-settlement) and applied by various state regulators, this structure compelled the conclusion that EasyPay — not TAB Bank — was the de facto lender, stripping the transaction of TAB Bank's interest-rate export privilege.

### Alleged Violations

The AG's office alleged violations of two Massachusetts statutes:

- **[M.G.L. c. 271, § 49](https://malegislature.gov/Laws/GeneralLaws/PartIV/TitleI/Chapter271/Section49) (Criminal Usury).** By charging average APRs exceeding 100% and rates as high as 189% on Massachusetts consumers, EasyPay contracted for and received interest far beyond the 20% annual cap. From 2018 through at least 2022, EasyPay issued "numerous" Massachusetts loans uniformly exceeding the cap.

- **[M.G.L. c. 93A](https://law.justia.com/codes/massachusetts/part-i/title-xv/chapter-93a/) (Consumer Protection Act — Unfair and Deceptive Practices).** The AG alleged EasyPay's rent-a-bank scheme constituted an unfair and deceptive trade practice. Chapter 93A gives the AG broad enforcement authority and supports injunctive relief, restitution, and civil penalties. Critically, it does not require a showing of criminal intent — lowering the evidentiary burden compared to a criminal usury prosecution.

### Settlement Terms

The AOD — executed on May 21, 2024 — includes the following obligations:

- **Monetary Restitution: $625,000.** EasyPay must pay $625,000 in restitution to Massachusetts consumers who paid excessive interest. This represents partial restitution for finance charges above the legal rate, not full disgorgement.
- **Credit Bureau Remediation.** EasyPay must request deletion of any negative tradelines it (or TAB Bank) reported to consumer credit bureaus for affected Massachusetts loans. This addresses the secondary harm of damaged credit scores, which can have cascading effects on housing, employment, and subsequent borrowing costs.
- **Cessation of Collection Activity.** EasyPay must cease all collection efforts — including third-party collections — on existing Massachusetts loans.
- **Permanent Exit from Massachusetts.** EasyPay agreed to stop making loans in Massachusetts and, notably, agreed never to re-enter the Massachusetts market. The perpetual nature of this prohibition is unusual and reflects the AG's determination to achieve a structural remedy rather than a temporary pause.

### Broader Enforcement Context

The Massachusetts action is part of a national pattern of state-level resistance to high-cost rent-a-bank lending following the OCC True Lender Rule's repeal. The [National Consumer Law Center's High-Cost Rent-a-Bank Loan Watch List](https://www.nclc.org/resources/high-cost-rent-a-bank-loan-watch-list/) documents multiple ongoing state enforcement actions and consumer lawsuits against similar partnerships.

Several state legislatures have codified the true lender doctrine through statute. Connecticut enacted legislation in June 2023 establishing a "predominant economic interest" test as a matter of state law. Illinois, Maine, and New Mexico have enacted similar consumer credit laws that treat the non-bank fintech — not the originating bank — as the lender for state regulatory purposes. [Minnesota and Connecticut enacted analogous statutes in 2023](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/business_law/resources/business-law-today/2024-april/true-lender-and-rate-exportation-reviewing-the-major-2023-legislation/). Colorado's [DIDMCA opt-out (HB23-1229)](https://leg.colorado.gov/bills/hb23-1229) represents a related but distinct mechanism: rather than applying a true lender test, Colorado simply withdrew from federal interest rate preemption for state-chartered banks operating within its borders, directly curtailing rate exportation at its source.

The California Department of Financial Protection and Innovation (DFPI) took a different tack, pursuing true lender litigation against [OppFi (Opportunity Financial)](https://natlawreview.com/article/california-court-rejects-dfpis-true-lender-allegations-fintech-bank-partnership) in a case that a Los Angeles County Superior Court tentatively resolved in OppFi's favor in early 2026 — demonstrating that enforcement outcomes in this area are not uniform and depend heavily on the specific contractual structure of the fintech-bank partnership.

## Impact Assessment [HIGH confidence]

### Affected Industries and Business Models

The primary target of the Massachusetts AOD is the installment lending segment of the consumer fintech industry — specifically non-bank lenders that:
- Partner with out-of-state or federally chartered banks to originate consumer loans
- Charge APRs substantially exceeding the state usury cap (here, more than five times the 20% limit)
- Control underwriting, marketing, servicing, and bear the economic risk of the loan portfolio
- Operate in the "near-prime" or "subprime" consumer credit segment (auto repair, medical, veterinary, home improvement point-of-sale financing)

The settlement does not directly affect TAB Bank, which was not a named respondent. However, the action creates reputational and regulatory risk for bank partners in high-cost programs. The FDIC has separately faced calls from consumer advocacy groups to take supervisory action against TAB Bank in connection with its EasyPay program.

### Compliance Exposure for Fintech-Bank Partnerships

The Massachusetts action reinforces several compliance-critical principles for fintech lenders operating in states with interest rate caps:

- **Participation Interest Structure is Evidence of True Lender Status.** A 90% participation interest is a significant red flag for regulators applying an economic substance test. Partnerships in which the bank retains minimal economic interest post-origination are inherently vulnerable to true lender challenge.
- **Charter Exportation Does Not Guarantee Rate Exportation in All States.** Even where a federally chartered or out-of-state bank nominally issues the loan, state AGs in Massachusetts and other states will look through the formal structure to the economic reality.
- **Operational Control Matters.** Fintechs that handle underwriting, marketing, and customer service — while the bank merely licenses its charter — face a strong argument that they are the true lender. Partnerships in which the bank exercises genuine independent credit judgment are more defensible.
- **Multi-State Risk is Cumulative.** EasyPay's trajectory — from Colorado and D.C. to Iowa and Massachusetts — illustrates how enforcement by one state can invite investigation by others. A settlement in any one state can be used by other AGs as a roadmap.

### Consumer Impact

Between 2018 and 2022, Massachusetts consumers who borrowed from EasyPay through TAB Bank paid APRs that averaged over 100%, with rates reaching as high as 189%. These were consumers financing essential services (auto repair, veterinary care) who may have had limited credit alternatives. The $625,000 restitution fund provides partial relief but does not fully compensate for finance charges paid above the legal rate over the four-year period. The credit bureau deletion obligation is a meaningful ancillary benefit, as negative tradelines from high-cost lenders can suppress credit scores and increase future borrowing costs for affected consumers.

## Action Items

- **Audit fintech-bank partnership structures** to assess whether the economic substance of the arrangement — who bears credit risk, who holds participation interests, who controls underwriting and marketing — would withstand a "predominant economic interest" analysis in each state of operation.
- **Map state usury exposure**: identify the applicable interest rate cap in each operating jurisdiction and confirm that partner-bank charter-exportation arguments remain viable given current state law and AG enforcement posture.
- **Review participation interest percentages**: consider whether the percentage of loan economics retained by the bank is sufficient to support a genuine "bank as lender" characterization, particularly in Massachusetts, Connecticut, Illinois, Minnesota, and states with statutory true lender tests.
- **Monitor state legislative activity**: track enactment of true lender statutes and DIDMCA opt-out legislation in states where your program operates. Colorado, Iowa, and Puerto Rico have opted out of DIDMCA; more states may follow.
- **Assess credit bureau reporting practices**: ensure that any future wind-down or settlement in a state includes a plan to remediate consumer credit tradelines, consistent with the Massachusetts model.
- **Engage banking partner on joint liability exposure**: fintechs should understand whether their program agreements shift or share liability for state law violations and what the bank's own regulatory exposure (FDIC, OCC, state banking regulator) might mean for program continuity.
