# Wilson v. Ancestry.com: Ohio Federal Court Grants Summary Judgment on Right of Publicity and Invasion of Privacy Claims Over Yearbook Photos

**Jurisdiction:** Ohio (U.S. District Court, Southern District of Ohio) | **Category:** Privacy | **Date:** 2024-08-27 (reported November 2024)

## Executive Summary [MEDIUM confidence]

On August 27, 2024, U.S. District Judge Edmund A. Sargus of the Southern District of Ohio granted summary judgment to Ancestry.com LLC in [Wilson v. Ancestry.com LLC, No. 2:2022cv00861 (S.D. Ohio)](https://dockets.justia.com/docket/ohio/ohsdce/2:2022cv00861/265646), a putative class action alleging Ancestry violated Ohio's right of publicity statute (Ohio Rev. Code § 2741) and Ohio common law invasion of privacy by using plaintiffs' yearbook photographs in marketing materials without consent. The court ruled that uncontested evidence demonstrated plaintiff John Wilson's image had never been displayed in the challenged marketing materials except when plaintiff's own counsel triggered the search, negating both Article III standing and the statutory "commercial purpose" element. The ruling builds on a broader pattern of federal courts rejecting similar yearbook database claims on evidentiary and statutory grounds, with genealogy sites also prevailing under Section 230 immunity in other jurisdictions. Companies operating large digital image databases should treat the evidentiary threshold for "use" as a meaningful litigation risk, particularly as follow-on class actions continue to be filed in Ohio and other states.

## Background [MEDIUM confidence]

### Ohio Right of Publicity Law

Ohio's right of publicity statute, codified at [Ohio Revised Code Chapter 2741](https://codes.ohio.gov/ohio-revised-code/chapter-2741), provides a statutory cause of action for unauthorized commercial use of an individual's "persona." The statute defines "persona" as a person's name, voice, signature, photograph, image, likeness, or distinctive appearance, provided any of those aspects have commercial value. [Ohio Rev. Code § 2741.01](https://codes.ohio.gov/ohio-revised-code/section-2741.01) defines "commercial purpose" to encompass two distinct prongs: (1) use of an aspect of an individual's persona "for advertising or soliciting the purchase of products, merchandise, goods, services, or other commercial activities" not otherwise exempted; and (2) use "on or in connection with a place, a product, merchandise, goods, services or other commercial activities" not otherwise exempted. Yearbook database plaintiffs typically invoke both prongs — the advertising/solicitation prong targeting the promotional "Sign Up Now" display, and the on-connection-with-a-product prong targeting the subscription service itself.

[Ohio Rev. Code § 2741.02](https://codes.ohio.gov/ohio-revised-code/section-2741.02) prohibits any person from using any aspect of an individual's persona for a commercial purpose without prior written consent from the rights holder. A violation gives rise to civil liability for actual damages, including any profits derived from and attributable to the unauthorized use, plus injunctive relief and attorney's fees. In addition to the statutory claim, Ohio recognizes a common law tort for invasion of privacy by appropriation of name or likeness, which shares substantially the same elements as the statutory claim.

### Ancestry.com's Yearbook Database and Litigation History

Ancestry.com operates a subscription-based genealogical records platform that maintains an extensive collection of digitized high school and college yearbooks. When users search the platform for a particular person's name, the site displays yearbook records associated with that name, accompanied by promotional material inviting non-subscribers to "Sign Up Now" and touting higher-resolution photos and additional personal information available to paid members.

Beginning around 2020-2022, plaintiffs across multiple states filed putative class actions against Ancestry.com and similar genealogy sites on the theory that this practice constitutes unauthorized commercial exploitation of individuals' names and photographs in advertising. These cases have been litigated under the right of publicity statutes of Illinois, California, Indiana, and Ohio, as well as under common law theories. Outcomes have varied: some courts dismissed on Section 230 (Communications Decency Act) grounds, finding that Ancestry was displaying third-party content rather than creating its own; others dismissed on statutory standing or failure-to-state-a-claim grounds; and some allowed claims to survive motions to dismiss before failing at summary judgment.

The Wilson case was filed on February 21, 2022, in the Southern District of Ohio. Plaintiff John Wilson alleged that Ancestry used his name and yearbook photographs to promote paid subscriptions without his consent, asserting both statutory right of publicity claims under Ohio Rev. Code § 2741 and common law invasion of privacy claims. An earlier motion to dismiss was denied, with the court finding sufficient allegations of a commercial use and Article III standing at the pleadings stage.

## Detailed Analysis [MEDIUM confidence]

### The Court's Summary Judgment Ruling

On August 27, 2024, Judge Sargus granted Ancestry's motion for summary judgment and, as moot, denied Wilson's pending motion for class certification and Ancestry's motion to exclude plaintiff's expert. The court's ruling rested on two independently dispositive grounds: lack of Article III standing and failure to satisfy the "commercial purpose" element of the Ohio right of publicity statute.

**Standing.** At the pleadings stage, the court had accepted Wilson's allegation that Ancestry had displayed his photos to third-party users as part of a commercial pitch. At summary judgment, however, the evidentiary record told a different story. Ancestry produced uncontested evidence that Wilson's yearbook photographs had never been displayed to any ordinary user of the site. The only documented instance in which Wilson's images appeared was when plaintiff's own counsel ran a targeted search during the litigation — a search deliberately designed to call up Wilson's records. The court held that this one-time, counsel-initiated display was insufficient to establish a concrete, particularized injury traceable to Ancestry's conduct. Without evidence of actual display to a genuine third party seeking to "view records" related to Wilson, there was no cognizable harm under Article III.

**Commercial Purpose.** Even setting aside standing, the court found that the evidentiary record could not support the "commercial purpose" element under Ohio Rev. Code § 2741.02. Ancestry's system was structured so that a "pop-up" advertisement displaying a user's yearbook photograph would only appear when another user ran a search for that individual's name. Because Wilson had not produced evidence that any user — other than his own attorneys — had triggered such a search and viewed such an advertisement in connection with his photographs, Wilson could not demonstrate that Ancestry used his persona "on or in connection with" a commercial product or service within the meaning of the statute. The court declined to treat Ancestry's general advertising practices as sufficient without a showing that Wilson's specific photographs had been part of any actual commercial display.

**Relationship to Earlier Motion to Dismiss Denial.** The summary judgment ruling reversed the court's prior stance. When the case survived the motion to dismiss, the court had found Wilson's allegations sufficient to state a claim: that Ancestry's alleged use of class members' personas was "for the purpose of appropriating to the defendant's benefit the commercial or other values associated with the name or the likeness." The key difference at summary judgment was evidentiary: bare allegations that could survive Rule 12(b)(6) scrutiny could not substitute for actual proof of use. Ancestry's evidence that Wilson's specific photographs had not appeared in any marketing context — except when his counsel manufactured the scenario — was uncontested, and Wilson provided no contrary evidence.

### Section 230 as an Alternative Ground

Court commentary in the summary judgment proceedings also noted that even if Wilson had established standing and a commercial purpose, Ancestry would likely be immune from liability under Section 230 of the Communications Decency Act, 47 U.S.C. § 230. Ancestry sourced its yearbook photographs from third-party publishers and digitizers; it did not create the underlying content. Under prevailing interpretations of Section 230, interactive computer services are not liable as publishers or speakers for third-party-provided content. This alternative defense has been the primary or sole basis for dismissal in several parallel cases against Ancestry and similar platforms in other jurisdictions.

### Parallel Litigation Landscape

The Wilson ruling is one of several similar outcomes in the broader yearbook database litigation wave. In Illinois, one Northern District of Illinois action against Ancestry.com under the Illinois Right of Publicity Act (IRPA) — No. 1:2020cv07390 — proceeded through two successive plaintiff classes before being fully resolved, with each class dismissed on distinct grounds:

- [Bonilla v. Ancestry.com Operations, Inc.](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/district-courts/illinois/ilndce/1:2020cv07390/394102/57/), No. 1:2020cv07390 (N.D. Ill. 2021): The original lead plaintiff, Sergio Bonilla, was dismissed at summary judgment on **statute of limitations** grounds. The court held that Bonilla's IRPA claims were time-barred under the statute's one-year limitations period. Bonilla's subsequent attempt to invoke the Illinois Biometric Information Privacy Act's five-year limitations period was rejected on reconsideration — marking the first court to refuse applying BIPA's longer period to IRPA claims.

- [Braundmeier v. Ancestry.com Operations, Inc.](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/district-courts/illinois/ilndce/1:2020cv07390/394102/243/), No. 1:2020cv07390 (N.D. Ill. Jan. 2025) (substituted plaintiffs in the same action): After Bonilla was dismissed, Joshua Braundmeier and Kevin Wallace were substituted as the lead plaintiffs continuing the same docket. This second class was dismissed at summary judgment in January 2025 for **failure to establish commercial purpose** under the IRPA. As in Wilson, the court found that Ancestry demonstrated the only party who had ever accessed or viewed the plaintiffs' yearbook records was their own lawyers — not genuine third-party users — making it impossible to show Ancestry used their personas for advertising or solicitation within the IRPA's meaning.

A French genealogy site faced a separate Illinois federal action that was dismissed for lack of personal jurisdiction. In California, Section 230 has been the principal shield in cases alleging similar conduct under California's right of publicity statute. Indiana federal courts have also seen actions premised on that state's right of publicity law.

Notwithstanding these wins, Ancestry continued to face new class actions. In December 2024, a new putative class action was filed in Ohio federal court asserting violations of Ohio Rev. Code § 2741 and Ohio common law, suggesting that plaintiffs' counsel views the factual record deficiencies in Wilson as curable in future litigation.

## Impact Assessment [MEDIUM confidence]

### For Genealogy and Digital Archive Platforms

The Wilson decision confirms that the evidentiary gatekeeping function of the "commercial purpose" element is robust and can defeat class certification strategies that depend on the assumption that a website's mere possession of someone's image constitutes actionable use. Ancestry's critical strategic asset at summary judgment was granular logs establishing that Wilson's photographs had never been displayed to a genuine third-party user. Digital archive operators should audit their logging and data retention practices to ensure they can reconstruct, at the individual level, when and to whom specific records were displayed. Absence of such records could leave companies unable to disprove use at summary judgment.

### For Plaintiffs' Practitioners

The ruling underscores a key lesson from the yearbook litigation wave: individual named plaintiffs in right of publicity class actions face a demanding evidentiary burden to show their specific photographs were displayed in a commercial context. Selecting plaintiffs with documented third-party display events — rather than relying on the structural nature of the platform — will be essential to surviving summary judgment. The emergence of a December 2024 follow-on Ohio complaint suggests plaintiffs' counsel are developing this theory.

### Ongoing Multi-State Risk

Despite the summary judgment victory, Ancestry and similar platforms face continuing litigation risk in states with active right of publicity statutes, including Ohio (new complaint filed December 2024), Illinois, Indiana, and California. Each jurisdiction presents distinct statutory elements and defenses, and Section 230 immunity — while frequently available — is not universally accepted and is subject to ongoing congressional scrutiny.

### Implications for the Ohio Right of Publicity Statute

The decision clarifies an important limiting principle for Ohio Rev. Code § 2741: the statute requires evidence of actual, plaintiff-specific commercial use, not merely the structural potential for such use based on a platform's general business model. This holding may inform the litigation strategy in the December 2024 Ohio class action and any future cases brought under Chapter 2741.

## Action Items

- Genealogy and digital archive companies should audit their system logs to confirm they can document, at the individual photograph level, when records were actually displayed to third-party users versus internal or legal-team searches.
- Legal teams defending right of publicity class actions should obtain early, granular discovery on plaintiff-specific display events and move promptly for summary judgment if no third-party display can be documented.
- Compliance officers at companies operating subscription-based image databases should review marketing flows to ensure promotional displays are logged with sufficient granularity to reconstruct individual display events.
- Monitor the December 2024 Ohio class action and any related filings for developments that may signal plaintiffs' evolving theories for satisfying the commercial purpose and standing requirements post-Wilson.
- Track legislative activity in Ohio and other states for potential amendments to right of publicity statutes that could lower the evidentiary threshold or expand the definition of "commercial purpose."
