# X Corp. Petitions FTC to Close 2022 Settlement Order Stemming from Twitter Privacy Violations

**Jurisdiction:** Federal | **Category:** Privacy — Enforcement Actions | **Date:** June 4, 2026

## Executive Summary [HIGH confidence]

X Corp. (formerly Twitter) filed a petition with the Federal Trade Commission on May 15, 2026, requesting that the agency set aside or terminate the [2022 consent order](https://www.ftc.gov/enforcement/cases-proceedings/092-3093/twitter-inc-corporation) arising from Twitter's misuse of users' phone numbers and email addresses — collected ostensibly for two-factor authentication — to target advertising. The 2022 order imposed a $150 million civil penalty and extended privacy and data-security compliance obligations through 2042. X Corp. advances four arguments for early termination: corporate transformation since the acquisition, duplicative compliance burden, First Amendment considerations, and AI competitiveness. The [FTC announced on June 3, 2026](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2026/06/ftc-seeks-comment-x-corp-petition-set-aside-or-modify-ftc-order-concerning-twitter), that it will seek public comment through July 2, 2026, before the full Commission votes on the petition. The outcome carries significant precedential weight: if granted, X Corp. would become the first major technology company to successfully terminate an FTC privacy consent order before its natural expiration.

## Background [HIGH confidence]

### The 2009 Security Incidents and the 2011 FTC Consent Order

The FTC's oversight of Twitter traces to two security incidents in 2009, in which hackers obtained administrative access to Twitter's systems and were able to view private user information and post fraudulent tweets from prominent accounts. The FTC found that Twitter had engaged in deceptive acts and practices in violation of [Section 5(a) of the FTC Act](https://www.ftc.gov/legal-library/browse/federal-register-notices/twitter-inc-analysis-proposed-consent-order-aid-public-comment) by misrepresenting the extent to which it protected consumer data.

In March 2011, Twitter entered into its first consent order with the FTC — [FTC Docket No. C-4316](https://h2o.law.harvard.edu/text_blocks/5708) — which ran for 20 years (through approximately 2031). That order barred Twitter from making misrepresentations about its data security practices and required the company to establish a comprehensive information security program subject to biennial third-party audits.

### The 2022 Violations and Expanded Settlement

Despite the 2011 order, Twitter engaged in a systematic misuse of account security data. From at least 2013 through 2019, Twitter collected phone numbers and email addresses from users under the representation that this information would be used solely to enable security features such as two-factor authentication. Twitter then fed that data into its advertising targeting machinery, exposing the contact information of more than 140 million users to advertisers without disclosure or consent.

The FTC charged that this conduct violated both the FTC Act and the 2011 consent order. On May 25, 2022, the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California entered a [Stipulated Order for Civil Penalty, Monetary Judgment, and Injunctive Relief](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/2023062TwitterFiledMtnEntryOrder.pdf) imposing:

- A **$150 million civil penalty** — one of the largest privacy fines in FTC history
- An extended consent order running for **20 additional years through 2042**, superseding the 2011 order
- Expanded obligations including a comprehensive privacy and data-security program, independent biennial audits, and prohibitions on the use of security-purpose data for advertising

The agency published a [modified consent order in 2023](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/2023062C4316TwitterModifiedOrder.pdf) following Elon Musk's acquisition of Twitter and its rebranding as X Corp., continuing the oversight regime under the new corporate owner.

### Post-Acquisition Tensions

The Musk-era X Corp. chafed under the FTC order almost immediately. In 2023, X Corp. sought to challenge the order in court, and internal communications suggested the company viewed FTC oversight as intrusive. A [2023 House Judiciary Committee report](https://judiciary.house.gov/sites/evo-subsites/republicans-judiciary.house.gov/files/evo-media-document/Weaponization_Select_Subcommittee_Report_on_FTC_Harrassment_of_Twitter_3.7.2023.pdf) — produced by Republican committee members — alleged the FTC used the order to conduct disproportionate investigations into Twitter's internal operations. The current petition represents a formal administrative avenue after prior litigation efforts stalled.

## Detailed Analysis [HIGH confidence]

### X Corp.'s Four-Part Petition

The [formal petition](https://cdn.arstechnica.net/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/X-Corp-petition-to-set-aside-FTC-consent-decree-5-15-26.pdf), filed with the FTC on May 15, 2026, advances four distinct grounds for setting aside or modifying the 2022 order:

**1. Corporate Transformation ("New Entity" Argument)**

X Corp. contends that the order was imposed on a legal and institutional entity that "no longer exists." Following Musk's 2022 acquisition and subsequent reorganization, every individual allegedly responsible for the underlying privacy violations has departed. X Corp. argues it has since constructed a "world-class privacy and data-protection program" and that holding the current company to an order directed at its predecessor is inappropriate.

This argument faces a recognized legal hurdle: FTC consent orders bind entities and their successors, not named individuals. The FTC's standard view is that corporate transformation does not automatically extinguish regulatory obligations — particularly where the underlying data and user base remain continuous.

**2. Compliance Redundancy ("No Valid Purpose" Argument)**

X Corp. argues that the order's requirements duplicate obligations already imposed by domestic and international privacy frameworks and industry standards, generating millions of dollars in annual compliance costs without corresponding public benefit. The petition characterizes FTC order compliance as a layer of administrative burden that adds cost but no meaningful privacy protection beyond what X Corp. already performs.

Critics note that the 2022 order's requirements — including independent auditing and prohibitions on security-data repurposing — are precisely the kind of structural safeguards that ordinary regulatory regimes do not replicate with the same specificity or enforcement leverage.

**3. First Amendment Considerations**

X Corp. invokes First Amendment values as a ground for termination, arguing that the order's constraints on data use limit the platform's ability to curate and distribute content and engage in advertising. This argument is novel in the context of FTC consent order termination petitions and may face skepticism, as the order restricts deceptive commercial data practices rather than expressive speech.

**4. AI Competitiveness**

The petition asserts that maintaining the consent order impedes X Corp.'s ability to advance U.S. leadership in artificial intelligence, specifically through its integration with xAI's Grok chatbot. X Corp. suggests that FTC oversight of its data practices creates barriers to the data pipelines required for AI training. This argument surfaced in a politically salient context: the [FTC's earlier 2025 Section 6(b) investigation into AI companies](https://therecord.media/ftc-considers-modifying-150-million-twitter-privacy-fine) included X.AI Corp. among the subjects, and privacy advocates note that Grok uses public X posts for AI training by default for non-EU users.

### Legal Standard for Termination

The petition proceeds under [16 C.F.R. § 2.51](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/2.51), which governs requests to reopen and modify FTC orders. Under this regulation, a petitioner must make a "satisfactory showing that changed conditions of law or fact require the rule or order to be altered, modified, or set aside, in whole or in part, or that the public interest so requires." The regulation expressly warns that "[t]his requirement shall not be deemed satisfied if a request is merely conclusory or otherwise fails to set forth by affidavit(s) specific facts demonstrating in detail the nature of the changed conditions."

The FTC must determine whether to grant or deny the petition within 120 days after the close of the public comment period. Based on the July 2, 2026 comment deadline, the Commission's decision window runs through approximately early November 2026.

### FTC's Public Comment Process

The [FTC's June 3, 2026 press release](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2026/06/ftc-seeks-comment-x-corp-petition-set-aside-or-modify-ftc-order-concerning-twitter) announced a 30-day public comment period closing July 2, 2026. The full five-member Commission will vote on how to resolve the petition after reviewing public submissions. The current FTC composition under the Trump administration — which has generally favored deregulation — introduces meaningful uncertainty about the outcome.

## Impact Assessment [MEDIUM confidence]

### Precedential Significance

X Corp.'s petition is, by all accounts, unusual. If granted, X Corp. would be the first major technology company to obtain early termination of an FTC privacy consent order. FTC orders of this type are not routinely terminated early; the agency has historically viewed them as a primary enforcement tool for sustained behavioral modification. A favorable ruling would signal to other companies that post-acquisition corporate restructuring may provide a viable pathway to exit inherited compliance regimes.

### Grok and AI Data Practices

The AI competitiveness argument links the petition directly to ongoing concerns about Grok's training data pipeline. Privacy advocates emphasize that the FTC consent order represents the only federal-level structural oversight of X Corp.'s data handling practices for U.S. users. The European Union's GDPR provides an equivalent framework for EU users, but no analogous federal privacy statute applies to the general U.S. user base. If the order is terminated, X Corp. would face no mandatory independent privacy audit requirement and no order-specific prohibition on repurposing user data for AI training.

The [Office of the Privacy Commissioner of Canada](https://www.priv.gc.ca/en/opc-actions-and-decisions/investigations/investigations-into-businesses/2026/pipeda-2026-004/) initiated two complaints against X Corp. and X.AI LLC under PIPEDA in January 2026, following reports that Grok generated and publicly disclosed sexually explicit deepfakes of real individuals. This parallel regulatory pressure underscores the broader privacy governance gaps that critics argue make the FTC order more, not less, relevant.

### Compliance Implications for the Industry

The petition's outcome will be watched closely by:

- **Technology companies subject to legacy FTC consent orders** (including Meta, Google, and Amazon), which may assess whether the "new entity" or "changed conditions" arguments support their own petitions
- **Privacy compliance professionals** advising companies on FTC enforcement exposure and the durability of consent order obligations through M&A
- **AI developers** seeking clarity on whether FTC data-handling orders constrain AI training pipeline design

The Arnold & Porter analysis of the [2022 Twitter settlement](https://www.arnoldporter.com/en/perspectives/blogs/enforcement-edge/2022/06/ftc-reaches-settlement-with-twitter) noted that the order's prohibitions on using security-purpose data for advertising were a signal to the industry that the FTC would closely scrutinize the repurposing of data collected under one stated purpose for an unrelated commercial use — a principle that extends well beyond Twitter.

## Action Items

- **Monitor FTC docket through July 2, 2026**: The public comment period closes July 2, 2026. Organizations, trade associations, or privacy advocates wishing to submit comments to the FTC should do so via the FTC's official docket.
- **Watch for Commission vote (by ~November 2026)**: Under 16 C.F.R. § 2.51, the FTC must act within 120 days of the comment period closing. A Commission vote is expected by early November 2026. Track the outcome through the [FTC's enforcement cases page](https://www.ftc.gov/enforcement/cases-proceedings/092-3093/twitter-inc-corporation).
- **Assess own consent order exposure**: Companies currently operating under FTC consent orders should consult counsel regarding whether the standards articulated in X Corp.'s petition — corporate transformation, duplicative regulation, AI development — are factually applicable to their own circumstances. Do not assume the petition will succeed; the legal bar under § 2.51 is high.
- **Review AI training data governance**: If X Corp. succeeds, the removal of mandatory independent auditing and data-use restrictions could signal a shift in FTC appetite for structural AI-data oversight. Companies involved in AI model training using user-generated content should reassess whether their consent management and data-purpose limitation practices are defensible independent of any FTC order.
- **Track Grok/xAI regulatory developments**: The Canadian PIPEDA investigation and other international enforcement actions against X Corp. and X.AI may inform U.S. regulatory responses regardless of the FTC petition outcome.
