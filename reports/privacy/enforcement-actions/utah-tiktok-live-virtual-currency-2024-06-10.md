# Utah AG Alleges TikTok LIVE Facilitates Payments for Illegal Activity with Virtual Currency

**Jurisdiction:** Utah | **Category:** privacy | **Date:** 2024-06-10

## Executive Summary [HIGH confidence]

On June 3, 2024, the Utah Division of Consumer Protection filed a lawsuit against TikTok Inc. in Third District Court, alleging that TikTok violated the [Utah Consumer Sales Practices Act (Utah Code § 13-11-1 through -24)](https://le.utah.gov/xcode/Title13/Chapter11/13-11.html) by deliberately designing its TikTok LIVE livestreaming feature to couple virtual currency payments with illegal activity, including the sexual exploitation of minors, money laundering, drug sales, and terrorism financing. This is Utah's second lawsuit against TikTok, following a 2023 action over addictive design targeting children. The complaint contains grave allegations supported in part by TikTok's own internal investigations, known as "Project Meramec" and "Project Jupiter," which allegedly documented that TikTok knew hundreds of thousands of underage users were accessing the LIVE feature and being sexually exploited — yet continued operations to protect profits. As of February 2025, Third District Court Judge Coral Sanchez denied TikTok's motion to dismiss, allowing the case to proceed to trial, and rejecting TikTok's Section 230 and jurisdictional defenses. This lawsuit is part of a nationwide wave of state attorney general enforcement actions against TikTok related to child safety and consumer protection.

## Background [HIGH confidence]

### Utah's First TikTok Lawsuit (October 2023)

Utah was among the earliest states to take enforcement action against TikTok related to child harm. In October 2023, the State filed a consumer protection lawsuit under the Utah Consumer Sales Practices Act alleging that TikTok uses manipulative algorithms and addictive design features targeting minors. That case alleged that TikTok illegally baits children into addictive and unhealthy use, misrepresents the app's safety, and deceptively portrays itself as independent from its Chinese parent company ByteDance. Third District Court Judge Richard Daynes denied TikTok's motion to dismiss that lawsuit in [November 2024](https://blog.commerce.utah.gov/2024/11/12/news-release-utah-court-denies-tiktoks-motion-to-dismiss-in-landmark-childrens-protection-lawsuit/), allowing it to also proceed.

### TikTok LIVE Feature and Virtual Currency Mechanics

TikTok LIVE is a livestreaming feature that allows creators to broadcast video content in real time to followers. The feature is paired with a virtual currency system: viewers purchase "coins" with real money, which they can then use to send "gifts" to streamers during broadcasts. Streamers receive "diamonds" convertible to real-world cash. TikTok retains as much as 50% of each monetary transaction brokered through the platform. The minimum age to access TikTok LIVE is 18 under TikTok's own policies.

### Nationwide Enforcement Context

By 2024, state attorneys general across the country had initiated enforcement actions against TikTok. In October 2024, [14 attorneys general — led by New York and California — filed multistate lawsuits](https://ag.ny.gov/press-release/2024/attorney-general-james-sues-tiktok-harming-childrens-mental-health) against TikTok for allegedly harming children's mental health. A separate bipartisan coalition of [46 state AGs sought court enforcement to compel TikTok to comply](https://ago.mo.gov/attorney-general-bailey-joins-46-state-coalition-asking-court-to-order-tiktok-to-comply-with-investigation/) with subpoenas issued in an ongoing multistate consumer protection investigation.

## Detailed Analysis [HIGH confidence]

### Core Allegations

The [June 3, 2024 complaint](https://attorneygeneral.utah.gov/wp-content/uploads/2024/06/2024.06.03-Utah-TikTok-PUBLIC-Complaint.pdf) alleges that TikTok's LIVE feature, combined with its virtual currency system, created an environment in which:

1. **Child Sexual Exploitation**: Adult users pay young users to strip, pose provocatively, and perform sexual acts on camera in exchange for TikTok's virtual "diamonds" that can be cashed out for real money. Despite knowing its age restriction mechanisms were ineffective, TikTok permitted this conduct to continue.

2. **Financial Crimes**: TikTok's virtual currency system was used to launder money, operate illegal gambling rings, sell drugs, and fund terrorist organizations including the Islamic State of Iraq and Levant (ISIL), as documented in TikTok's internal "Project Jupiter" investigation.

3. **Dark Patterns**: TikTok deliberately employs manipulative design techniques — known as "dark patterns" — including an intentionally complex virtual currency system that obscures the real-dollar cost of coins, thereby deceiving consumers into spending more than they would if the true exchange value were transparent.

4. **Deceptive Age Restrictions**: TikTok represented to users and regulators that the LIVE feature was restricted to users aged 18 and older, while internally knowing its age-verification mechanisms were ineffective.

### Internal Investigation Evidence: Project Meramec and Project Jupiter

The complaint draws heavily on TikTok's own internal investigations. "Project Meramec" found that in January 2022 alone, [112,000 users between ages 13 and 15](https://www.stgeorgeutah.com/news/tiktoks-project-meramec-exposed-allegations-of-child-exploitation-detailed-in-utah-court-filing/article_41855562-c9df-11ef-acf6-3f5dda9ac720.html) accessed TikTok LIVE — a feature ostensibly restricted to adults. The investigation allegedly confirmed that children were being sexually exploited through the feature, and that TikTok chose not to shut down or sufficiently restrict LIVE because of the significant revenue generated.

"Project Jupiter," a second internal investigation, concluded that the LIVE feature and virtual currency enabled criminal financial activity. These internal documents became available to the public in January 2025 after a [court order required TikTok to release previously redacted complaint paragraphs](https://blog.commerce.utah.gov/2025/01/03/news-release-utah-division-of-consumer-protections-announces-release-of-previously-redacted-information-in-tiktok-inc-complaint-filing/). The Division of Consumer Protection characterized the newly revealed information as "shocking."

### Statutory Framework

The lawsuit is brought under [Utah Code §§ 13-11-1 through 13-11-24](https://le.utah.gov/xcode/Title13/Chapter11/13-11.html), Utah's Consumer Sales Practices Act (CSPA). The CSPA prohibits suppliers from engaging in deceptive or unconscionable acts or practices in connection with consumer transactions. Relevant provisions include:

- **§ 13-11-4**: Prohibits suppliers from making false representations about the characteristics, quality, or benefits of goods or services.
- **§ 13-11-5**: Prohibits unconscionable acts — a broad standard applied when a supplier takes advantage of consumers' inability to protect their own interests.

The state argues that TikTok is a "supplier" under the CSPA and that its virtual currency system constitutes a "consumer transaction" even though the app itself is free, because the coin-purchase system involves real money.

### TikTok's Defenses and the Motion to Dismiss Ruling

TikTok moved to dismiss on three grounds:

1. **Personal Jurisdiction**: TikTok argued Utah courts lacked jurisdiction because TikTok is a foreign-owned company (via ByteDance) with no physical presence in Utah.
2. **Section 230 Immunity**: TikTok argued it was protected by [Section 230 of the Communications Decency Act (47 U.S.C. § 230)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/47/230) as a publisher of third-party content.
3. **Free Service**: TikTok argued Utah's consumer protection laws do not apply because TikTok's app is provided free of charge.

In [February 2025, Judge Coral Sanchez rejected all three arguments](https://commerce.utah.gov/2025/02/21/utah-court-denies-tiktoks-motion-to-dismiss-in-the-states-second-lawsuit-regarding-the-live-feature/). On jurisdiction, the court held: "TikTok's presence in Utah is not that of a passive website that does little more than make information available ... TikTok purposefully directs its commercial activity toward residents of Utah and financially benefits from that activity." On Section 230, the court found that the state's claims do not seek to hold TikTok liable as a publisher of user-generated content — they target TikTok's own algorithmic design and the virtual currency system it designed and operates. On the free-service argument, the court found that TikTok's virtual currency revenue model constitutes consumer transactions within the meaning of the CSPA.

### Subpoena Contempt Proceedings

During pre-litigation investigation, TikTok repeatedly resisted state subpoenas. A Utah judge found TikTok in contempt of court for failing to produce documents required by the Division's investigative subpoenas, a development that significantly aided the state in ultimately obtaining internal investigation documents.

## Impact Assessment [MEDIUM confidence]

### Platform Liability and Section 230 Erosion

The February 2025 denial of TikTok's motion to dismiss is among a growing line of state court decisions rejecting Section 230 defenses where a platform's own design choices — rather than merely third-party content — are alleged to cause harm. The [Massachusetts Supreme Judicial Court reached a similar conclusion in April 2026](https://blog.commerce.utah.gov/2025/02/21/utah-court-denies-tiktoks-motion-to-dismiss-in-the-states-second-lawsuit-regarding-the-live-feature/) regarding Meta's Instagram. This trajectory signals that courts are increasingly willing to distinguish between "platform as publisher" (Section 230-protected) and "platform as product designer" (not protected).

### Virtual Currency Ecosystem Risk

The lawsuit's theory — that virtual currency systems embedded in social media platforms constitute regulated consumer transactions subject to consumer protection statutes — has broad implications for the $26 billion in-app purchase market. If Utah's legal theory is sustained at trial, platforms that deploy virtual currency to monetize user-generated content could face consumer protection liability in every state where they operate. This is especially significant given the proliferation of similar "gifting" and "tipping" mechanics across platforms including Twitch, YouTube Live, and Instagram Live.

### Child Safety Regulatory Convergence

This action is consistent with a broader national convergence of child safety enforcement and legislation. Alongside multistate AG lawsuits, Congress has advanced the [KOSA (Kids Online Safety Act)](https://www.congress.gov/bill/118th-congress/senate-bill/1409) and the [COPPA 2.0](https://www.congress.gov/bill/118th-congress/senate-bill/1418) proposals. Utah itself enacted the [Social Media Regulation Act (Utah Code § 13-63)](https://le.utah.gov/xcode/Title13/Chapter63/C13-63_2023043.pdf) in 2023, though that law faces constitutional challenges. The TikTok LIVE lawsuit uses existing consumer protection authority as an alternative enforcement pathway where dedicated social media legislation remains legally uncertain or absent.

### Affected Organizations

- **Social media platforms with virtual currency or gifting features** — must audit whether their monetization mechanics could constitute deceptive consumer transactions under applicable state CSPA-equivalent statutes.
- **Technology companies with livestreaming features** — face heightened scrutiny of age-verification systems and the adequacy of internal safety investigations.
- **Compliance officers** — need to assess whether internal safety investigations (analogous to "Project Meramec") create discoverable litigation risk if the company elects not to act on findings.

## Action Items

- **Audit virtual currency and in-app gifting mechanics** for deceptive design features ("dark patterns"), particularly any obscuring of real-dollar exchange values — review against state consumer protection statutes in jurisdictions where the platform operates.
- **Review age-gating procedures** for any livestreaming, gifting, or monetization features restricted to adults; assess whether documented failures in age verification create exposure under state consumer protection or child protection laws.
- **Assess internal investigation governance**: Any internal study or audit documenting known harms (analogous to Project Meramec) that is not acted upon may constitute a key litigation document; evaluate privilege protections and whether findings trigger mandatory compliance action.
- **Track this litigation**: No trial date is yet set; monitor for discovery rulings, any appeal of the motion-to-dismiss denial, and any settlement discussions. The parallel 2023 Utah lawsuit (also past a motion to dismiss) suggests TikTok will litigate vigorously.
- **Monitor multistate enforcement**: The 46-state AG coalition and separate 14-state lawsuits indicate escalating multi-jurisdictional pressure; compliance strategies should anticipate coordinated demands across jurisdictions.
