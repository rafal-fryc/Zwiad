# CPPA's First Enforcement Advisory: Data Minimization Applies to Consumer Request Processing

**Jurisdiction:** California | **Category:** Privacy | **Date:** April 2, 2024

## Executive Summary [HIGH confidence]

On April 2, 2024, the California Privacy Protection Agency's (CPPA) Enforcement Division issued [Enforcement Advisory No. 2024-01](https://cppa.ca.gov/pdf/enfadvisory202401.pdf) — the agency's first-ever enforcement advisory — titled "Applying Data Minimization to Consumer Requests." The advisory warns that certain businesses are improperly requiring consumers to provide excessive and unnecessary personal information when submitting California Consumer Privacy Act (CCPA) privacy rights requests. The CPPA characterizes data minimization as a "foundational principle" of the CCPA and states it applies not only to ordinary business operations but to the mechanics of handling consumer rights requests themselves, including opt-out and deletion requests. The advisory provides two illustrative scenarios and a three-question analytical framework to guide businesses. Deputy Director of Enforcement Michael S. Macko made clear the advisory is a prompt for voluntary compliance, but warned: "We won't hesitate to act when necessary."

## Background [HIGH confidence]

### The California Privacy Protection Agency and Its Enforcement Division

The CPPA was created by California voters through Proposition 24, the California Privacy Rights Act of 2020 (CPRA), which amended and significantly expanded the CCPA. The CPPA assumed primary regulatory and enforcement authority over the CCPA from the California Attorney General's office. The agency formally came into existence in 2021, with CPRA amendments operative as of January 1, 2023.

The CPPA's Enforcement Division is a relatively young unit. According to [CPPA Deputy Director of Enforcement Michael S. Macko](https://cppa.ca.gov/meetings/materials/20240716_item6_enforcement_update_and_priorities.pdf), the division built itself from the ground up following the CPRA's effective date. As of mid-2024, the division had grown from a single staff member to approximately a dozen positions and was still recruiting additional subject-matter experts in technology. Macko brings nearly two decades of litigation and investigative experience from the private sector, the U.S. Department of Justice, and the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission.

The CPPA's enforcement powers are broad. In addition to handling consumer complaints and initiating investigations, the agency can conduct audits, issue civil fines of up to $2,500 per unintentional violation and $7,500 per intentional violation, and seek injunctive relief. The agency had already signaled an active enforcement posture in 2024: a California appellate court ruled in February 2024 that the CPPA could [immediately begin enforcing CPRA regulations](https://www.foley.com/insights/publications/2024/02/ca-appellate-court-privacy-agency-enforce-cpra-regulations/) notwithstanding a prior delay injunction.

### The Legal Basis: Data Minimization Under CCPA/CPRA

The data minimization requirement is codified at [California Civil Code § 1798.100(c)](https://law.justia.com/codes/california/code-civ/division-3/part-4/title-1-81-5/section-1798-100/), which provides:

> "A business' collection, use, retention, and sharing of a consumer's personal information shall be reasonably necessary and proportionate to achieve the purposes for which the personal information was collected or processed, or for another disclosed purpose that is compatible with the context in which the personal information was collected, and not further processed in a manner that is incompatible with those purposes."

This provision was added by the CPRA and became operative January 1, 2023. It introduced a statutory data minimization obligation that had no direct analog in the original CCPA.

The implementing regulations under [11 CCR § 7002(c)-(d)](https://cppa.ca.gov/regulations/) elaborate on the analysis. Whether a business's collection, use, retention, or sharing of personal information is "reasonably necessary and proportionate" must be assessed by reference to three dimensions: (1) the minimum personal information necessary to achieve the identified purpose; (2) the possible negative impacts to consumers posed by the collection or processing; and (3) the existence of additional safeguards for the personal information.

The advisory is the Enforcement Division's first public guidance interpreting how these requirements apply in the specific context of consumer privacy rights requests — an operational context that businesses often treat as distinct from ordinary data collection practices. The CPPA is making clear those boundaries do not exist.

## Detailed Analysis [HIGH confidence]

### The Trigger: Observed Over-Collection in Consumer Request Processing

The [advisory](https://cppa.ca.gov/pdf/enfadvisory202401.pdf) states that the Enforcement Division issued the guidance because it had observed "certain businesses are asking consumers to provide excessive and unnecessary personal information in response to requests that consumers make under the California Consumer Privacy Act." The advisory does not name specific businesses or describe pending enforcement actions, but the framing — issuing a formal advisory rather than simply opening investigations — suggests the CPPA viewed the problem as industry-wide rather than isolated.

### The Three-Question Framework

The advisory distills the data minimization analysis into three questions that businesses must answer when deciding what information to collect from a consumer in connection with a CCPA request:

1. **What is the minimum personal information that is necessary to achieve this purpose** (i.e., completing the request or verifying identity)?
2. **Do we need to ask for more personal information than we already have?** Businesses should use data they already hold before requesting additional information.
3. **What are the possible negative impacts posed if we collect or use the personal information in this manner?** Disproportionate collection creates risks to consumers even in an administrative context.

This framework, drawn from 11 CCR § 7002(c)-(d), applies equally to the opt-out context and the identity-verification context, but with different practical implications in each.

### Scenario 1: Opt-Out Requests for Sale or Sharing of Personal Information [HIGH confidence]

The advisory's first scenario addresses businesses that receive a consumer request to opt out of the sale or sharing of personal information — including requests submitted via a Global Privacy Control (GPC) signal.

Key principles:
- **Identity verification is not required for opt-out requests.** The CCPA regulations expressly prohibit requiring a consumer to verify their identity in order to exercise an opt-out right. A business may ask for information "necessary to complete the request," but only if the process is not burdensome.
- **Scope must match business practices.** If a business sells or shares consumer data only in the context of online behavioral advertising, it generally does not need a name or email address to honor a GPC opt-out signal — the signal alone suffices. Requiring a driver's license photograph or ID upload in this context would be "excessive and unnecessary."
- **More complex profiles may justify limited additional information.** If a business sells or shares profiles that include both online activity and offline data (such as purchasing history), it may need the consumer to identify themselves to ensure the opt-out is applied across all data categories. But the additional information collected must be narrowly tailored to that need.
- **Driver's license generally prohibited for opt-outs.** The advisory gives a specific example: if a business sells or shares purchase history, requesting unrelated personal information such as a driver's license photograph would likely exceed the "minimum personal information" standard.

### Scenario 2: Identity Verification for Deletion Requests [HIGH confidence]

The second scenario addresses the identity verification step required for deletion requests (and other CCPA rights where identity verification is permitted). Unlike opt-out requests, identity verification is sometimes appropriate — but the process must itself comply with data minimization.

Key principles drawn from the advisory and [11 CCR § 7002](https://cppa.ca.gov/regulations/):
- **Use information you already have first.** Before requesting new information from the consumer, the business should assess what it already holds and whether that is sufficient to verify identity to the required degree of certainty.
- **Match verification rigor to sensitivity of the request.** The degree of certainty needed to verify identity should be proportionate to the sensitivity of the information to be deleted and the risk of unauthorized deletion. Deleting a name and email address requires less certainty than deleting financial account information.
- **Sensitive identifiers require heightened justification.** Businesses should, whenever possible, avoid requesting Social Security numbers, driver's license numbers, financial account numbers, or biometric information for purposes of identity verification.
- **The biometric ID photo example.** The advisory specifically flags the practice of requiring consumers to submit a photograph of themselves holding a government-issued ID. If the consumer is requesting deletion of browsing history, this requirement is likely "disproportionate and excessive" under the standard.
- **Non-account holders.** For consumers who do not have an account with the business, the business must establish a reasonable verification method that does not include collecting information disproportionate to and excessive relative to the information the business already holds about the consumer.

### What the Advisory Does and Does Not Do [MEDIUM confidence]

Privacy practitioners have noted that the advisory, while meaningful as the CPPA's first formal public guidance, provides relatively limited specificity. [Taft Privacy & Data Security Insights observed](https://www.privacyanddatasecurityinsight.com/2024/04/california-privacy-protection-agency-issues-minimal-guidance-on-ccpa-in-first-enforcement-advisory/) that the two scenarios are illustrative rather than exhaustive, and the "reasonably necessary and proportionate" standard requires fact-specific analysis that the advisory does not fully resolve.

The advisory is not a regulation — it does not have the force of law and does not amend the CCPA or CPRA regulations. However, as official guidance from the agency with enforcement authority, it is highly relevant to assessing whether a business's verification practices constitute a violation, and it would likely be cited in any future enforcement action involving these fact patterns.

## Impact Assessment [HIGH confidence]

### Who Is Affected

The advisory applies to any business subject to the CCPA that handles consumer privacy rights requests — which includes most businesses operating in California above the applicable threshold (gross annual revenue exceeding $25 million, buying/selling personal data of 100,000 or more consumers, or deriving 50% or more of revenue from selling personal information). The advisory is particularly relevant to:

- **Data brokers and advertising technology companies**, which frequently require identity verification to process opt-out requests and maintain consumer profiles that span multiple data categories.
- **Retailers and e-commerce platforms**, which combine online behavioral data with transactional data and may require consumers to create accounts or provide additional information to process deletion requests.
- **Any business that has deployed ID verification workflows** (e.g., requiring government ID photos) for consumer requests.
- **Businesses processing opt-out preference signals** (GPC), where the advisory makes clear that additional information collection is generally not appropriate.

### Compliance Requirements

Based on the advisory and underlying regulations, businesses should:

1. **Audit their consumer request intake forms** to identify any fields that collect personal information beyond what is strictly necessary for the type of request being made.
2. **Separate opt-out and deletion workflows.** The information required to complete an opt-out is categorically different from (and generally less than) what may be appropriate for identity verification in a deletion request.
3. **Document the data minimization rationale** for each data field collected during consumer request processing. The three-question framework from the advisory provides a ready-made documentation template.
4. **Eliminate government ID photograph requirements** unless the business can affirmatively justify them as proportionate to the sensitivity of the information and risk of unauthorized action.
5. **Review GPC/opt-out signal processing** to ensure no additional personal information is being requested as a condition of honoring the signal.

### Enforcement Signals

The advisory is significant beyond its substantive content. As the CPPA's inaugural enforcement advisory, it signals the agency's preferred enforcement methodology: public guidance to prompt voluntary compliance, followed by formal action for those who do not correct course. Macko's statement — "we intend for our Enforcement Advisories to promote voluntary compliance, but sometimes stronger medicine will be in order" — is a direct warning that the advisory is a threshold notice, not an endpoint.

The CPPA followed the first advisory with a [second enforcement advisory on dark patterns](https://cppa.ca.gov/announcements/2024/20240904.html) (Enforcement Advisory No. 2024-02) issued on September 4, 2024. This pattern confirms the agency intends to build a library of public guidance across multiple compliance domains before escalating to formal enforcement in those areas.

## Action Items

- **Immediately audit consumer request intake workflows** for each type of CCPA request (access, deletion, opt-out of sale/sharing, correction, portability) and identify any data fields that are not strictly necessary to fulfill the request type.
- **Remove government ID photograph requirements from opt-out workflows** without a documented, proportionate justification tied to the specific data categories involved.
- **Test GPC/opt-out preference signal processing** to verify no additional consumer data is being collected or required to complete the opt-out.
- **Update identity verification procedures for deletion requests** to prioritize matching against existing data before requesting any new personal information from the consumer.
- **Develop written documentation for each verification step** answering the CPPA's three-question framework (minimum necessary; what do we already have; negative impacts).
- **Train customer service staff** handling privacy requests on the data minimization principle and what questions they are and are not permitted to ask.
- **Monitor for future CPPA enforcement advisories** as the agency signals it will continue to issue guidance before escalating to formal action in each compliance area.
