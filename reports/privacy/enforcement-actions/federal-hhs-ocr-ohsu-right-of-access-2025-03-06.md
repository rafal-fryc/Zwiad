# HHS OCR Imposes $200,000 Civil Monetary Penalty on Oregon Health & Science University for HIPAA Right of Access Violations

**Jurisdiction:** Federal, Oregon | **Category:** Privacy | **Date:** 2025-03-06

## Executive Summary [HIGH confidence]

On March 6, 2025, the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services (HHS) [Office for Civil Rights (OCR) announced](https://www.hhs.gov/press-room/penalty-against-or-health-science-university.html) a $200,000 civil monetary penalty (CMP) against Oregon Health & Science University (OHSU), a public academic health center and research university in Portland, Oregon, for violating the HIPAA Privacy Rule's individual right of access. OCR's investigation found that OHSU failed to provide a patient's personal representative with complete access to medical records for approximately 16 months following the initial April 2019 request, in violation of [45 C.F.R. § 164.524](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-45/subtitle-A/subchapter-C/part-164/subpart-E/section-164.524). The case marks OCR's 53rd enforcement action under the Right of Access Initiative, under which OCR announced the initiative in spring 2019 (with the first enforcement action against Bayfront Health, $85,000, announced September 9, 2019), and was finalized under the Biden Administration in December 2024 before being publicly announced in March 2025. A particularly significant aspect of this case is OCR's explicit rejection of the argument that covered entities can delegate right-of-access obligations to business associates and escape liability when those business associates fail to fulfill requests on time. Health systems, hospitals, and academic medical centers that rely on third-party medical records vendors should treat this case as a direct warning.

## Background [HIGH confidence]

### The HIPAA Right of Access Rule

The HIPAA Privacy Rule codifies the individual right of access at [45 C.F.R. § 164.524](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-45/subtitle-A/subchapter-C/part-164/subpart-E/section-164.524). Under this provision, covered entities — which include health care providers, health plans, and health care clearinghouses — must act on a valid access request no later than 30 calendar days after its receipt. If the covered entity cannot fulfill the request within 30 days, a single 30-day extension is available, but only if the covered entity provides the requesting individual with written notice of the reasons for the delay and the anticipated completion date.

The right extends to personal representatives, who under 45 C.F.R. § 164.502(g) stand in the shoes of the patient for purposes of exercising rights under the Privacy Rule. A personal representative with legal authority to act on a patient's behalf — such as a parent for a minor child or a legal guardian — has the same right of access as the patient and must receive the same timely response.

HHS has supplemented the regulatory text with [comprehensive guidance on individual access rights](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/privacy/guidance/access/index.html), emphasizing that the 30-day requirement is computed in calendar days, that covered entities must provide records in the electronic form and format requested if readily producible, and that permissible fees are strictly capped at labor and supply costs (or a $6.50 flat-rate safe harbor for electronic delivery). No fee may be charged when PHI is transmitted to a third-party application at the individual's direction.

### OCR's Right of Access Initiative

OCR announced the Right of Access Initiative in spring 2019 during the first term of the Trump Administration in response to a pattern of noncompliance with timely access requirements. The first enforcement action under the initiative — against Bayfront Health St. Petersburg ($85,000) — was announced September 9, 2019. The initiative was designed to send a clear market signal that OCR would investigate and resolve complaints in this area systematically rather than selectively. By 2019, the agency had received thousands of right-of-access complaints and determined that enforcement actions — not merely technical assistance — were necessary to shift covered entity behavior.

From its launch through the OHSU announcement in March 2025, the initiative had produced 53 enforcement actions. Enforcement has been spread across covered entity types: solo physician practices, dental offices, hospitals, academic medical centers, and specialty clinics have all been subject to penalties. The initiative continued without interruption across administrations. Cumulative penalties under the initiative have exceeded $2 million across the first 50 cases, with the pace of enforcement accelerating in 2023 and 2024, as OCR resolved 13 enforcement actions in 2023 alone, totaling approximately $4.18 million. The 54th enforcement action — against [Concentra, Inc.](https://www.hhs.gov/press-room/ocr-settles-with-concentra.html) for $112,500 — was announced shortly after the OHSU penalty.

The OHSU case is notable within the initiative's history because it involves a large academic medical center rather than a small or solo practice, and because it explicitly addresses the business associate delegation issue — a fact pattern that has broad relevance for large health systems that commonly outsource records management functions.

## Detailed Analysis [HIGH confidence]

### Facts of the OHSU Case

OHSU is a public academic health center and research university in Portland, Oregon. It operates Oregon's only academic medical center and serves as the state's only teaching hospital for the professions of medicine, dentistry, and pharmacy.

The case originated from a records request submitted in **April 2019** by the personal representative of a patient. OHSU provided a partial response in April 2019 but failed to produce the complete set of requested records at that time. The personal representative subsequently filed a complaint with OCR in **May 2020**. In **September 2020**, OCR contacted OHSU regarding potential noncompliance with the Privacy Rule's right-of-access provisions.

Despite this intervention, OHSU still did not provide all requested records. A **second complaint** was filed against OHSU in **January 2021** by the same personal representative. OHSU did not furnish the complete requested records until **August 2021** — approximately 16 months after the initial request and nearly a year after receiving OCR's September 2020 communication.

### Administrative Timeline

The enforcement timeline unfolded over several years:

- **April 2019**: Personal representative submits records request; OHSU provides partial response.
- **May 2020**: First OCR complaint filed.
- **September 2020**: OCR notifies OHSU of potential Privacy Rule noncompliance.
- **January 2021**: Second OCR complaint filed by the same personal representative.
- **August 2021**: OHSU provides the remaining requested records — approximately 16 months after the original request.
- **September 2024**: OCR issues [Notice of Proposed Determination](https://www.hhs.gov/sites/default/files/oregon-health-science-university-npd.pdf) seeking a $200,000 CMP. OHSU does not contest the determination.
- **December 2024**: OCR issues [Notice of Final Determination](https://www.hhs.gov/sites/default/files/oregon-health-science-university-nfd.pdf), rendering the $200,000 CMP final. (Finalized in the last weeks of the Biden Administration.)
- **March 6, 2025**: OCR publicly announces the $200,000 CMP.

The decision by OHSU not to contest the Notice of Proposed Determination avoided administrative litigation but also meant no corrective action plan was negotiated. In the Right of Access Initiative, covered entities that settle typically agree to corrective action plans; those against whom a CMP is uncontested or litigated to conclusion do not have the benefit of a negotiated compliance roadmap.

### The Business Associate Issue

A central and significant aspect of the OHSU case — highlighted in OCR's [Notice of Proposed Determination](https://www.hhs.gov/sites/default/files/oregon-health-science-university-npd.pdf) — is the role of a business associate in processing records requests. OHSU had contracted with a third-party vendor (a business associate) to handle the processing and fulfillment of patient records requests. OHSU relied upon this business associate arrangement as part of its records response workflow.

OCR's determination makes clear that this delegation does not relieve a covered entity of its obligations under the Privacy Rule. Under [45 C.F.R. § 164.524](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-45/subtitle-A/subchapter-C/part-164/subpart-E/section-164.524), the duty to provide timely access rests with the covered entity. A covered entity cannot satisfy a right-of-access request by directing the individual to a third-party vendor and then treating the matter as outside its control if that vendor fails to timely respond.

[HHS guidance on business associates](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/faq/business-associates/index.html) establishes that business associates can have direct liability for certain Privacy Rule violations under the HITECH Act, including failure to disclose PHI to a covered entity or to an individual as specified in a business associate agreement. However, this direct liability does not displace the covered entity's own obligation. OCR's position, confirmed in the OHSU enforcement action, is that a covered entity remains responsible for ensuring timely fulfillment of access requests regardless of whether it has delegated the operational function to a business associate.

This principle has material compliance implications for large health systems. Academic medical centers, hospital networks, and multi-site providers frequently use third-party release-of-information (ROI) vendors to centralize and process records requests. If those vendors experience backlogs, system failures, or workflow deficiencies, the covered entity — not merely the vendor — bears enforcement risk.

### Penalty Amount and Rationale

OCR sought and imposed a $200,000 CMP. The [penalty tier structure](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/compliance-enforcement/index.html) under the HIPAA Enforcement Rule (45 C.F.R. Part 160) ranges from a minimum of $141 per violation for unknowing violations (the 2024 inflation-adjusted rate applicable at the time OCR issued the September 2024 NPD; HIPAA CMPs are subject to annual inflation adjustment under the Federal Civil Penalties Inflation Adjustment Act) to up to $2,134,831 per violation category per calendar year for willful neglect not corrected (the 2024 inflation-adjusted annual cap effective August 8, 2024, applicable at the time of the September 2024 NPD; this figure adjusts annually for inflation). OCR did not publicly disclose which culpability tier it applied in the OHSU case, but the $200,000 amount is consistent with prior academic medical center enforcement and reflects both the multi-year delay and OHSU's failure to resolve the matter even after OCR's September 2020 intervention.

For context, the $200,000 CMP in the OHSU case is toward the higher end of Right of Access enforcement penalties, which have ranged from $3,500 to $240,000 across the initiative's 53 cases. The penalty reflects the severity of the delay (16 months), the existence of prior OCR contact, and the filing of a second complaint before records were finally produced.

## Impact Assessment [HIGH confidence]

### Affected Entities

This enforcement action is directly relevant to:

- **Academic medical centers and teaching hospitals**: OHSU is the first academic medical center penalized under the Right of Access Initiative at this penalty level in the 53rd action. The action signals that OCR targets institutions of all sizes, not only small practices.
- **Large health systems using third-party ROI vendors**: The business associate finding is the most operationally significant aspect of the case for large organizations. Health systems that delegate records fulfillment to third-party ROI vendors (e.g., Ciox Health, IOD, MRO Corp, HealthMark Group) must ensure contractual and operational controls guarantee timely fulfillment — and must monitor vendor performance rather than assume contract terms are sufficient.
- **Health plans and clearinghouses**: The Right of Access Initiative covers all covered entity types. Health plans that rely on TPAs or administrative vendors for records management face the same delegation risk.
- **Providers treating patients with personal representatives**: Any patient population that frequently includes individuals accessing records through legal guardians, parents, healthcare proxies, or other authorized representatives creates higher-volume access request workflows that must be tracked and fulfilled within the 30-day window.

### Compliance Requirements

The regulatory baseline under [45 C.F.R. § 164.524](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-45/subtitle-A/subchapter-C/part-164/subpart-E/section-164.524) is non-negotiable:

1. **30-day fulfillment**: All valid access requests must be fulfilled — or formally denied — within 30 calendar days of receipt. The clock runs from the date the request is received, not the date it is routed to a vendor.
2. **One permissible extension**: A single 30-day extension requires written notice provided to the requester before the original 30 days expire, stating reasons for the delay and the projected completion date.
3. **Personal representative parity**: Requests from personal representatives carry the same legal weight and timeline requirements as requests from the patient directly. Systems must be designed to recognize and process personal representative requests without additional friction or delay.
4. **Business associate oversight**: Covered entities must implement written policies, contractual provisions, and operational monitoring to ensure that any business associate responsible for fulfilling access requests does so within applicable timeframes. A covered entity cannot satisfy its obligation by asserting that the vendor did not perform.
5. **No fee conditioning**: Records cannot be withheld pending payment of unauthorized fees. Permissible fees are limited to labor, supplies, and postage, or the $6.50 flat-rate safe harbor for electronic delivery.

### Enforcement Outlook

The Right of Access Initiative has now persisted across three administrations — Trump I, Biden, and Trump II (with the OHSU announcement coming early in Trump II). The current HHS leadership has not indicated any intent to wind down the initiative. OCR's 54th enforcement action under the initiative — against Concentra, Inc. — was announced in 2025, demonstrating continuity.

With 54 cases closed and enforcement penalties reaching the $200,000 range for large institutions, OCR has sufficient precedent to impose higher penalties on covered entities that exhibit willful neglect or that fail to act even after receiving OCR technical assistance or a Notice of Proposed Determination. The OHSU case, in which records were not produced until 16 months after the initial request and nearly a year after OCR's own intervention, illustrates the kind of egregious fact pattern that supports penalties at or above this level.

## Action Items

- Audit all active business associate agreements with third-party ROI vendors to confirm they include express requirements to fulfill access requests within 30 calendar days of receipt — not 30 days from the date the vendor receives the request from an internal queue.
- Implement performance monitoring for ROI vendor fulfillment rates and timelines; do not rely solely on vendor self-reporting. Establish contractual remedies for vendor non-performance, including indemnification provisions for OCR penalties arising from vendor delay.
- Train patient access and registration staff on personal representative identification and routing procedures. Requests from personal representatives must be triaged and tracked with the same urgency as direct patient requests.
- Establish a centralized access request tracking system with automated escalation triggers at Day 20 (10 days before the hard deadline) and Day 25 (5 days before the hard deadline), enabling supervisors to intervene before a violation occurs.
- Review OCR's [Notice of Proposed Determination for OHSU](https://www.hhs.gov/sites/default/files/oregon-health-science-university-npd.pdf) and [Notice of Final Determination](https://www.hhs.gov/sites/default/files/oregon-health-science-university-nfd.pdf) to understand the specific factual findings that drove OCR's penalty rationale, and assess whether analogous fact patterns exist in your organization's records fulfillment operations.
- Monitor OCR's [Resolution Agreements and CMPs index](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/compliance-enforcement/agreements/index.html) for additional Right of Access enforcement actions as the initiative continues under the current administration.
- Respond promptly and cooperatively to any OCR technical assistance letter or data request. The OHSU case demonstrates that failure to remedy known noncompliance after OCR contact is a significant aggravating factor in CMP determinations.
