# Federal Court Upholds Massachusetts Chapter 93A Claim for Medical Data Disclosure Omissions in A.J. v. LMND Medical Group, Inc.

**Jurisdiction:** California (Federal — N.D. Cal.) | **Category:** Privacy | **Date:** 2024-11-04

## Executive Summary [MEDIUM confidence]

On October 25, 2024, the United States District Court for the Northern District of California issued a significant ruling in a putative nationwide class action involving a healthcare company's alleged sale of patient data to pharmaceutical companies. The court denied a motion to dismiss the plaintiffs' claim under the Massachusetts Consumer Protection Act, [M.G.L. c. 93A](https://malegislature.gov/Laws/GeneralLaws/PartI/TitleXV/Chapter93A), when that claim was grounded in a theory of material omission — specifically the company's failure to disclose that it was collecting and sharing patients' sensitive health information with third parties. Critically, the court dismissed a parallel 93A claim premised on affirmative misrepresentation, finding the complaint lacked sufficient factual allegations that any plaintiff had actually seen the defendant's privacy policy representations. The decision extends a growing line of federal case law allowing state consumer-protection omission theories to support privacy class actions even in the absence of a private right of action under HIPAA, and it underscores the litigation exposure that healthcare companies face when their data-sharing practices are not conspicuously disclosed.

## Background [HIGH confidence]

### The Rise of Healthcare Data Class Action Litigation

Between 2022 and 2024, plaintiffs' attorneys filed hundreds of class actions against healthcare providers, telehealth companies, and digital health platforms for deploying third-party tracking technologies — including Meta Pixel, Google Analytics, and session-replay tools — on patient-facing websites. These technologies capture user interactions such as pages visited, form inputs, and symptom selections, and transmit bundles of data to advertising platforms in real time. Unlike traditional HIPAA-covered data sharing, these transfers often occur without individualized notice or consent, raising questions about whether they constitute unauthorized disclosures of protected health information.

A parallel category of litigation involves digital health and telehealth companies that monetize patient data more directly — by selling or licensing de-identified or pseudonymized data sets to pharmaceutical companies, health insurers, or health data aggregators. Plaintiffs in these cases typically allege that the defendant's privacy policies were silent or misleading about such secondary uses of patient data, and that a reasonable consumer would not have shared sensitive health information had they known of those practices.

### Massachusetts Chapter 93A as a Privacy Litigation Tool

[Massachusetts General Laws Chapter 93A, § 2](https://malegislature.gov/Laws/GeneralLaws/PartI/TitleXV/Chapter93A/Section2) declares unlawful "unfair methods of competition and unfair or deceptive acts or practices in the conduct of any trade or commerce." Chapter 93A is notable for several characteristics that make it attractive in data privacy litigation:

- **Broad reach**: Unlike many state privacy statutes, 93A does not require physical presence in Massachusetts. Courts have applied it to companies transacting business with Massachusetts residents, even when the company is headquartered elsewhere.
- **Omission liability**: Massachusetts courts have long held that a material omission — the failure to disclose facts that a reasonable consumer would consider important — can constitute an "unfair or deceptive act or practice" under 93A even without an affirmative misrepresentation.
- **Multiple damages and fees**: Section 9 permits courts to award double or treble actual damages where the defendant's conduct was "willful or knowing," plus mandatory attorneys' fees for successful plaintiffs.
- **Class action viability**: Unlike HIPAA, 93A carries a private right of action, making it a usable class action predicate in the federal courts.

The statute's flexibility has made it a preferred tool for privacy plaintiffs in Massachusetts and in federal courts applying Massachusetts law. For background on 93A's application in healthcare tracking cases, see the District of Massachusetts's prior ruling in [*Jane Doe v. Tenet Healthcare Corporation* (Civil Action 23-12978-PBS, D. Mass. Apr. 23, 2024)](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/us-dis-crt-d-mas/116091358.html), which denied dismissal of a Chapter 93A claim arising from a hospital's deployment of website tracking pixels to disclose patient data to third-party advertisers.

### LMND Medical Group and the Underlying Complaint

[LMND Medical Group, Inc.](https://dockets.justia.com/docket/california/candce/4:2023cv03288/414928) (case No. 4:2023cv03288, N.D. Cal.) is a San Francisco-based healthcare provider associated with the Lemonaid Health telehealth platform. The case caption in the published opinion was anonymized to *A.J. v. LMND Medical Group, Inc.* to protect the plaintiff's identity. Plaintiffs filed a putative nationwide class action alleging that the company surreptitiously collected patients' confidential health information through website trackers and disclosed that information to third parties — including, according to reports, pharmaceutical companies — without adequate disclosure to patients. Plaintiffs asserted that they would not have shared their health information with the defendant had they been aware of these practices.

The complaint advanced Massachusetts Chapter 93A claims on two alternative theories:
1. **Affirmative misrepresentation**: that the defendant's privacy policies contained false statements about how patient data would be used.
2. **Material omission**: that the defendant failed to disclose its data-sharing practices, which a reasonable consumer would have considered material to their decision to use the platform.

## Detailed Analysis [MEDIUM confidence]

### The Court's Ruling on the Omission Claim

The court declined to dismiss the Chapter 93A omission claim. Applying the Massachusetts standard for omission-based 93A liability, the court found that plaintiffs had adequately alleged:

1. **A failure to disclose**: The complaint plausibly alleged that the defendant did not inform patients that their health data — including information about medical conditions and pharmaceutical needs — was being shared with third parties for advertising or commercial purposes.

2. **Materiality**: The omitted facts were "likely to have influenced a reasonable consumer's" decision to use the platform and share sensitive health information. Courts applying 93A's omission standard look to whether the non-disclosed fact would matter to an objectively reasonable consumer, not merely to a particularly sensitive plaintiff.

3. **Injury causally linked to the omission**: Plaintiffs alleged that as a result of the undisclosed data sharing, they received targeted pharmaceutical advertisements that revealed to third parties (family members, colleagues who might view their screens or social media feeds) that they had particular medical conditions. The court found this adequately alleged mental distress and the fear that their private health information had been exposed to their social circles — a cognizable injury sufficient to survive a motion to dismiss.

The court's reasoning on injury is notable: it accepted the inference that targeted pharma ads, visible to others, can cause reputational harm and emotional distress arising specifically from the non-disclosed data-sharing. This is consistent with the growing judicial recognition — in both HIPAA-adjacent cases and general consumer privacy actions — that dignitary and emotional harms from privacy violations can constitute Article III standing and state-law damages.

### Dismissal of the Affirmative Misrepresentation Claim

By contrast, the court dismissed the 93A claim based on affirmative misrepresentation. The problem was evidentiary specificity: the Third Amended Complaint failed to allege sufficient facts from which the court could infer that any named plaintiff had actually read or been exposed to the specific misrepresentations in the defendant's privacy policies. Under 93A's misrepresentation theory, a plaintiff must generally plead that the deceptive statement reached and affected them. Absent those factual allegations, the misrepresentation claim could not survive. This distinction — between omission claims (no need to prove exposure to a specific statement) and affirmative misrepresentation claims (exposure is part of the causal chain) — has significant implications for how privacy class actions are pleaded.

### Nationwide Class Action Dynamics

The case was filed as a putative nationwide class action despite involving Massachusetts law. This reflects plaintiffs' counsel's strategy to use favorable state consumer-protection statutes on behalf of a nationwide class — a practice that has become common in privacy litigation but that courts scrutinize carefully at the class certification stage. While the October 25, 2024, ruling addressed only the pleading-stage motion to dismiss, class certification remains a substantial hurdle. Courts applying 93A in nationwide class actions must confront choice-of-law questions about whether Massachusetts law can apply to the claims of non-Massachusetts class members. A December 2024 ruling from the District of Massachusetts, discussed in a companion analysis by the same blog ([Chapter 93A Application Limited in Nationwide Class Actions](https://www.masschapter93ainsights.com/2024/12/chapter-93a-application-limited-in-nationwide-class-actions-district-of-massachusetts/)), suggests that courts may limit 93A's reach in subsequent proceedings.

### Relationship to HHS OCR Tracking Technology Guidance

The ruling comes against the backdrop of a significant regulatory dispute over HHS OCR's 2022 guidance declaring that HIPAA-covered entities cannot use certain tracking technologies without patient authorization. A federal court vacated portions of that guidance in June 2024, creating a gap in federal enforcement. In that environment, state law theories — including Chapter 93A omission claims — have become more important as a mechanism for plaintiffs to pursue healthcare data privacy violations. The survival of the omission claim in *A.J. v. LMND Medical Group, Inc.* is consistent with that broader trend.

## Impact Assessment [MEDIUM confidence]

### Healthcare and Telehealth Companies

The ruling is most directly relevant to telehealth platforms, digital health apps, and other healthcare companies that:
- Operate websites with tracking technologies that capture patient-identifiable data
- License, sell, or share patient data with pharmaceutical companies or data aggregators
- Have not disclosed these practices conspicuously in patient-facing privacy notices

For these companies, the decision demonstrates that even if HIPAA does not provide a private right of action, state consumer-protection laws — and specifically 93A's omission theory — can provide the plaintiffs' bar with a viable path to federal court. A motion to dismiss is no longer a reliable exit from these cases.

### Pharmaceutical Companies and Data Purchasers

Pharmaceutical companies and health data aggregators that purchase or receive patient data from healthcare platforms also face indirect exposure: they may become named defendants in future amended complaints or related actions once plaintiffs have survived an initial motion to dismiss and begin discovery into data-sharing agreements.

### Scope of the 93A Omission Standard

Compliance officers and outside counsel should note that the 93A omission standard is plaintiff-friendly at the pleading stage: it does not require a named plaintiff to demonstrate that they saw a specific misstatement, only that a material fact was withheld that would have influenced a reasonable consumer. This means that detailed, accurate privacy policies that are buried in terms of service — but not highlighted in the onboarding flow — may be insufficient to defeat a 93A omission claim. The practical implication is that healthcare companies may need to provide prominent, just-in-time disclosures about data sharing at the point of patient data collection.

### Multi-State Class Action Risk

The case illustrates the risk that a single state's consumer-protection statute can be deployed as a vehicle for nationwide class actions. Healthcare companies with national patient bases should audit their data-sharing agreements and privacy disclosures with an eye to Massachusetts 93A — even if they are headquartered outside Massachusetts — because any Massachusetts-resident patients who use the platform are potential class members under a 93A theory.

## Action Items

- Audit data-sharing agreements with pharmaceutical companies, data brokers, health analytics vendors, and advertising platforms. Confirm that the purpose and recipient of each data transfer is described in patient-facing privacy notices.
- Review privacy notice adequacy: a disclosure buried in a lengthy terms-of-service document that patients must scroll to find may not defeat a 93A omission claim. Consider prominent pop-up or inline disclosures at the point of data collection.
- Assess tracking technology deployments on patient-facing web properties. Map each pixel, tag, or analytics tool to the data it captures and the downstream recipients.
- Evaluate the Massachusetts population of your user or patient base. If Massachusetts residents are among your patients, 93A omission liability is available to the plaintiffs' bar regardless of your company's state of incorporation.
- Monitor class certification proceedings in this case. Nationwide class actions based on a single state's consumer-protection law face serious choice-of-law challenges at certification; track how the court resolves those issues, as they will shape the template for future cases.
- Coordinate with litigation counsel to evaluate whether your privacy policy adequately defeated the affirmative misrepresentation theory, and whether the omission theory still presents exposure.
