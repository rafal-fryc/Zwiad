# California AG Launches CCPA Investigative Sweep Targeting Location Data Industry

**Jurisdiction:** California | **Category:** privacy | **Date:** 2025-03-13

## Executive Summary [HIGH confidence]

On March 10, 2025, California Attorney General Rob Bonta announced an ongoing investigative sweep targeting the location data industry under the California Consumer Privacy Act (CCPA), as amended by the California Privacy Rights Act (CPRA). The sweep involves formal investigation letters sent to mobile app providers, advertising technology companies, and data brokers that collect, sell, or share consumers' precise geolocation data. The AG's sweep focuses specifically on two consumer rights established by the CPRA: the right to opt out of the sale or sharing of personal information, and the right to limit the use and disclosure of sensitive personal information — a category that expressly includes precise geolocation. This is the latest in a series of CCPA investigative sweeps that AG Bonta has conducted since taking office, following prior sweeps targeting mobile apps (2023), streaming services (January 2024), and employers (2023). The sweep does not identify specific companies by name but signals that enforcement actions, and potentially civil penalties up to $7,500 per intentional violation, are forthcoming for non-compliant location data businesses.

## Background [HIGH confidence]

### The CCPA/CPRA Framework for Location Data

California's consumer privacy framework traces to the California Consumer Privacy Act of 2018 ([Cal. Civ. Code §§ 1798.100 et seq.](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displayText.xhtml?division=3.&part=4.&lawCode=CIV&title=1.81.5)), significantly expanded by Proposition 24, the California Privacy Rights Act of 2020. The CPRA amendments became operative on January 1, 2023, and added two consumer rights directly relevant to the sweep:

1. **Right to Opt Out of Sale or Sharing (§ 1798.120):** Consumers have the right to direct businesses to stop selling or sharing their personal information. Businesses must provide a "Do Not Sell or Share My Personal Information" link and must honor opt-out requests submitted through the browser-based [Global Privacy Control (GPC)](https://oag.ca.gov/privacy/ccpa/gpc) signal.

2. **Right to Limit Use and Disclosure of Sensitive Personal Information (§ 1798.121):** The CPRA created a new right allowing consumers to restrict businesses to using sensitive personal information only for limited purposes — providing the requested service, safety, and a few other enumerated uses. [Cal. Civ. Code § 1798.140(ae)](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?sectionNum=1798.140.&lawCode=CIV) defines "sensitive personal information" to include "precise geolocation," which the statute defines as data derived from a device used to locate a consumer within a geographic area with a radius of 1,850 feet or less.

The [California Attorney General retains independent enforcement authority](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?sectionNum=1798.155.&lawCode=CIV) under Cal. Civ. Code § 1798.155 and may bring civil actions seeking penalties of up to $2,500 per unintentional violation and $7,500 per intentional violation or violation involving the personal information of consumers under 16 years of age. Notably, the CPRA eliminated the mandatory 30-day cure period that the original CCPA provided, giving the AG discretion over whether to provide notice before commencing legal action.

### History of AG Bonta's CCPA Investigative Sweeps

This sweep is part of a documented pattern of AG enforcement activity. Prior AG sweeps under the CCPA include:

- **August 2022:** Settlement with Sephora ($1.2 million) for failure to disclose sale of personal information and failure to honor GPC opt-out requests — the first CCPA enforcement action to result in a settlement ([Sephora settlement](https://oag.ca.gov/news/press-releases/attorney-general-bonta-announces-settlement-sephora-first-major-enforcement-action)).
- **2023:** Investigative sweep of popular mobile applications in the retail, travel, and food service industries for failing to offer CCPA opt-out mechanisms.
- **2023:** Sweep of California employers regarding compliance with employee privacy rights under the CCPA.
- **January 26, 2024:** [Investigative sweep of streaming services](https://oag.ca.gov/news/press-releases/attorney-general-bonta-announces-investigative-sweep-focuses-streaming-services%E2%80%99) for compliance with consumers' right to opt out of data sales, which produced a [$530,000 settlement with Sling TV](https://oag.ca.gov/news/press-releases/attorney-general-bonta-secures-530000-settlement-sling-tv-first-enforcement) in October 2025 — nearly two years after the sweep was announced, illustrating the typical lag between investigative letters and enforcement outcomes.

The AG's investigative sweep methodology involves sending formal notice letters that: (1) notify recipients of a potential CCPA violation; and (2) request additional information about the recipient's business practices. Receipt of such a letter does not automatically result in an enforcement action but typically triggers a cure-and-respond window and substantially increases the risk of civil litigation if violations are not addressed.

### Why Location Data Now

The AG's announcement cited several reasons why location data warrants focused enforcement attention. [AG Bonta stated](https://oag.ca.gov/news/press-releases/attorney-general-bonta-announces-investigative-sweep-location-data-industry) that location data "can let anyone know if you visit a health clinic or hospital, and can identify your everyday habits and movements." The announcement also noted heightened sensitivity of location data in the current political environment: surveillance of reproductive and gender-affirming healthcare seekers and tracking of immigrant communities represent real risks that granular location data enables. This connects the enforcement action to broader policy debates about the use of commercial location data for law enforcement and immigration enforcement purposes, a topic that has grown significantly since 2023.

## Detailed Analysis [HIGH confidence]

### Scope of the Sweep

The [official AG press release](https://oag.ca.gov/news/press-releases/attorney-general-bonta-announces-investigative-sweep-location-data-industry) identifies the sweep as targeting three categories of business:

1. **Mobile app providers** that collect consumers' precise geolocation data and sell or share it with advertising networks and data brokers;
2. **Advertising networks** that receive geolocation data from mobile apps and further sell or share it with other third parties; and
3. **Data brokers** that buy geolocation data from app providers or advertising networks and further disseminate it.

The sweep focuses on the chain of data collection and monetization, not merely on the initial data collector. This multi-party scope is significant: it signals the AG intends to hold downstream recipients of location data — not just the original collecting apps — responsible for CCPA compliance failures at each step of the data supply chain.

### Legal Theories Targeted

The sweep's focus on "how covered businesses offer and effectuate" opt-out rights and the right to limit sensitive personal information points to several specific compliance failures:

**Opt-Out Compliance Failures (§ 1798.120):**
- Failure to provide a conspicuous "Do Not Sell or Share My Personal Information" link or button in mobile apps;
- Failure to honor GPC browser signals as valid opt-out requests;
- Failure to honor authorized agent opt-out requests;
- Using deceptive or overly burdensome processes that functionally prevent opt-out (so-called "dark patterns");
- Re-engaging in sale or sharing before the mandatory 12-month waiting period for re-authorization expires.

**Sensitive Personal Information Limitations (§ 1798.121):**
- Continuing to use or disclose precise geolocation for purposes beyond providing the requested service after a consumer has invoked the right to limit;
- Failure to provide a "Limit the Use of My Sensitive Personal Information" notice or link;
- Processing geolocation data for ad targeting, profiling, or sale after a limiting request.

**Privacy Notice Deficiencies (§ 1798.130):**
- Failure to disclose in the privacy policy that precise geolocation is sold or shared;
- Failure to identify the categories of third parties with whom geolocation data is shared.

[Multiple law firm analyses](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-cybersecurity-law-blog/california-ag-announces-new-ccpa-enforcement-sweep-targeting-location-data-industry) note that the sweep is explicitly grounded in CPRA provisions (effective January 1, 2023), not merely the original CCPA, meaning the full weight of the post-2023 amendment requirements — including the right to limit sensitive personal information — is implicated.

### Distinction from CPPA Enforcement

The California Privacy Protection Agency (CPPA), which assumed primary CCPA rulemaking and enforcement authority from the AG under the CPRA, separately conducts its own enforcement actions. The [March 2025 enforcement environment](https://www.jenner.com/en/news-insights/client-alerts/latest-developments-in-california-privacy-enforcement-in-march-2025--investigative-sweep-and-cppa-enforcement-action) features both the AG's location data sweep (announced March 10) and a concurrent CPPA enforcement action — underscoring that the location data industry faces dual enforcement pressure from two separate California authorities simultaneously.

### Relationship to Related Legislative Developments

The sweep occurred contemporaneously with [California Assembly Bill 1355](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/billTextClient.xhtml?bill_id=202520260AB1355), the California Location Privacy Act, introduced February 21, 2025. AB 1355 would go significantly further than the CCPA/CPRA by prohibiting covered entities from collecting or processing location information unless necessary to provide requested goods or services — a data minimization standard for location data that would function as an affirmative use restriction, not merely an opt-out right. Under AB 1355, "location information" covers GPS data, IP addresses, cell-site information, and data from automated license plate recognition systems and traffic monitoring systems (including speed safety systems), where the information identifies street-level location within a range of five miles or less. The AG's sweep signals that existing law is being actively enforced while the legislature considers whether additional protections are needed.

## Impact Assessment [MEDIUM confidence]

### Affected Industries

The sweep directly implicates the following industry segments:

- **Mobile application developers** that use precise location (e.g., navigation, weather, retail, food service, fitness, health, dating apps) and monetize location data through advertising SDKs or data sales;
- **Advertising technology (adtech) platforms** that ingest mobile location signals for programmatic advertising, audience segmentation, and behavioral targeting;
- **Data brokers** that aggregate and resell location data, whether for advertising, insurance underwriting, financial services, or other commercial purposes;
- **Analytics firms** that provide footfall measurement, proximity marketing, and location intelligence services using commercially sourced location data.

The sweep arrives against the backdrop of the [Texas AG's landmark TDPSA enforcement action against Allstate and Arity](https://www.texasattorneygeneral.gov/news/releases/attorney-general-ken-paxton-sues-allstate-and-arity-unlawfully-collecting-using-and-selling-over-45) — filed January 13, 2025 — which specifically targets SDK-based precise geolocation collection. The convergence of California and Texas enforcement actions targeting location data in the same quarter signals cross-jurisdictional coordination and a broad regulatory consensus that SDK-embedded location collection without adequate consent mechanisms is a priority enforcement target.

### Compliance Requirements

For businesses that receive AG investigative letters, immediate obligations include:

- Responding to the information request within the stated deadline with documentation of current privacy practices;
- Implementing a remediation plan for any identified deficiencies;
- Preserving all relevant documents and data related to location collection, sharing, and opt-out processing.

For businesses in the location data ecosystem that have not received letters but operate in the identified categories, the sweep is a strong signal to conduct a self-assessment covering:

- Whether a "Limit the Use of My Sensitive Personal Information" mechanism is provided for precise geolocation collection;
- Whether opt-out requests via GPC and authorized agents are being honored promptly and correctly;
- Whether the 12-month restriction on re-soliciting consent after an opt-out has lapsed is being respected;
- Whether downstream data sharing agreements contractually require CCPA compliance from recipients of location data.

### Penalty Exposure

CCPA civil penalties are assessed per violation. At $7,500 per intentional violation, companies with large consumer datasets can face substantial aggregate liability. The prior Google location privacy settlement — [$93 million](https://oag.ca.gov/news/press-releases/attorney-general-bonta-announces-93-million-settlement-regarding-google%E2%80%99s-location%E2%80%99s) — demonstrates that purely location-focused enforcement can yield nine-figure outcomes.

#### Subsequent Enforcement Outcomes (Postdating This Sweep)

The following settlements were reached after the March 10, 2025 sweep announcement and illustrate the trajectory of AG enforcement under this same legal framework:

- **July 2025:** The AG announced a [$1.55 million settlement with Healthline Media](https://oag.ca.gov/news/press-releases/attorney-general-bonta-announces-largest-ccpa-settlement-date-secures-155) for CCPA opt-out violations — at the time the largest CCPA settlement in California history.
- **February 11, 2026:** The AG announced a [$2.75 million settlement with Disney](https://oag.ca.gov/news/press-releases/california-wont-let-it-go-attorney-general-bonta-announces-275-million) for failure to honor opt-out requests across multiple streaming services — now the largest CCPA settlement in California history as of early 2026. The Disney settlement is directly relevant to location data enforcement: it demonstrates the AG's willingness to pursue major consumer-facing brands and to aggregate per-violation penalties into multi-million-dollar outcomes.

Together, these subsequent outcomes confirm that the March 2025 location data sweep is not merely investigative posturing. The AG has demonstrated it will convert investigative sweeps into significant enforcement settlements.

## Action Items

- **If you received an AG investigative letter:** Engage outside privacy counsel immediately. Do not respond without counsel. Preserve all relevant records. Assess the gap between current practices and the CCPA's opt-out and sensitive-data-limiting requirements.
- **Mobile app developers:** Audit all advertising and analytics SDKs embedded in your apps. Confirm each SDK's data sharing practices, and verify that you have contractual assurances that SDK providers are CCPA-compliant recipients.
- **Adtech platforms and data brokers:** Review downstream contracts to confirm you have a basis to receive geolocation data and that your own processing (including resale) is disclosed to consumers and subject to opt-out compliance.
- **All location data businesses:** Implement or verify the "Limit the Use of My Sensitive Personal Information" mechanism if any sensitive data (including precise geolocation) is processed beyond the purposes expressly permitted by § 1798.121.
- **Monitor AB 1355:** If enacted, the California Location Privacy Act would require affirmative justification for any location data collection — a far stricter standard than the current opt-out model. Businesses should assess compliance gaps against AB 1355 requirements now, before enactment.
- **Check GPC compliance:** The Global Privacy Control must be honored as a valid opt-out signal. Verify that your mobile and web infrastructure correctly processes and propagates GPC signals to downstream partners within the required timeframe.
