# Court Permits Arkansas Attorney General's Lawsuit Against TikTok to Proceed

**Jurisdiction:** Arkansas | **Category:** privacy | **Date:** 2024-06-04

## Executive Summary [MEDIUM confidence]

The Circuit Court of Cleburne County, Arkansas, denied TikTok and its parent company ByteDance's motion to dismiss a consumer-protection lawsuit brought by Arkansas Attorney General Tim Griffin under the Arkansas Deceptive Trade Practices Act (ADTPA), Ark. Code Ann. §§ 4-88-101 et seq. Circuit Judge Holly L. Meyer rejected TikTok's jurisdictional challenge, finding that TikTok's substantial commercial activity targeting Arkansas residents — particularly minors — established personal jurisdiction in the state. The court also rejected TikTok's argument that its platform falls outside the ADTPA's coverage, holding that the state plausibly alleged unconscionable business practices. The ruling, issued in May 2024 after February 2024 oral arguments, allows the AG's March 2023 complaint to move forward on the merits. Arkansas was one of the earliest states to bring this type of enforcement action against TikTok, and the case has since attracted scrutiny at the Arkansas Supreme Court level after TikTok attempted an interlocutory appeal. Trial is scheduled for September 2025.

## Background [HIGH confidence]

### The Arkansas ADTPA Framework

The Arkansas Deceptive Trade Practices Act, codified at [Ark. Code Ann. Title 4, Chapter 88](https://law.justia.com/codes/arkansas/title-4/subtitle-7/chapter-88/), is Arkansas's primary consumer-protection statute. The Act prohibits "deceptive and unconscionable trade practices" and grants the Attorney General authority to investigate and prosecute violations. Civil penalties of up to $10,000 per violation are available, and the AG may seek injunctive relief. Key substantive provisions include [§ 4-88-107](https://law.justia.com/codes/arkansas/title-4/subtitle-7/chapter-88/subchapter-1/section-4-88-107/), which enumerates deceptive and unconscionable trade practices, including misrepresentations about the safety or quality of goods and services, and exploiting vulnerable consumers.

In 2023, Arkansas also amended the ADTPA to add [Subchapter 14 — Regulation of Social Media](https://law.justia.com/codes/arkansas/title-4/subtitle-7/chapter-88/subchapter-14/section-4-88-1402/) (§§ 4-88-1401 through 4-88-1404), which requires social media companies to use third-party age verification before allowing minors to create accounts, and mandates express parental consent for users under 18. This Subchapter was enacted as part of S.B. 396 (the "Social Media Safety Act," signed April 12, 2023), though the Social Media Safety Act itself was subsequently blocked by a federal court on First Amendment grounds. The ADTPA deceptive-practices claims in the TikTok lawsuit are legally distinct from the Social Media Safety Act and remain operative.

### Filing of the Lawsuit — March 2023

On March 28, 2023, Governor Sarah Huckabee Sanders and AG Tim Griffin jointly [announced the filing of separate lawsuits against Meta and TikTok](https://arkansasag.gov/news-release/governor-sanders-and-attorney-general-griffin-announce-filing-of-lawsuits-against-meta-tiktok/) in the Cleburne County Circuit Court. Arkansas was at that time one of the very few states to initiate affirmative ADTPA enforcement against a major social media platform on behalf of minors.

The complaint alleged that TikTok and ByteDance knowingly misled the public about the nature of TikTok's content environment and its safety for teenage users. Specifically, the AG alleged that TikTok marketed itself as appropriate for teen audiences while making easily accessible a substantial volume of content involving mature themes, nudity, drugs, and other material harmful to minors. The complaint further alleged that TikTok exploited the addictive design features of its algorithm to keep minors engaged despite the platform's known psychological harms, while misrepresenting to parents and guardians that parental controls and content restrictions were effective.

### Multistate Context

While Arkansas was among the first movers, the legal theory it advanced — holding a social media platform liable under state deceptive-practices law for misrepresentations about safety and addictiveness — proved influential nationally. By October 2024, [fourteen additional state attorneys general had filed coordinated lawsuits against TikTok](https://www.nbcnews.com/tech/tech-news/tiktok-sued-14-attorneys-general-rcna174395), with California and New York leading a bipartisan coalition that included Illinois, Kentucky, Louisiana, Massachusetts, Mississippi, New Jersey, North Carolina, Oregon, South Carolina, Vermont, Washington, and D.C. Multiple other states — including Iowa, Indiana, Kansas, Nevada, New Hampshire, Nebraska, and Utah — filed individual suits earlier.

The Arkansas lawsuit is therefore both a foundational enforcement precedent and an early data point in what became a nationwide wave of state AG consumer-protection litigation against social media companies targeting minors.

## Detailed Analysis [MEDIUM confidence]

### TikTok's Motion to Dismiss: Jurisdictional Challenge

TikTok's primary argument in its motion to dismiss was that Arkansas lacked personal jurisdiction over TikTok and ByteDance. As a company incorporated outside of Arkansas with its principal place of business abroad, TikTok contended that it had insufficient minimum contacts with Arkansas to be subject to the state's courts.

Circuit Judge Holly L. Meyer [rejected this argument](https://arkansasag.gov/news-release/attorney-general-griffin-celebrates-big-win-allowing-state-lawsuit-against-tiktok-to-proceed/), finding that as a global company doing business throughout the United States, "it is entirely reasonable for TikTok to expect that it could be haled into court in Arkansas, especially considering TikTok profits handsomely off Arkansas minors." The court's reasoning tracked the purposeful availment doctrine: TikTok actively sought out and monetized Arkansas users, derived advertising revenue from their engagement, and operated with full knowledge that a significant portion of its Arkansas user base consisted of minors. These commercial contacts were deemed sufficient to establish specific personal jurisdiction for claims arising directly from that Arkansas-targeted conduct.

This ruling has significance beyond the immediate case. If affirmed on appeal, it establishes that technology platforms cannot use their offshore corporate structures as a shield against enforcement by states where they actively conduct business and whose residents they monetize.

### ADTPA Scope: Is TikTok a "Good or Service"?

TikTok made a second threshold argument that its platform does not constitute a "good or service" within the ADTPA's coverage, and that the statute therefore does not apply to it at all. Judge Meyer also rejected this argument. The court found that the state had plausibly alleged the ADTPA's substantive provisions apply to TikTok's conduct, characterizing the platform's services as falling within the Act's broad scope of prohibited unconscionable business and trade practices.

The court's analysis reflects a growing consensus among state courts interpreting consumer-protection statutes to cover digital platforms. Platform services — even when nominally free to end users — constitute trade or commerce when they generate revenue through user data and advertising. Several other state courts considering analogous claims have reached similar conclusions.

### Substantive Claims: Misrepresentation and Unconscionable Practices

On the merits of the ADTPA claims, the court found that the AG's complaint plausibly stated causes of action for:

1. **Deceptive representations about safety.** The state alleged TikTok misrepresented to consumers and regulators that the platform was safe for minor users despite internal knowledge of the psychological and behavioral harms associated with extended use by adolescents.

2. **Misrepresentation about content access.** The complaint alleged TikTok marketed the app as appropriate for teenagers while knowing that harmful and adult content was readily accessible to minor users, and that content moderation controls were less effective than publicly represented.

3. **Unconscionable exploitation of minors.** The state alleged TikTok's algorithm was specifically engineered to exploit the developmental vulnerabilities of adolescent users to maximize engagement, and that TikTok knew minors are "especially susceptible to addiction and the mental health harms associated with addiction to the app."

These claims survived dismissal at the pleading stage, meaning the state presented factual allegations that, taken as true, state legally cognizable violations.

### Subsequent Appellate Proceedings

After the motion to dismiss was denied in May 2024, TikTok escalated. On August 12, 2024, TikTok filed a petition for writ of certiorari or other extraordinary writ with the [Arkansas Supreme Court](https://www.kark.com/news/state-news/arkansas-supreme-court-decides-tiktok-vs-arkansas-case-will-go-forward-denies-dismissal-request/), seeking emergency review of the jurisdictional ruling before trial. The Cleburne County proceedings were [stayed](https://arktimes.com/arkansas-blog/2024/09/12/ags-lawsuit-against-tiktok-on-hold-as-state-supreme-court-considers-request-for-dismissal) pending the high court's consideration.

In May 2025, the [Arkansas Supreme Court declined to entertain the interlocutory petition](https://arktimes.com/arkansas-blog/2025/05/29/arkansass-suit-against-tiktok-to-proceed-after-state-supreme-court-declines-to-end-it), holding that jurisdictional challenges do not qualify as the type of extraordinary circumstances warranting emergency appellate intervention. The high court indicated that TikTok's jurisdictional arguments can be preserved and raised as part of a regular appeal following any adverse trial court judgment. The stay was lifted and the case was returned to Cleburne County Circuit Court. Trial is currently scheduled for September 29, 2025, with a jury trial expected to last three weeks.

## Impact Assessment [MEDIUM confidence]

### Affected Entities

**Social media platforms.** Any platform with substantial engagement from Arkansas-resident minors is potentially subject to ADTPA enforcement claims paralleling those advanced against TikTok. The court's jurisdictional analysis establishes a low threshold: companies that monetize Arkansas users cannot use offshore incorporation or lack of a physical presence in the state as a categorical shield.

**Platform counsel and compliance teams.** The case demonstrates that state deceptive-practices statutes can reach social media conduct, including algorithmic design choices and representations about content safety — not only explicit data-collection violations. Compliance programs focused narrowly on data privacy may underestimate the ADTPA exposure.

**Multistate enforcement landscape.** The June 2024 ruling comes in the context of rapidly expanding multistate coordination. By the time of the motion-to-dismiss ruling, the AG coalition against TikTok had not yet fully coalesced, but the trend was clear. Platforms must now plan for simultaneous, coordinated enforcement actions from dozens of state attorneys general advancing coordinated legal theories.

### Compliance Considerations

The Arkansas AG's complaint — and the legal theories it advanced — signal that state enforcement of social media safety obligations is not limited to explicit statutory regimes such as COPPA or state equivalents. General consumer-protection statutes, applied creatively, can reach:

- Representations made in terms of service or marketing materials about platform safety
- Effectiveness (or lack thereof) of parental controls and age-gating features
- Algorithmic design features that the state characterizes as exploiting minor users
- Content moderation claims that differ from actual platform performance

Platforms that make affirmative public statements about their safety practices for minor users face potential ADTPA liability if those statements are materially inconsistent with actual platform operations or internal knowledge.

### Enforcement Outlook

The September 2025 trial date — if it proceeds — would make the Arkansas TikTok case one of the first state AG consumer-protection lawsuits against a social media company to reach trial on the merits. A plaintiff verdict would likely trigger further enforcement and legislative activity; a defense verdict or significant damages limitation could provide the industry with a counterweight to the current enforcement wave.

Given the parallel federal litigation over TikTok's Chinese ownership (the divest-or-ban law enacted in 2024), the company faces compounding regulatory pressure at both state and federal levels. Resolution of the federal ban question does not moot or resolve the state consumer-protection claims.

## Action Items

- Social media platforms with Arkansas-based minor users should audit public representations about content safety, parental controls, and algorithmic safeguards against internal documentation and actual platform performance.
- Ensure that terms of service and marketing materials accurately describe content moderation practices, particularly with respect to minor-accessible content.
- Review whether existing ADTPA compliance risk assessments account for consumer-protection theories targeting design and representational practices — not just data collection.
- Monitor the Cleburne County case for trial developments in September 2025, as the first merits verdict in this generation of social media AG enforcement cases will be highly consequential.
- Track the parallel multistate TikTok litigation coordinated by California and New York for precedent development on ADTPA/UDAP theory applicability to platform design.
