# Tennessee's ELVIS Act: AI-Driven Amendments to the Right of Publicity

**Jurisdiction:** Tennessee | **Category:** Privacy | **Date:** 2024-05-28

## Executive Summary [HIGH confidence]

Tennessee Governor Bill Lee signed the Ensuring Likeness, Voice, and Image Security (ELVIS) Act on March 21, 2024, making Tennessee the first state to enact legislation directly targeting AI-generated voice clones and digital replicas in the context of right of publicity law. The Act amends Tennessee's Personal Rights Protection Act of 1984 (Tenn. Code Ann. §§ 47-25-1101 et seq.) to add "voice" as a protected attribute alongside name, photograph, and likeness — and creates three separate causes of civil action, one of which imposes direct liability on providers of AI tools or algorithms whose "primary purpose or function" is generating unauthorized voice or likeness replicas. The law took effect July 1, 2024, and carries both civil remedies and Class A misdemeanor criminal penalties. It is the most comprehensive state-level protection for artists against AI impersonation enacted to date, and it serves as a model for analogous federal proposals including the NO FAKES Act and the No AI FRAUD Act.

## Background [HIGH confidence]

Tennessee's Personal Rights Protection Act of 1984 was itself a landmark statute. It was enacted primarily to extend [Elvis Presley's postmortem right of publicity](https://www.tn.gov/governor/news/2024/1/10/tennessee-first-in-the-nation-to-address-ai-impact-on-music-industry.html) after multiple courts questioned whether the estate could rely on anything beyond copyright, trademark, and false-endorsement law without an explicit state statutory right. The 1984 Act — now renamed the ELVIS Act — established a property-based right of publicity in Tennessee that survives the death of the individual and is descendible to heirs and assigns.

For four decades, the statute provided protection against unauthorized commercial use of an individual's name, photograph, or likeness for advertising, merchandise, fundraising, and solicitation. Its scope was explicitly commercial: the unauthorized use had to be in a commercial context to trigger liability. The 1984 Act also contained a criminal enforcement provision at Tenn. Code Ann. § 39-14-152(g), making willful violations a Class A misdemeanor.

By 2024, the explosion of generative AI had rendered the 1984 statute insufficient. AI voice-cloning technology enabled the creation of near-perfect audio replicas of artists without their consent. Several high-profile cases — including an AI-generated song mimicking Drake and The Weeknd that went viral in April 2023 — illustrated the urgency. Governor Lee announced exploratory work on AI music-industry legislation in January 2024, [describing Tennessee as "first in the nation" to address the AI threat](https://www.tn.gov/governor/news/2024/1/10/tennessee-first-in-the-nation-to-address-ai-impact-on-music-industry.html). The ELVIS Act emerged from that effort.

The bill — [House Bill 2091](https://legiscan.com/TN/text/HB2091/id/2900923) by Rep. William Lamberth (R-44) and [Senate Bill 2096](https://wapp.capitol.tn.gov/apps/BillInfo/Default?BillNumber=HB2091&GA=113) by Sen. Jack Johnson (R-27) — passed the Tennessee House 93-0 and the Senate 30-0, an unusual display of bipartisan unanimity. Artists including Chris Janson and Luke Bryan attended the March 21, 2024 [signing ceremony at Robert's Western World](https://www.tn.gov/governor/news/2024/3/21/photos--gov--lee-signs-elvis-act-into-law.html) in Nashville. The Act's full text is available from the [Tennessee General Assembly](https://www.capitol.tn.gov/Bills/113/Bill/HB2091.pdf).

## Detailed Analysis [HIGH confidence]

### Renaming and Structural Changes

The ELVIS Act formally renames the "Personal Rights Protection Act of 1984" to the "Ensuring Likeness, Voice, and Image Security Act." This renaming is more than symbolic: it signals that the statute is no longer a narrow postmortem right-of-publicity protection for celebrity estates but a living instrument to address contemporary misuse of identity.

### Addition of "Voice" as a Protected Attribute

The most consequential substantive change is the addition of "voice" throughout Tenn. Code Ann. §§ 47-25-1101 et seq. [The Act defines "voice"](https://www.capitol.tn.gov/Bills/113/Bill/HB2091.pdf) as "a sound in a medium that is readily identifiable and attributable to a particular individual, regardless of whether the sound contains the actual voice or a simulation of the voice of the individual." This definition intentionally captures AI-generated voice clones: a simulation that is identifiable as a particular person falls within the definition even if no actual audio of that person was used to generate it.

The definition's breadth is significant. It covers voice actors, musicians, podcasters, and virtually any individual whose voice is distinctive enough to be "readily identifiable and attributable" to them — not just celebrities or performers.

### Three Causes of Civil Action

[Section 6 of HB 2091 creates three distinct causes of action](https://www.lw.com/admin/upload/SiteAttachments/The-ELVIS-Act-Tennessee-Shakes-Up-Its-Right-of-Publicity-Law-and-Acts-On-Generative-AI.pdf):

1. **Commercial use without authorization** (§ 47-25-1105(a)(1)): The traditional right-of-publicity cause of action — knowingly using an individual's name, photograph, voice, or likeness for advertising, fundraising, or solicitation without authorization. This is effectively the 1984 Act extended to include voice.

2. **Unauthorized distribution of voice or likeness** (§ 47-25-1105(a)(2)): A new cause of action targeting any person who "publishes, performs, distributes, transmits, or otherwise makes available to the public an individual's voice or likeness, with knowledge that use of the voice or likeness was not authorized." Critically, this cause of action is not limited to commercial contexts. It extends liability to any public distribution of unauthorized voice or likeness content — including, potentially, posting an AI-generated audio clip on social media.

3. **Distribution of AI tools whose primary purpose is unauthorized voice/likeness generation** (§ 47-25-1105(a)(3)): A novel secondary-liability provision that targets any person who "distributes, transmits, or otherwise makes available an algorithm, software, tool, or other technology, service, or device, the primary purpose or function of which is the production of an individual's photograph, voice, or likeness without authorization." This provision directly targets AI voice-cloning and deepfake services. The "primary purpose or function" limiting principle is intended to exclude general-purpose AI platforms, but its precise contours remain untested in litigation.

### Secondary Liability for AI Platform Providers

The third cause of action is the statute's most innovative — and contested — provision. As [Latham & Watkins analyzed](https://www.lw.com/admin/upload/SiteAttachments/The-ELVIS-Act-Tennessee-Shakes-Up-Its-Right-of-Publicity-Law-and-Acts-On-Generative-AI.pdf), the provision imposes a form of secondary liability on AI developers that goes beyond what most state right-of-publicity statutes contemplate. A provider of a voice-cloning service whose tool is designed specifically to reproduce identifiable voices could face liability under this provision even if it is not the entity that created the specific unauthorized replica.

The "primary purpose or function" qualifier creates a significant ambiguity. A general-purpose text-to-speech tool is unlikely to qualify. A service specifically marketed for "celebrity voice cloning" or "artist voice replication" almost certainly would. Tools in the middle of this spectrum — capable of voice cloning but also marketed for legitimate use cases — present the most uncertain exposure.

[Davis Wright Tremaine's analysis](https://www.dwt.com/blogs/artificial-intelligence-law-advisor/2024/04/tennessee-elvis-act-ai-voice-replica) raises the additional question of whether Section 230 of the Communications Decency Act would shield platforms that host user-generated AI voice content. Because the ELVIS Act does not explicitly carve out "interactive computer service providers" and the second cause of action covers entities that "transmit or otherwise make available" infringing content, platforms could face arguments that Section 230 immunity does not apply to a state right-of-publicity claim of this type.

### Exemptions and Safe Harbors

The ELVIS Act preserves and modifies several exemptions from the 1984 Act. Under [Tenn. Code Ann. § 47-25-1107](https://www.capitol.tn.gov/Bills/113/Bill/HB2091.pdf), uses are not actionable if they are:

- In connection with news, public affairs, or sports broadcast or account;
- For the purpose of comment, criticism, scholarship, satire, or parody;
- A representation in an audiovisual work that does not create a false impression that the work is an authentic recording of that individual's participation;
- Fleeting or incidental use; or
- Advertisements for the above-listed permitted uses.

Notably, [Latham & Watkins and Phelps both observe](https://www.phelps.com/insights/the-elvis-act-protects-artists-voices-but-creates-risks-for-media-companies.html) that the ELVIS Act narrowed several prior exemptions, potentially expanding liability for media companies. The "fleeting or incidental use" exemption, for instance, is now more tightly scoped. Parody and satire defenses survive but are bounded: a work that creates a "false impression of authenticity" is not protected even if comedic intent exists.

The Act also incorporates the First Amendment as an express limiting principle, referencing First Amendment protections in contexts where the use of a voice or likeness might constitute protected speech. This grafting of First Amendment jurisprudence directly into Tennessee statutory claims is unusual and will likely generate novel questions for state courts about the intersection of publicity rights and free expression.

### Enforcement: Civil and Criminal Remedies

Under the Act, civil plaintiffs may seek:

- Injunctive relief to prevent or restrain unauthorized use;
- Impoundment and destruction of infringing materials;
- Actual damages plus any profits attributable to the unauthorized use;
- Attorney's fees in appropriate cases.

Separately, [Tenn. Code Ann. § 39-14-152(g)](https://www.capitol.tn.gov/Bills/113/Bill/HB2091.pdf) preserves criminal liability: willful violation constitutes a Class A misdemeanor, punishable by up to 11 months and 29 days in jail and/or fines up to $2,500.

The Act also clarifies enforcement standing in the context of the music industry: where an individual has entered into an exclusive recording contract or granted an exclusive license to distribute sound recordings, either the individual or the contracting party (i.e., a record label) may bring an enforcement action. This provision addresses the practical reality that labels, not artists, often bear the direct economic injury from unauthorized AI voice use.

## Impact Assessment [MEDIUM confidence]

### Entertainment and Music Industry

The ELVIS Act primarily targets the music industry but extends to all individuals whose voice or likeness is commercially valuable. Nashville-based artists and record labels were the most visible proponents, and the Act's enforcement-standing provision explicitly accommodates label enforcement. However, the Act applies to any identifiable individual — not just professional entertainers — meaning its theoretical scope is extremely broad.

For record labels and artists, the Act provides new tools to combat AI voice services that train on protected recordings and offer voice cloning of specific artists. Legal demand letters citing the ELVIS Act have been reported in the industry, though as of the finding date no appellate decisions have interpreted the statute.

### AI Platform Developers and Distributors

[Holland & Knight](https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2024/04/first-of-its-kind-ai-law-addresses-deep-fakes-and-voice-clones), [Skadden](https://www.skadden.com/insights/publications/2024/04/tennessee-law-addresses-proliferation-of-deepfakes), and [ArentFox Schiff](https://www.afslaw.com/perspectives/alerts/elvis-alive-tennessee-first-implement-rights-publicity-protections-against-ai) each note that the Act creates new litigation exposure for AI developers and distributors operating in Tennessee or whose services are accessible in Tennessee. The territorial reach of the Act is unresolved: because the statute does not specify a geographic nexus, there is uncertainty about whether a California-based AI company that has no physical presence in Tennessee could be subject to suit if a Tennessee artist's voice is cloned using the company's tool.

Companies operating voice-synthesis, audio-deepfake, or voice-conversion services should assess whether their tools' "primary purpose or function" could be characterized as producing unauthorized voice replicas, regardless of other use cases the tools support.

### Media Companies

[Phelps' analysis](https://www.phelps.com/insights/the-elvis-act-protects-artists-voices-but-creates-risks-for-media-companies.html) warns that media companies that republish, broadcast, or host audio or video content may face exposure under the Act's second cause of action (distribution with knowledge of unauthorized use) if they knowingly disseminate AI-generated content. The narrowing of fair use and incidental use exemptions increases this risk.

### Federal Legislative Landscape

The ELVIS Act is explicitly positioned as a model for federal legislation. The bipartisan [NO FAKES Act](https://www.congress.gov/bill/118th-congress/senate-bill/2770), introduced in the Senate in September 2023, would create a federal right against unauthorized digital replicas. The [No AI FRAUD Act](https://www.congress.gov/bill/118th-congress/house-bill/6943), introduced in the House in January 2024, would establish a federal right protecting voice and likeness. Neither bill has passed as of the writing of this report, but Tennessee's unanimous enactment creates political momentum for federal action.

## Action Items

- **AI voice and image service providers**: Audit your product's "primary purpose or function" against the ELVIS Act's third cause of action. If your service is marketed or primarily used for generating identifiable voice or likeness replicas, obtain legal counsel on Tennessee exposure before July 1, 2024 effective date (now past).
- **Record labels and artist representatives**: Review existing recording contracts and license agreements to confirm that enforcement standing under the ELVIS Act is properly allocated. Both the artist and the label may have independent standing; coordinate enforcement strategy.
- **Media companies and platforms**: Review content policies for AI-generated audio and video content. Ensure that the "knowledge" element of the second cause of action is addressed — platforms that knowingly host unauthorized AI-voice content may face liability.
- **General-purpose AI developers**: Monitor litigation under the "primary purpose or function" standard as courts begin to interpret this threshold. Develop internal documentation of legitimate use cases that distinguish your tool from purpose-built voice-cloning services.
- **Compliance teams**: Track proposed federal legislation (NO FAKES Act, No AI FRAUD Act) that would create a uniform national standard, which could preempt or complement Tennessee law.
- **Legal teams**: Note the narrowing of fair use and incidental use exemptions relative to the 1984 Act. Update clearance checklists to reflect the ELVIS Act's modified exemption scope.
