# Digital Cover-Up Triggers Career-Altering Sanctions for Deleting ChatGPT Account Logs

**Jurisdiction:** Federal (N.D. Ala.) | **Category:** AI Law / Legal Ethics / E-Discovery | **Date:** June 29, 2026

## Executive Summary [MEDIUM confidence]

On May 21, 2026, U.S. District Judge Harold D. Mooty III of the Northern District of Alabama imposed sweeping sanctions on solo-practitioner attorney H. Gregory Harp in *Miller v. Regions Bank*, No. 2:24-cv-1324-HDM, after Harp filed a summary-judgment brief containing four fabricated case quotations and then deleted his ChatGPT Plus account three days after being ordered to preserve and produce his ChatGPT usage history for in camera review, according to [eDiscovery Today's summary of the ruling](https://ediscoverytoday.com/2026/05/28/career-altering-sanctions-imposed-on-counsel-for-deleting-chatgpt-account-ediscovery-case-law/) and independent reporting from [FindLaw](https://www.findlaw.com/legalblogs/uncategorized/alabama-attorney-suspended-for-filing-false-quotes-and-deleting-chatgpt-account/). The court found that Harp's initial submission of fabricated legal authority, combined with his subsequent lack of candor and deliberate destruction of the ChatGPT account (including seeking a prorated refund to accelerate the account's termination), warranted sanctions under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 11, the court's inherent authority, and Alabama Rule of Professional Conduct 3.3, as well as a finding of civil contempt. This case is significant because it is one of the first documented rulings in which a court applied a spoliation-based adverse inference specifically to a litigant's deletion of generative-AI chat logs, treating an AI tool's account history as discoverable evidence subject to the same preservation duties as any other electronically stored information (ESI). It signals to courts and practitioners nationwide that AI usage records are not exempt from litigation holds and that destroying them after a preservation order can independently trigger severe sanctions distinct from — and in this case more consequential than — the underlying AI-hallucination misconduct.

## Background [MEDIUM confidence]

The case arises from an ordinary civil dispute, *Jackie Miller v. Regions Bank*, pending in the Southern Division of the Northern District of Alabama. According to [eDiscovery Today](https://ediscoverytoday.com/2026/05/28/career-altering-sanctions-imposed-on-counsel-for-deleting-chatgpt-account-ediscovery-case-law/), on November 26, 2025, plaintiff's counsel H. Gregory Harp purchased a paid ChatGPT Plus subscription, and four days later, on November 30, 2025, filed a response in opposition to Regions Bank's motion for summary judgment. That brief contained four quotations attributed to legitimate, existing appellate decisions — but the quoted language did not actually appear in those opinions, a pattern consistent with generative-AI "hallucination" of citations.

This ruling follows a broader and growing line of federal sanctions cases involving fabricated AI-generated citations, beginning with *Mata v. Avianca, Inc.* in the Southern District of New York in 2023 (the first widely publicized ChatGPT-hallucination sanctions case) and continuing through 2025 rulings such as Judge Anna Manasco's reprimand of Butler Snow attorneys defending Alabama's prison system for submitting AI-hallucinated citations, reported by [Alabama Daily News](https://aldailynews.com/judge-sanctions-lawyers-defending-alabamas-prison-system-for-using-fake-chatgpt-cases-in-filings/). What distinguishes the *Miller* matter from these prior cases is not the initial hallucination — courts have by now developed a fairly consistent Rule 11 framework for that — but the litigant's response once confronted with the error.

## Detailed Analysis [LOW confidence]

According to [eDiscovery Today's account of the opinion](https://ediscoverytoday.com/2026/05/28/career-altering-sanctions-imposed-on-counsel-for-deleting-chatgpt-account-ediscovery-case-law/), when opposing counsel could not locate the quoted language in the cited cases, Harp initially denied that AI had generated the offending material, telling the court he had used ChatGPT only "to do a general search" and that AI had not "wrote a single word" of the brief. The court subsequently issued two orders to show cause: one directing Harp to explain why he should not be sanctioned for making false statements to the court, and a second directing him to explain why he should not be held in civil contempt for violating a preservation/production order and for bad-faith obstruction and spoliation of evidence.

When the court ordered Harp to produce screenshots of his ChatGPT conversation history for in camera review, Harp deleted his ChatGPT account on April 23, 2026 — three days after the order — and, according to the reporting, contacted OpenAI seeking a prorated refund specifically so that his account access would terminate immediately rather than persist through the end of the billing cycle. Emails later produced in the sanctions proceeding revealed that Harp had purchased the paid ChatGPT Plus subscription only four days before filing the brief, directly contradicting his earlier representation to the court that he had used only the free version of ChatGPT.

Judge Mooty found that this sequence of conduct — the fabricated authority, the initial denial, and the subsequent destruction of the very evidence that would have proven or disproven AI involvement — justified an adverse inference that the deleted ChatGPT history would have confirmed that the fabricated quotations were AI-generated. The court held that the destruction of evidence, occurring after a direct preservation and production order, was in bad faith. Sanctions were imposed under Rule 11, the court's inherent authority to sanction bad-faith litigation conduct, and Alabama Rule of Professional Conduct 3.3 (candor toward the tribunal), and the court additionally held Harp in civil contempt, per [eDiscovery Today](https://ediscoverytoday.com/2026/05/28/career-altering-sanctions-imposed-on-counsel-for-deleting-chatgpt-account-ediscovery-case-law/) and corroborating detail surfaced in web search results describing the same order.

## Sanctions Imposed [MEDIUM confidence]

Per [eDiscovery Today](https://ediscoverytoday.com/2026/05/28/career-altering-sanctions-imposed-on-counsel-for-deleting-chatgpt-account-ediscovery-case-law/) and [FindLaw](https://www.findlaw.com/legalblogs/uncategorized/alabama-attorney-suspended-for-filing-false-quotes-and-deleting-chatgpt-account/), Judge Mooty ordered that Harp:

- Be publicly reprimanded;
- Distribute the court's opinion to his clients, opposing counsel, and the presiding judges in every other pending case in which he appears;
- Be disqualified from further participation in the *Miller* case;
- Be suspended from practicing law in the Northern District of Alabama for six months; and
- Be referred to the Alabama State Bar and other applicable disciplinary authorities for further investigation.

The court expressly distinguished the sanctionable conduct from mere use of generative AI, noting that attorneys may use such tools responsibly; the sanctions rested on the submission of false legal authority, failure to make reasonable inquiry into the accuracy of AI-generated content, lack of candor to the court, and — most significantly for the severity of the penalty — the deliberate destruction of evidence during the sanctions proceeding itself.

## Impact Assessment [MEDIUM confidence]

This ruling has direct implications for litigators, in-house counsel, and e-discovery professionals across all practice areas, not solely those in Alabama or the Eleventh Circuit. It establishes, at least at the district-court level, that:

1. **AI chat histories are discoverable ESI subject to litigation holds.** Once a court has reason to believe generative-AI tools were used to produce filed content, a party's or attorney's account history for that tool becomes subject to standard preservation and production obligations, and a preservation order can require production for in camera review.
2. **Deleting an AI account after a preservation order can independently support spoliation sanctions and an adverse inference**, separate from and potentially more severe than sanctions for the underlying AI-hallucination error itself. Here, the sanctions imposed (six-month practice suspension, disqualification, bar referral) go well beyond the monetary sanctions typically seen in pure hallucination cases such as *Mata v. Avianca*.
3. **Affirmative acts to accelerate destruction of evidence (e.g., seeking a prorated refund to end account access immediately) will be treated as strong evidence of bad faith**, likely foreclosing arguments that the deletion was routine account "housekeeping."
4. **Law firms and solo practitioners should extend litigation-hold and preservation protocols to cover generative-AI tool accounts** (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Copilot, and similar) whenever AI-assisted drafting is used in connection with matters that are or may become the subject of litigation or sanctions proceedings.

The ruling is a district-court order and does not bind other courts, but it is likely to be cited persuasively in future e-discovery and Rule 11/AI-hallucination disputes given the paucity of precedent specifically addressing AI account deletion as spoliation.

## Action Items

- Law firms should update litigation-hold notice templates to explicitly include generative-AI tool accounts (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Copilot, etc.) and instruct custodians not to delete, downgrade, or cancel such accounts once a hold issues.
- Attorneys who use generative AI for drafting or research should independently verify every citation and quotation against the primary source before filing, consistent with Rule 11's reasonable-inquiry standard.
- Firms should monitor for the underlying docket (N.D. Ala., No. 2:24-cv-1324-HDM) to become available on PACER/CourtListener and verify the specific rule citations and quoted findings once the official filing can be directly reviewed.
- General counsel and e-discovery teams should incorporate AI-tool account preservation into standard ESI preservation checklists and outside-counsel guidelines.
- Monitor the Alabama State Bar disciplinary docket for the referral outcome against Attorney Harp.
