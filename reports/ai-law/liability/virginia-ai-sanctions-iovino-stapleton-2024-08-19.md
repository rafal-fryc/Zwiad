# Attorneys Face Potential Sanctions for Alleged Misuse of AI: Iovino v. Michael Stapleton Associates

**Jurisdiction:** Virginia (W.D. Va.) / Federal | **Category:** Privacy / AI Law / Legal Ethics | **Date:** August 19, 2024

## Executive Summary [HIGH confidence]

In August 2024, U.S. District Judge Thomas T. Cullen of the Western District of Virginia issued a show cause order in [*Iovino v. Michael Stapleton Associates, Ltd.*, No. 5:21-cv-00064](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/district-courts/virginia/vawdce/5:2021cv00064/123582/177/), ordering plaintiff's counsel to explain why they should not be sanctioned under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 11(c) after their brief cited two apparently nonexistent cases and attributed fabricated quotations to two real decisions. Defense counsel characterized the errors as "ChatGPT run amok." Judge Cullen held a show cause hearing on October 9, 2024, and ultimately declined to sanction lead attorney Thomas Guyer, finding the errors were an honest mistake by a lawyer with an unblemished record rather than intentional misconduct. The case is one of a growing line of federal court proceedings in which judges have directly confronted the professional-responsibility implications of attorney use of generative AI, and it coincided with the American Bar Association's issuance of [Formal Opinion 512](https://www.americanbar.org/news/abanews/aba-news-archives/2024/07/aba-issues-first-ethics-guidance-ai-tools/) — the ABA's first comprehensive ethics opinion on generative AI — in July 2024.

## Background [HIGH confidence]

### The Underlying Litigation

*Iovino v. Michael Stapleton Associates, Ltd.* is a federal whistleblower case filed in 2021 in the Western District of Virginia. The plaintiff, Karen Iovino, alleges that defendant Michael Stapleton Associates, Ltd. d/b/a MSA Security, Inc. (MSA) violated federal whistleblower protections. The case involved extended and contentious discovery disputes before [Magistrate Judge Joel C. Hoppe](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/district-courts/virginia/vawdce/5:2021cv00064/123582/55/).

### The Show Cause Order

The triggering event was plaintiff's brief objecting to Magistrate Judge Hoppe's order granting MSA's motion for a protective order. In reviewing that brief, MSA's counsel identified several anomalous citations and flagged them in its opposition brief, positing they were the result of generative AI "run amok." Judge Cullen reviewed the cited authorities and [issued a show cause order](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/district-courts/virginia/vawdce/5:2021cv00064/123582/177/), citing *Iovino v. Michael Stapleton Assocs., Ltd.*, 2024 WL 3520170 (W.D. Va. 2024), and giving attorneys 21 days to respond.

### Growing National Trend

The *Iovino* proceeding was neither the first nor the last instance of courts confronting AI-generated citation errors. The seminal case was *Mata v. Avianca, Inc.* in the Southern District of New York, where a judge sanctioned attorneys who submitted a brief citing multiple nonexistent ChatGPT-generated cases and ordered a $5,000 monetary sanction. By late 2024, courts had documented hundreds of incidents involving AI-generated hallucinations in legal filings. By the close of 2025, [ABA Journal reporting and tracking databases](https://www.abajournal.com/news/article/sanctions-ramping-up-in-cases-involving-ai-hallucinations) had catalogued over 700 incidents, with the pace accelerating.

## Detailed Analysis [HIGH confidence]

### The Specific Errors

Judge Cullen's show cause order identified four distinct categories of citation error in plaintiff's brief:

1. **Two apparently nonexistent cases**: Plaintiff cited *United Therapeutics Corp. v. Watson Labs, Inc.*, No. 3:17-cv-00081, 2017 WL 2483620 (E.D. Va. June 7, 2017), and *United States v. Mosby*, 2021 WL 2827893, at *4 (D. Md. July 7, 2021), neither of which could be located in any legal database.

2. **Fabricated quotation in a real case**: Plaintiff quoted language purportedly from [*Graves v. Lioi*, 930 F.3d 307, 318 (4th Cir. 2019)](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/district-courts/virginia/vawdce/5:2021cv00064/123582/177/) — including the phrase "decided by necessary implication" — that does not appear anywhere in the Fourth Circuit's actual opinion.

3. **Fabricated quotation in a second real case**: Plaintiff attributed a quotation to a Supreme Court opinion that exists but does not contain the attributed language.

These errors are characteristic of [large language model "hallucinations"](https://www.esquiresolutions.com/federal-court-turns-up-the-heat-on-attorneys-using-chatgpt-for-research/) — plausible-sounding but fabricated outputs generated when AI tools are asked to produce citations without access to verified legal databases.

### Rule 11 Framework

Judge Cullen grounded the show cause order in [Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 11(c)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/rules/frcp/rule_11). Rule 11 requires that by presenting any pleading or paper to the court, an attorney certifies to the best of their "knowledge, information, and belief, formed after an inquiry reasonable under the circumstances" that the legal contentions are "warranted by existing law." Courts have applied this standard to hold that attorneys must read and verify every authority cited — a duty that cannot be outsourced to technology.

As Judge Cullen stated in his order: "If counsel relies on artificial intelligence or other technology to draft a filing, the attorney is still responsible for ensuring the filing is accurate and does not contain fabricated caselaw or quotations." The Second Circuit had reached a parallel conclusion earlier in 2024, finding that no local rule was necessary to impose sanctions for AI-generated errors because [Rule 11 already covered the conduct](https://ndnyfcba.org/column-second-circuit-indicates-that-rule-11-covers-relying-on-generative-ai-to-conduct-legal-analysis/), requiring attorneys to "ensure that her submissions to the Court are accurate."

### Attorney's Response and Hearing Outcome

After receiving the show cause order, lead attorney Thomas Guyer filed a response in which he denied intentionally using fictitious citations but acknowledged the errors and attributed them to AI-assisted drafting. At the October 9, 2024, show cause hearing, according to reporting by [LawSites/LawNext](https://www.lawnext.com/2025/03/federal-judge-in-virginia-declines-to-sanction-lawyer-who-filed-ai-generated-erroneous-citations.html), Judge Cullen ruled orally that no sanctions would be imposed. Judge Cullen credited Guyer's candor, observing that the attorney "owned the mistake" and took sole responsibility. The judge characterized Guyer's record as "unblemished" and concluded the errors reflected an "honest mistake" rather than an intent to mislead the court.

Judge Cullen nonetheless used the proceeding to articulate standards for AI use in legal practice: generative AI has become the "new normal," but attorneys "must still adhere to basic tenants of conduct," which includes "taking reasonable measures" to ensure filings are accurate — regardless of the technology used to draft them.

### Concurrent ABA Guidance

The *Iovino* show cause order was entered against the backdrop of [ABA Formal Opinion 512](https://www.americanbar.org/news/abanews/aba-news-archives/2024/07/aba-issues-first-ethics-guidance-ai-tools/), issued on July 29, 2024 — just weeks before the August 2024 order. The ABA's Standing Committee on Ethics and Professional Responsibility, in Formal Opinion 512, articulated that:

- Lawyers need not become AI experts, but "must have a reasonable understanding of the capabilities and limitations" of any generative AI tool they use.
- The duty of competence explicitly encompasses AI literacy.
- AI tools "cannot solely substitute for a lawyer's competent legal work."
- Verification requirements are fact-specific and "will necessarily depend on the GAI tool and the specific task."

These principles directly track the concerns animating the *Iovino* show cause order and the broader wave of AI citation sanctions cases.

### Virginia State Bar Guidance

Contemporaneously, the [Virginia State Bar released guidance on generative AI](https://virginialawyer.vsb.org/articles/trust-but-verif-ai-a-judicious-approach-to-incorporating-ai-in-virginia-s-legal-profession) in August 2024. The Virginia Bar's position:

- Lawyers' basic ethical responsibilities have not changed due to AI; generative AI presents issues "fundamentally similar" to those arising with other technology.
- Lawyers must verify AI-generated citations: specifically, "verify that any citations are accurate (and real)."
- The duty of supervision extends to AI outputs: if a paralegal or associate uses AI, the responsible lawyer must ensure proper use.
- Billing ethics are implicated: a lawyer may not bill for time saved by using generative AI at an hourly rate the work would otherwise not warrant.

## Impact Assessment [MEDIUM confidence]

### Professional Responsibility Consequences

The *Iovino* proceeding illustrates several liability vectors for attorneys who use generative AI carelessly:

- **Rule 11 sanctions**: Monetary fines, payment of opposing counsel's fees, or injunctive relief (e.g., disqualification). In the Fifth Circuit, a $2,500 sanction was imposed on an attorney who used AI drafting tools; in the Sixth Circuit, two attorneys were collectively fined $30,000 for dozens of fake citations. [Sanctions are escalating nationally](https://www.abajournal.com/news/article/sanctions-ramping-up-in-cases-involving-ai-hallucinations).

- **State bar referral**: Judge Cullen's show cause order expressly contemplated referral to state bars for professional misconduct — a consequence that can result in suspension or disbarment.

- **Client harm**: Fabricated citations undermine client positions, risk dismissal of claims or defenses relying on nonexistent authority, and expose attorneys to malpractice liability.

### Affected Practitioners

Any attorney who uses AI-assisted drafting tools — whether general-purpose large language models (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini) or legal-specific AI tools (Harvey, Lexis+ AI, Westlaw AI) — faces this risk. [Law firm risk management analyses](https://www.bakerbotts.com/thought-leadership/publications/2024/december/trust-but-verify-avoiding-the-perils-of-ai-hallucinations-in-court) consistently identify citation hallucination as the highest-risk category of AI-related professional liability.

### Court Procedural Implications

While Judge Cullen declined to sanction Guyer in this instance, the proceeding adds to a body of judicial statements articulating that:

- Reliance on AI does not diminish the attorney's personal, non-delegable verification obligation.
- Courts will not treat AI hallucinations as an automatic excuse for inaccurate filings.
- Candor, prompt acknowledgment of error, and a clean disciplinary record weigh heavily in mitigation.

## Action Items

- **Implement mandatory citation verification**: Every AI-generated citation — case name, reporter, volume, page, year, and quoted language — must be independently verified in Westlaw, Lexis, or official government repositories before filing. Do not rely on AI output alone.
- **Establish law firm AI usage policies**: Adopt or update written protocols governing attorney and staff use of generative AI, consistent with [ABA Formal Opinion 512](https://www.americanbar.org/news/abanews/aba-news-archives/2024/07/aba-issues-first-ethics-guidance-ai-tools/) and applicable state bar guidance.
- **Train on AI limitations**: Ensure all attorneys and paralegals understand what AI hallucinations are, why they occur, and how to detect them — particularly in citation-dense legal research contexts.
- **Review local court AI rules**: Check whether the courts in which you practice have adopted local AI disclosure or certification rules; many courts, including several in Virginia, have issued standing orders requiring disclosure of AI use in filings.
- **Document verification steps**: In high-stakes matters, maintain a record of the verification process used to confirm AI-generated citations so the firm can demonstrate reasonable inquiry if a court questions a filing.
- **Monitor the Virginia State Bar**: Watch for formal ethics opinions building on the August 2024 guidance, particularly regarding AI billing practices and supervisory responsibilities.
