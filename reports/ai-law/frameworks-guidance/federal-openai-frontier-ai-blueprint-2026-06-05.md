# OpenAI Releases Blueprint for Democratic Governance of Frontier AI, Calls on Congress to Create Federal Institution

**Jurisdiction:** Federal | **Category:** AI Law | **Date:** June 5, 2026

## Summary [HIGH confidence]

On June 3, 2026, OpenAI published a nine-page policy document titled ["Democratic Governance of Frontier AI: A Blueprint for a Federal Framework"](https://cdn.openai.com/pdf/25752ecb-0e5c-47f9-b9e4-c0f4d76f8d3d/a-blueprint-for-a-federal-framework.pdf), urging Congress to enact binding federal AI safety legislation and establishing the Center for AI Standards and Innovation (CAISI) as the government's primary evaluation institution for frontier models. The blueprint explicitly diverges from the Trump administration's [executive order on AI Innovation and Security](https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2026/06/promoting-advanced-artificial-intelligence-innovation-and-security/) (signed the day before, on June 2) by advocating for civilian, not NSA-led, oversight of AI safety evaluations. Released in close temporal proximity to the House's discussion draft of the [Great American AI Act](https://www.dlapiper.com/en-us/insights/publications/2026/06/unpacking-the-great-american-ai-act), the blueprint marks a significant shift in OpenAI's posture: from rule-follower to active institutional architect.

## Key Facts [HIGH confidence]

- OpenAI released the blueprint on June 3, 2026, one day after President Trump signed an executive order on AI innovation and security — a deliberate temporal alignment that positions the company as an alternative voice in the federal governance debate. ([SiliconANGLE](https://siliconangle.com/2026/06/03/policy-paper-openai-diverges-white-house-ai-safety/))
- The nine-page document is publicly available as a [PDF from OpenAI's CDN](https://cdn.openai.com/pdf/25752ecb-0e5c-47f9-b9e4-c0f4d78f8d3d/a-blueprint-for-a-federal-framework.pdf) and summarized on [OpenAI's official policy page](https://openai.com/index/frontier-safety-blueprint/).
- The blueprint outlines a three-part strategy: (1) enact a national framework absorbing consensus rules from existing state frontier AI safety laws; (2) strengthen CAISI as the federal government's primary institution for frontier AI safety; and (3) mobilize a resilience plan across government to address national security risks from frontier AI. ([OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/frontier-safety-blueprint/))
- OpenAI proposes that policymakers **require** the most capable frontier models to undergo CAISI evaluation before public release — but explicitly argues CAISI's role should be to "evaluate and recommend mitigations," not to approve or block deployments. Developers would retain final release authority. ([OpenAI Blueprint PDF](https://cdn.openai.com/pdf/25752ecb-0e5c-47f9-b9e4-c0f4d76f8d3d/a-blueprint-for-a-federal-framework.pdf))
- The blueprint calls for **mandatory incident reporting** for critical incidents involving deployed models, including dangerous model behavior and unauthorized access to sensitive model weights. ([OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/frontier-safety-blueprint/))
- OpenAI supports **international governance bodies** to coordinate around frontier AI risks, safeguards, and security incidents — a call for multilateral coordination beyond the US domestic framework. ([OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/frontier-safety-blueprint/))
- OpenAI supports **federal preemption of state laws** that regulate the same frontier safety risks covered by the proposed federal framework — a "reverse federalism" model in which states pioneer rules and Washington absorbs the consensus before switching off divergent state provisions. ([implicator.ai](https://www.implicator.ai/openai-asks-congress-to-federalize-state-ai-laws-then-preempt-them/))
- OpenAI executive Chris Lehane publicly stated the company is "pleased" with the Trump executive order but will push for transferring evaluation authority from the NSA to CAISI, arguing CAISI has the "sophisticated testing" infrastructure needed. ([SiliconANGLE](https://siliconangle.com/2026/06/03/policy-paper-openai-diverges-white-house-ai-safety/))
- The blueprint asserts that "Democratic governments — not private companies acting alone — must ultimately determine the rules, safeguards, and accountability mechanisms" — an unusual concession from a company whose market position benefits from regulatory clarity. ([OpenAI Blueprint PDF](https://cdn.openai.com/pdf/25752ecb-0e5c-47f9-b9e4-c0f4d76f8d3d/a-blueprint-for-a-federal-framework.pdf))

## Divergence from White House Approach [HIGH confidence]

The blueprint diverges from the Trump administration on a central institutional question: who evaluates frontier AI models. The June 2, 2026 executive order, ["Promoting Advanced Artificial Intelligence Innovation and Security"](https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2026/06/promoting-advanced-artificial-intelligence-innovation-and-security/), placed the NSA in charge of evaluating potential AI risks and established a voluntary pre-release engagement window — initially proposed at 90 days, reduced to 30 days in the final order after industry pushback. OpenAI's blueprint, published the following day, counters that civilian agency CAISI — housed within the Commerce Department's NIST — is better suited for this role, having already developed close working relationships with both OpenAI and Anthropic during prior evaluation efforts. ([SiliconANGLE](https://siliconangle.com/2026/06/03/policy-paper-openai-diverges-white-house-ai-safety/))

The White House approach is also voluntary, with the executive order explicitly disclaiming any licensing, pre-clearance, or permitting requirement. OpenAI's blueprint, by contrast, calls for mandatory pre-release CAISI evaluation for the most capable models — a harder regulatory line than the current administration is willing to draw. ([Akin Gump](https://www.akingump.com/en/insights/alerts/trump-administration-and-house-lawmakers-launch-new-ai-governance-initiatives))

## Context: Relationship to the Great American AI Act [HIGH confidence]

The blueprint was released one day before the House bipartisan discussion draft of the [Great American Artificial Intelligence Act](https://www.dlapiper.com/en-us/insights/publications/2026/06/unpacking-the-great-american-ai-act) was circulated on June 4, 2026. The proposed legislation — sponsored by Representatives Jay Obernolte (R-CA) and Lori Trahan (D-MA) — shares significant structural overlap with OpenAI's blueprint:

- Both center CAISI as the primary federal AI safety institution and direct it to certify independent verification organizations.
- Both apply binding requirements only to "large frontier developers" — companies with more than $500 million in annual AI revenue.
- Both contemplate federal preemption of state AI safety laws, though the bill's discussion draft imposes a three-year freeze on state regulation of frontier model development.
- The bill would codify mandatory transparency and risk assessment frameworks for frontier developers, analogous to OpenAI's call for mandatory reporting. ([FedScoop](https://fedscoop.com/bipartisan-great-american-ai-act-draft-proposes-new-federal-ai-governance-framework/))

The temporal proximity of OpenAI's blueprint and the House discussion draft — separated by a single day — suggests coordination or at minimum mutual reinforcement between the company's policy agenda and the congressional effort.

## Compliance and Strategic Implications [MEDIUM confidence]

- **For frontier AI developers (>$500M revenue):** The blueprint's proposals, if enacted through legislation like the Great American AI Act, would require mandatory pre-release model evaluations by CAISI, risk framework disclosures, and incident reporting obligations. Developers should track both the Great American AI Act's progress and any formal rulemaking from CAISI.
- **For state regulators:** OpenAI's explicit support for federal preemption of state frontier AI safety laws — consistent with the Administration's general stance — signals coordinated industry pressure on state-level frontier AI legislation. States with enacted or pending frontier AI safety bills (notably California, Colorado, Connecticut, and Illinois) face heightened preemption risk.
- **For compliance teams:** The mandatory incident reporting proposal mirrors existing frameworks in cybersecurity (e.g., CIRCIA). Compliance professionals familiar with critical infrastructure incident reporting should begin mapping those protocols to AI incident scenarios.
- **On international coordination:** OpenAI's call for international governance bodies on frontier AI is aspirational and lacks a concrete mechanism; the UK's AI Security Institute is the most developed international analog. ([Resultsense](https://www.resultsense.com/insights/2026-06-04-openai-frontier-ai-federal-framework-uk-implications/))

## Action Items

- **Monitor Congressional progress** on the Great American AI Act discussion draft. The bill has not yet been formally introduced; track for formal introduction and committee markup dates.
- **Assess threshold exposure:** Determine whether your organization meets the proposed "large frontier developer" threshold ($500M annual AI revenue) that triggers the most demanding obligations under both the blueprint and the draft legislation.
- **Map state-level obligations:** Identify any existing compliance programs built around state frontier AI safety laws (California, Colorado, Connecticut). Begin scenario planning for a federal preemption event that could invalidate those state-specific workflows.
- **Track CAISI capacity and authority:** The June 2 executive order and the OpenAI blueprint pull in opposite directions on CAISI's institutional role. Monitor agency announcements, appropriations riders, and any forthcoming Commerce Department rulemaking for signals on which model prevails.
- **Build incident reporting infrastructure:** If mandatory AI incident reporting is codified (either through legislation or CAISI rulemaking), organizations will need classification criteria, response protocols, and reporting pipelines. Begin scoping this infrastructure now.
