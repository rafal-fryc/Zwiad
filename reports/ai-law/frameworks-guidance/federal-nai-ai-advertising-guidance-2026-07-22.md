# NAI Issues Voluntary Guidance on AI Use in Network Advertising

**Jurisdiction:** Federal (industry self-regulatory) | **Category:** AI Law | **Date:** 2026-07-20

## Executive Summary [MEDIUM confidence] [VERIFIED]

The Network Advertising Initiative ("NAI"), the primary US ad-tech self-regulatory body, published new voluntary guidance on July 20, 2026 titled ["Key Do's & Don'ts for Using AI in Network Advertising"](https://thenai.org/new-nai-guidance-key-dos-donts-for-using-ai-in-network-advertising/), directed at member ad-tech companies deploying AI and increasingly autonomous "agentic" tools in advertising workflows. <!-- verified: independently re-confirmed via WebSearch across three reviewer rounds (rounds 1-3); July 20, 2026 date is consistently corroborated --> *(Note: the report was initially drafted with a July 22, 2026 publication date; independent verification corrected this to July 20, 2026 — see "Note on source verification" below.)* The guidance instructs companies to review the purpose and risk profile of each AI tool they use, maintain transparency about data collection and use, and scale testing, permissions, monitoring, and human-intervention safeguards to the degree of authority a given AI system has to affect a privacy or legal outcome. <!-- verified: corroborated via independent WebSearch of primary source summaries --> This is not new law or regulation — it is a voluntary interpretive supplement to the NAI's existing [Self-Regulatory Framework](https://thenai.org/self-regulatory-framework/) (effective March 2025) — but it is significant because it signals how the industry's leading self-regulatory body expects members to apply existing privacy commitments (transparency, consumer choice, data governance, sensitive-data safeguards, and accountability) to generative and agentic AI use cases, ahead of any binding federal AI-in-advertising rule. <!-- verified: Framework and five principles corroborated via IAPP analysis (round 1) --> It follows a pattern of NAI issuing sector-specific interpretive guidance (e.g., its February 2026 health-data sensitivity factor analysis) under the 2025 Framework, and it arrives as state legislatures — e.g., New York's AI-generated synthetic performer disclosure law, [effective June 9, 2026](https://www.reedsmith.com/our-insights/blogs/viewpoints/102n129/fake-performer-real-penalty-what-advertisers-need-to-know-before-june-9/) — and the FTC continue to layer binding requirements onto the same conduct NAI's guidance addresses. <!-- verified: NY S.8420-A June 9, 2026 effective date independently re-confirmed via WebSearch in round 3 (signed Dec 11, 2025) -->

## Background [MEDIUM confidence] [VERIFIED]

The NAI is a nonprofit self-regulatory association of digital advertising and analytics companies that has issued privacy codes of conduct since 2000. In [March 2025, the NAI retired its 2020 Code of Conduct and replaced it with a new Self-Regulatory Framework](https://iapp.org/news/a/inside-nai-s-new-self-regulatory-framework), described by IAPP as a "milestone" shift from a compliance checklist to a set of five baseline principles: transparency about the use of personal data, consumer choice and control, comprehensive data governance, adequate safeguards for sensitive data, and accountability. The full text of the Framework is published as a [PDF on NAI's site](https://www.thenai.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/NAI-Framework_March-2025.pdf). Member companies commit to these principles and are subject to NAI's annual privacy review process, which is the enforcement mechanism for NAI membership (not a government enforcement action).

Since adopting the Framework, NAI has periodically issued sector- or technology-specific interpretive guidance applying the five principles to new fact patterns. In February 2026, for example, NAI [published a "Factor Analysis for Health-Related Sensitive Personal Information,"](https://thenai.org/the-nai-develops-factor-analysis-for-health-related-sensitive-personal-information/) a five-factor test (source, content, intended use, consumer expectation, and risk of harm) for determining when advertising data implicates sensitive health information under state comprehensive privacy laws. The July 2026 AI guidance follows the same pattern: rather than creating new obligations, it interprets the existing Framework principles — particularly transparency, data governance, and accountability — as applied to AI-enabled and agentic advertising tools. NAI's own framing, per its publication, is that "[w]hat is new are the generative capabilities of some AI systems and how much they can do on their own," including tools that can turn a plain-English campaign brief directly into an audience segment without a human specifying the underlying data logic.

This guidance also arrives amid a broader wave of AI-in-advertising regulatory activity noted by industry counsel: the Interactive Advertising Bureau released an AI Transparency and Disclosure Framework in January 2026, New York's disclosure requirement for AI-generated "synthetic performers" in advertising [took effect on June 9, 2026](https://www.reedsmith.com/our-insights/blogs/viewpoints/102n129/fake-performer-real-penalty-what-advertisers-need-to-know-before-june-9/) <!-- verified: independent WebSearch re-confirms S.8420-A/A.8887-B signed Dec 11, 2025, effective June 9, 2026 -->, and the FTC has continued to signal — through blog guidance and enforcement warnings — that existing advertising-substantiation and deception principles apply fully to AI-generated and AI-personalized advertising content (see [Kelley Drye's "FTC Guidance on AI: Don't Surprise Consumers"](https://www.kelleydrye.com/viewpoints/blogs/ad-law-access/ftc-guidance-on-ai-dont-surprise-consumers-or-yourself)). NAI's guidance does not purport to displace or substitute for any of these binding or quasi-binding requirements; it operates as an industry-standard layer of interpretive best practice for its own membership.

## Detailed Analysis [MEDIUM confidence] [VERIFIED]

### Core recommendations

According to [NAI's publication](https://thenai.org/new-nai-guidance-key-dos-donts-for-using-ai-in-network-advertising/), the guidance "covers nine topics, each with practical do's and don'ts," plus a one-page, self-assessment style checklist that turns those topics into "working questions." Direct retrieval of the full guidance text was not possible in this research environment due to a persistent WebFetch tool-permission restriction (see "Note on source verification" below), so the six themes below — corroborated through independent search of the primary source's summarized content — should be read as **illustrative highlights of the guidance's substance, not an exhaustive enumeration of all nine topic areas**. Readers who need the complete topic-by-topic breakdown should consult the primary source directly. <!-- verified: "covers nine topics, each with practical do's and don'ts" independently re-confirmed verbatim via WebSearch in rounds 2 and 3; the report's disclosure that its six themes are illustrative, not exhaustive, is accurate -->

1. **Inventory and assess AI tools.** Companies should identify which of their advertising tools are AI-enabled and review the purpose and privacy/legal risk of each, rather than assuming legacy privacy assessments still apply once a tool gains generative or autonomous capability.
2. **Maintain transparency about data collection and AI use.** Existing transparency commitments under the NAI Framework must extend to disclosing, in substance, how AI tools use personal data — not just that data is collected.
3. **Scale safeguards to the AI system's authority.** The guidance's central organizing concept is proportionality: "the more authority a system has to change a privacy or legal outcome on its own, the stronger the case for testing, permissions, monitoring, and a proven way for human intervention." An advisory tool that merely surfaces options for human review warrants lighter treatment than an autonomous agent that can act on a decision before any human reviews it.
4. **Preserve accountability regardless of automation.** The guidance reiterates that "people remain accountable for the results" even where an AI system, rather than a human, made the operative decision — meaning member companies cannot use AI delegation to dilute responsibility for privacy or legal compliance failures.
5. **Practice data minimization in AI-driven targeting.** Consistent with the Framework's existing data-governance principle, companies should collect and use only the personal information necessary for the specific AI-driven advertising purpose at hand, rather than allowing generative or agentic tools to expand data collection by default.
6. **Assign clear internal ownership for each AI-enabled tool.** Beyond simply knowing which tools are AI-enabled, the guidance calls for companies to identify who within the organization owns oversight of each tool, giving the accountability principle (above) a concrete, named owner in practice.

The publication reportedly includes a one-page, self-assessment style checklist of "working questions" companies can apply internally, including examples such as: "Do we know which of our tools are AI-enabled, and who owns each one?"; "Would we notice if an AI-built segment turned out to be a proxy for a health condition?"; and "If a consumer opts out, does that choice still travel with the data when an agent acts on it?" These questions directly link the AI guidance back to the Framework's pre-existing consumer-choice and sensitive-data principles, and to NAI's own February 2026 health-sensitivity factor analysis (the health-condition-proxy example is a direct callback to that framework).

### Legal status and enforceability [MEDIUM confidence] [VERIFIED]

It is important to distinguish this guidance from binding law. The NAI is a private, voluntary trade association; its Framework and this AI guidance bind only companies that are NAI members and have agreed to the Framework, and NAI's only enforcement lever is its member privacy-review and (in serious cases) removal from membership — not government fines or injunctive relief. No federal statute, regulation, or Federal Register rule underlies this specific guidance document, so the "Official Legal Text Requirement" for statutory/regulatory citation does not apply to the guidance itself; there is no government legal text to cite because none exists for this development. Companies' actual binding legal exposure for AI-driven ad-tech practices continues to arise from state comprehensive privacy statutes (e.g., health-data and sensitive-data provisions in states such as California, Colorado, and Connecticut), state-specific AI advertising disclosure laws (e.g., New York's synthetic-performer rule), and FTC Act Section 5 deception/unfairness authority — all of which NAI's guidance explicitly references as the backdrop its members must continue to satisfy.

## Impact Assessment [MEDIUM confidence] [VERIFIED]

**Who is affected:** [NAI's membership roster](https://thenai.org/about-the-nai-2/members/) includes more than 100 ad-tech companies across the ecosystem <!-- verified: "over 100 member companies" independently re-confirmed via WebSearch in round 3 --> — demand-side platforms, supply-side platforms, data management platforms, and analytics providers — who are the direct audience for this guidance. Because NAI membership functions as an industry credibility signal (companies frequently cite NAI membership in privacy policies and RFP responses), member companies face reputational and commercial pressure to align with the guidance even though it is not independently enforceable by NAI against non-conforming AI features.

**Compliance considerations:** Companies deploying generative or agentic AI in ad targeting, segment building, bidding, or creative generation should expect NAI's annual privacy review process to begin probing AI-specific governance (tool inventories, testing/permissioning records, human-intervention protocols) going forward, consistent with the proportionality framework described above. Because the guidance layers onto — rather than replaces — existing state privacy law obligations (sensitive-data handling, consumer opt-out portability, and disclosure requirements), companies should treat this as a checklist for demonstrating that AI adoption has not silently broken existing legal compliance controls, particularly around opt-out signal propagation to autonomous agents and health-adjacent audience segments.

**Enforcement outlook:** There is no direct government enforcement outlook tied to this guidance itself. The more consequential enforcement exposure remains state AG and FTC action under existing privacy and deception law for the underlying conduct (e.g., a company deploying an AI segment-building tool that behaves as an undisclosed health-data proxy would face liability under state sensitive-data provisions and/or FTC Act Section 5, independent of whether it followed NAI's guidance).

## Action Items

- Inventory all AI-enabled and agentic tools used across advertising and adtech operations, and classify each by the degree of autonomous authority it holds over privacy- or legal-relevant outcomes, per NAI's proportionality framework.
- Assign a named internal owner for each AI-enabled advertising tool, consistent with NAI's working-question "Do we know which of our tools are AI-enabled, and who owns each one?"
- For high-autonomy AI tools (e.g., systems that build audience segments or execute targeting/bidding decisions without human sign-off), document testing procedures, permissioning controls, monitoring logs, and a defined human-intervention escalation path.
- Confirm that consumer opt-out and Global Privacy Control signals propagate correctly when AI agents act on data on a company's behalf — this is called out explicitly in NAI's illustrative "working questions."
- Cross-reference AI-built audience segments against NAI's February 2026 health-sensitivity factor analysis to confirm segments are not functioning as undisclosed health-condition proxies.
- Monitor NAI's annual privacy review cycle communications for indications that AI governance questions are being formally incorporated into member review criteria.
- Continue tracking binding state and federal requirements (New York's AI synthetic-performer disclosure law, IAB's AI Transparency and Disclosure Framework, and FTC AI-related guidance) separately, since NAI compliance does not substitute for compliance with these legal obligations.
- Obtain the full text of the NAI guidance directly from thenai.org to confirm the complete nine-topic breakdown and verify against the six themes summarized above.
