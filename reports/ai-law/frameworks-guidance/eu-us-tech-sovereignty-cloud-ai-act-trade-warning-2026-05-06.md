# US Ambassador Warns EU's Tech Sovereignty Plans Risk Derailing Trade Negotiations

**Jurisdiction:** EU | **Category:** AI Law | **Date:** 2026-05-06

## Executive Summary [MEDIUM confidence]

U.S. Ambassador to the European Union Andrew Puzder has warned the European Commission that its forthcoming technology sovereignty initiatives — principally the proposed Cloud and AI Development Act (CADA) — are inconsistent with the August 2025 EU-U.S. trade framework and could derail ongoing implementation negotiations. Puzder urged the EU to step back from plans to favor domestic cloud and AI providers and allow the private sector to operate freely, arguing that American AI infrastructure is a prerequisite for Europe's participation in the global AI economy. The warning comes as the European Commission prepared to publish its full "Tech Sovereignty Package" on May 27, 2026, which includes the CADA alongside a revised Chips Act and new public procurement rules restricting U.S. cloud providers from handling sensitive government data. The clash represents the sharpest point of transatlantic friction in digital policy since the August 2025 framework was signed, with European legislators publicly rejecting U.S. calls to revisit Brussels' tech rulebooks.

## Background [MEDIUM confidence]

The U.S.-EU trade relationship has been significantly reshaped since July 2025, when European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen and U.S. President Donald Trump agreed a tariff framework known informally as the Turnberry Agreement. A [joint statement formalizing the framework](https://policy.trade.ec.europa.eu/news/joint-statement-united-states-european-union-framework-agreement-reciprocal-fair-and-balanced-trade-2025-08-21_en) was published on August 21, 2025, establishing a 15% tariff ceiling on European exports with commitments to reduce tariffs on most U.S. industrial goods to 0%. As of May 2026, implementation is contested: the [European Parliament failed to finalize the deal](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-05-07/eu-fails-to-finalize-us-trade-deal-despite-trump-s-demands) at its May 7 session, with Trump simultaneously threatening to raise tariffs on EU cars to 25% for non-compliance.

Against this backdrop, the European Commission has pursued an ambitious digital agenda. The Commission's [Work Programme for 2026](https://www.twobirds.com/en/insights/2025/eu-a-bid-for-tech-sovereignty-drives-commissions-work-programme-for-2026) identifies "digital sovereignty" as a primary strategic priority, with the CADA as its flagship instrument. The Commission plans to propose the CADA on [May 27, 2026](https://www.europarl.europa.eu/legislative-train/theme-a-new-plan-for-europe-s-sustainable-prosperity-and-competitiveness/file-cloud-and-ai-development-act), alongside the Chips Act 2.0 and a new unified EU cloud policy for public administrations.

European digital sovereignty concerns have intensified following the Trump administration's tariff actions and its aggressive posture on data access. The [2026 U.S. National Trade Estimate Report on Foreign Trade Barriers](https://ustr.gov/about/policy-offices/press-office/press-releases/2026/march/ustr-releases-2026-national-trade-estimate-report), released in March 2026, identified GDPR-style data privacy regimes and cloud localization requirements as trade barriers across at least 27 named countries — including EU member states — [per Rethink Trade analysis](https://rethinktrade.org/national-trade-estimate/), framing such policies as barriers to U.S. trade and investment. A U.S. State Department cable signed by Secretary Marco Rubio separately warned that data sovereignty and data localization laws could disrupt U.S. cloud and AI service operations.

Andrew Puzder was confirmed as U.S. Ambassador to the EU and has consistently used his platform to press the EU on its tech regulatory posture. In March 2026, he [publicly called on the EU](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/03/27/big-tech-eu-fines-ai-data-centers-us-ambassador-puzder.html) to stop fining U.S. big tech companies, warning that continued financial penalties could cause American companies to stop investing in Europe entirely.

## Detailed Analysis [MEDIUM confidence]

**Puzder's Warning on CADA and the Trade Framework**

Ambassador Puzder's most recent intervention, reported by the IAPP Daily Dashboard on May 6, 2026, targeted the EU's planned CADA and broader domestic-preference technology measures. His core argument: the EU's plan to preference European cloud and AI products through regulation and procurement rules is inconsistent with the spirit of the August 2025 trade framework, which Puzder characterized as calling for open private-sector competition. Puzder urged the Commission to allow the private sector to operate freely rather than using legislative instruments to tilt the market toward domestic providers.

His position reflects a broader U.S. government stance. The [2026 NTE report](https://sice.oas.org/ctyindex/USA/USTR_Reports/2026/2026%20NTE%20Report%20_%20Final.pdf) frames European cloud-localization and data-governance policies as trade barriers. Puzder [told Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/videos/2025-12-05/puzder-says-eu-ai-regulations-make-cooperation-difficult-video) separately that EU AI regulations make transatlantic cooperation difficult and argued the EU cannot win the AI race by "bringing others down." At the 2026 AmCham Transatlantic Dinner, Puzder made [formal remarks](https://useu.usmission.gov/2026-amcham-transatlantic-dinner-ambassador-puzder-remarks/) reiterating that Europe must allow U.S. companies to bring the AI hardware stack and data center infrastructure to Europe without punitive regulatory constraints.

**The Cloud and AI Development Act (CADA)**

The CADA, as described in [European Parliament Think Tank briefing EPRS_BRI(2025)779251](https://www.europarl.europa.eu/thinktank/en/document/EPRS_BRI(2025)779251), aims to triple EU data center capacity within five to seven years and establish EU-wide eligibility requirements for cloud service providers used in public procurement. Its legal basis is Article 114 of the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union (internal market harmonization), making it a directly applicable regulation across all member states.

Key anticipated provisions include:

- **Domestic-preference procurement**: A unified EU-wide cloud policy for public administrations that would prioritize providers meeting Sovereignty Effectiveness Assurance Level (SEAL) criteria, with potential restrictions on U.S. hyperscalers (Amazon Web Services, Microsoft Azure, Google Cloud) for sensitive workloads.
- **Data center capacity targets**: Requirements aimed at tripling EU data center capacity by 2030–2032, supported by accelerated permitting and public subsidies.
- **Supply chain transparency**: SEAL levels ranging from SEAL-0 (no sovereignty) to SEAL-4 (full EU supply chain), with at least SEAL-2 required for eligibility in public tenders.

The Commission has already piloted this framework. In April 2026, the Commission [awarded a €180 million sovereign cloud tender](https://commission.europa.eu/news-and-media/news/commission-advances-cloud-sovereignty-through-strategic-procurement-2026-04-17_en) to four European provider consortia — Post Telecom/CleverCloud/OVHcloud, STACKIT, Scaleway, and Proximus/S3NS (the latter a Thales-Google Cloud joint venture, awarded at SEAL-2) — explicitly applying the Cloud Sovereignty Framework as selection criteria. Notably, the inclusion of S3NS, which relies on Google Cloud infrastructure, has drawn criticism from European cloud industry bodies as sovereignty-washing.

**EU Plans to Restrict U.S. Cloud Platforms for Sensitive Government Data**

Separately, CNBC [reported on May 7, 2026](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/05/07/eu-commission-cloud-sensitive-data.html) that the Commission is considering rules to restrict member states' use of U.S. cloud providers — specifically AWS, Microsoft Azure, and Google Cloud, which collectively control roughly 70% of Europe's cloud market — for processing highly sensitive public-sector data in healthcare, finance, and judicial systems. The contemplated restrictions would not ban U.S. cloud companies from public contracts outright, but would limit their use for the most critical workloads. Underlying the proposal is concern over the U.S. CLOUD Act (18 U.S.C. § 2713), which allows U.S. law enforcement to compel American companies to produce data regardless of where it is stored.

**European Pushback Against U.S. Pressure**

The European side has not been receptive. [The Register reported](https://www.theregister.com/2026/01/05/eu_us_tech_regulation) that the EU vowed to stand firm as U.S. pressure on tech regulation intensified. German MEP Andreas Schwab reportedly told U.S. counterparts that Brussels officials are "tired of Washington's talking points." In the [European Council on Foreign Relations analysis](https://ecfr.eu/publication/get-over-your-x-a-european-plan-to-escape-american-technology/), European officials have articulated a strategic imperative to escape dependence on U.S. software and platforms, framing digital sovereignty not as protectionism but as strategic resilience in light of Trump administration trade and political pressure.

**AI Act Trajectory**

Separately but relatedly, the EU's AI Act Omnibus simplification process reached a provisional agreement on May 7, 2026 [per IAPP reporting](https://iapp.org/news/a/ai-act-omnibus-what-just-happened-and-what-comes-next), amending the EU AI Act to reduce compliance burdens partially in response to pressure from industry and the Trump administration. August 2, 2026 remains the deadline for transparency obligations (including GPAI model enforcement actions) and non-high-risk AI Act provisions. However, the Omnibus provisional agreement pushes high-risk AI system compliance deadlines significantly later: to [December 2, 2027 for standalone Annex III systems](https://www.consilium.europa.eu/en/press/press-releases/2026/05/07/artificial-intelligence-council-and-parliament-agree-to-simplify-and-streamline-rules/) (such as biometric identification, employment screening, and credit scoring) and August 2, 2028 for high-risk AI systems embedded in Annex I regulated products (such as medical devices and machinery). The Omnibus text still requires formal endorsement by Council and Parliament before entry into force.

## Impact Assessment [MEDIUM confidence]

**U.S. Cloud and AI Companies**

Amazon, Microsoft, and Google face the most direct exposure if CADA procurement restrictions are implemented as contemplated. These three providers together hold approximately 70% of Europe's cloud market. Restrictions on public-sector contracts for sensitive workloads could foreclose a significant revenue segment, and SEAL-level requirements that favor full EU supply chains are structurally difficult for U.S. companies to meet.

U.S. AI companies now have extended runways for high-risk AI Act compliance following the Omnibus provisional agreement: standalone Annex III high-risk systems must comply by December 2, 2027, and Annex I embedded systems by August 2, 2028. Transparency obligations and non-high-risk provisions remain on the original August 2, 2026 schedule. The extended deadlines reduce immediate compliance urgency for high-risk AI deployments but do not eliminate the underlying regulatory obligations.

**EU-Based Technology Companies**

European cloud providers — OVHcloud, STACKIT, Scaleway, and others — stand to benefit materially from CADA procurement preferences and the SEAL framework. The €180 million Commission tender already awarded in April 2026 signals that the Commission is implementing sovereignty-oriented procurement proactively, ahead of the formal CADA legislation.

**Trade Negotiations**

Puzder's warning introduces digital sovereignty as an explicit point of friction in the broader EU-U.S. trade implementation process. While the Turnberry Agreement itself does not cover digital trade in detail, U.S. officials have framed CADA-style measures as inconsistent with the framework's market-access principles. If the EU proceeds with the Tech Sovereignty Package as planned, the U.S. may escalate trade pressure — a risk made credible by Trump's May 1, 2026 threat to raise tariffs on EU cars.

**Compliance Practitioners**

Organizations operating cloud infrastructure in Europe or advising EU public-sector clients need to monitor CADA's final text (expected Q2 2026 proposal, with multi-year legislative process ahead) and the Commission's sensitive-data cloud restriction proposal. The SEAL framework's eligibility criteria will directly determine which cloud service providers can compete for EU public contracts.

## Action Items

- Monitor the European Commission's May 27, 2026 Tech Sovereignty Package publication for CADA legislative text and any immediate implementing rules on public-sector cloud restrictions.
- Track EU AI Act Omnibus final text for revised compliance deadlines and any changes to obligations applicable to U.S.-based AI system providers.
- Assess exposure of cloud service agreements with EU public-sector clients against anticipated SEAL eligibility requirements; evaluate whether joint-venture or EU-domiciled entity structures would satisfy SEAL-2 or higher criteria.
- Review data storage architecture for EU operations in light of potential restrictions on U.S. hyperscalers for sensitive workloads (healthcare, finance, judicial systems).
- Monitor EU-U.S. trade negotiation developments — particularly European Parliament ratification progress and any U.S. tariff escalation responses — for signals of whether digital trade rules will be incorporated into the Turnberry Agreement framework.
- Consult the 2026 USTR National Trade Estimate Report for the EU section to understand U.S. government's characterization of specific EU digital policies as trade barriers.
