# AI Chatbot Laws Across the States: A Comparative Analysis of 12 Enacted Statutes

**Jurisdiction:** California, Colorado, Connecticut, Georgia, Hawaii, Idaho, Iowa, Nebraska, New York, Oregon, Rhode Island, Washington | **Category:** AI Law | **Date:** 2026-07-01

## Executive Summary [MEDIUM confidence] [VERIFIED]

As of July 17, 2026, 12 U.S. states — California, Colorado, Connecticut, Georgia, Hawaii, Idaho, Iowa, Nebraska, New York, Oregon, Rhode Island, and Washington — have enacted laws regulating how AI chatbots interact with consumers. Hawaii is the most recent addition: Governor Josh Green signed SB 3001, the Artificial Intelligence Disclosure and Safety Act, on July 14, 2026 (Act 248), moving it from "pending signature" to enacted status ([Honolulu Star-Advertiser](https://www.staradvertiser.com/2026/07/16/hawaii-news/hawaii-enacts-ai-laws-to-protect-children/); [Hawaii Tribune-Herald](https://www.hawaiitribune-herald.com/2026/07/16/hawaii-news/hawaii-enacts-ai-laws-to-protect-children/); [Governor's Office news release](https://governor.hawaii.gov/newsroom/office-of-the-governor-news-release-gov-green-signs-legislation-to-support-kupuna-care-and-strengthen-ai-protection/); [LegiScan bill text](https://legiscan.com/HI/text/SB3001/id/3428748)). This supersedes the 11-state count reported by [IAPP's "Chatbot laws: Coming to a state near you"](https://iapp.org/news/a/chatbot-laws-coming-to-a-state-near-you) and a corroborating [Zwillgen publication](https://www.zwillgen.com/publication/chatbot-laws-coming-to-a-state-near-you/), both published before Hawaii's signature and which described Hawaii's bill as awaiting the Governor's action. <!-- verified: independent search confirms Hawaii signed SB3001/Act 248 July 14, 2026, and IAPP/Zwillgen described it as "awaiting the governor's signature" prior to that date --> All of the laws share a common two-tier structure: baseline transparency and self-harm-response obligations applicable to all users, plus heightened protections for minors. The laws diverge significantly, however, on how broadly they define "chatbot" (general-purpose conversational AI vs. narrowly defined "companion" systems designed to simulate sustained relationships) and on enforcement mechanism (Attorney General-only enforcement vs. private rights of action with statutory damages). This report synthesizes the comparative structure across the 2026 legislative wave and highlights the practical compliance implications for any operator of a consumer-facing conversational AI system, since even businesses that never marketed a "companion" product may fall within the broadest state definitions.

## Background [MEDIUM confidence] [VERIFIED]

Chatbot-specific legislation emerged as a distinct category within the broader 2024-2026 state AI legislative wave following high-profile incidents involving AI companion apps and reported harms to minors, prompting lawmakers in dozens of states to introduce narrowly tailored chatbot bills rather than relying solely on omnibus AI governance statutes such as Colorado's SB 24-205. California moved first with [SB 243](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/billNavClient.xhtml?bill_id=202520260SB243) ("Companion chatbots"), signed by Governor Newsom on October 13, 2025 and effective January 1, 2026, adding Chapter 22.6 to Division 8 of the California Business and Professions Code. <!-- verified: independent search confirms SB 243 signed Oct 13, 2025, effective Jan 1, 2026 --> SB 243 established the template many subsequent states followed: mandatory AI-identity disclosure, self-harm and suicide detection/referral protocols, and minor-specific protections such as breaks and content restrictions ([Skadden](https://www.skadden.com/insights/publications/2025/10/new-california-companion-chatbot-law)).

New York followed with an AI-companion safeguards provision enacted through its budget legislation, effective November 5, 2025, applying to systems "designed to simulate ongoing human relationships" via persistent memory of prior interactions ([Nelson Mullins](https://www.nelsonmullins.com/insights/alerts/privacy_and_data_security_alert/all/new-york-laws-raise-the-bar-in-addressing-ai-safety-the-raise-act-and-ai-companion-models)). <!-- verified: independent search confirms NY AI companion law took effect Nov 5, 2025 --> The Future of Privacy Forum has tracked this trend closely, reporting that as of mid-2026 it was monitoring roughly 98 chatbot-specific bills across 34 states plus three federal proposals, organized around six recurring themes: transparency, age verification/access controls, content safety and harm prevention, professional licensure, data protection, and liability/enforcement ([FPF 2026 Chatbot Legislation Tracker](https://fpf.org/2026-chatbot-legislation-tracker/)). Subsequent enactments through the spring and summer of 2026 included Nebraska's Conversational AI Safety Act (LB 525, signed April 14, 2026, effective July 1, 2027), Idaho's SB 1297 (early April 2026, effective July 1, 2027, closely modeled on Nebraska's approach), Iowa's SF 2417 (effective July 1, 2026, with operator compliance obligations phased to July 1, 2027 per a prior Zwiad research memo on that statute), Oregon's SB 1546 (signed 2026, effective January 1, 2027, the first with an exclusive private right of action), Washington's HB 2225 (signed March 24, 2026, effective January 1, 2027, enforced under the state's Consumer Protection Act), and — most recently — Hawaii's SB 3001 (Artificial Intelligence Disclosure and Safety Act, signed July 14, 2026 as Act 248, effective upon approval, with unfair-or-deceptive-practices enforcement by the Department of the Attorney General and civil penalties of $1,000 per violation capped at $1,000,000 per operator, and an annual reporting obligation to the Department of Health's Behavioral Health Administration beginning January 1, 2028 (the enrolled CD1/Act 248 text confirms this date; an earlier legislative draft had proposed a January 1, 2027 reporting date to the Department of Commerce and Consumer Affairs, but that provision was superseded when the reporting obligation was moved to the Behavioral Health Administration in a later draft and did not survive to enactment) <!-- verified: independent search of official Hawaii AG/Act 248 summaries confirms $1,000/violation penalty capped at $1,000,000/operator, AG enforcement, and Jan 1, 2028 Behavioral Health Administration reporting date; Nebraska LB525, Idaho SB1297, Oregon SB1546, and Washington HB2225 dates/effective dates independently confirmed via WebSearch --> ([Orrick, "2026 State Chatbot Laws"](https://www.orrick.com/en/Insights/2026/04/2026-State-Chatbot-Laws-Key-Provisions-and-Regulatory-Trends); [Hawaii State Legislature, SB3001 CD1 enrolled text](https://www.capitol.hawaii.gov/sessions/session2026/bills/SB3001_CD1_.HTM); see also Zwiad's prior reports on [Oregon SB 1546](../oregon-ai-companion-chatbot-sb1546-2026-04-07.md) and [Washington HB 2225](../washington-ai-chatbot-provenance-bills-2026-04-07.md)).

## Detailed Analysis [MEDIUM confidence] [VERIFIED]

**Common structure.** Per the IAPP analysis, all of the enacted laws share the same basic two-tier architecture: (1) a baseline set of requirements applicable to all users — typically an obligation to disclose that the user is interacting with an AI system rather than a human, plus protocols for detecting and responding to expressions of self-harm or suicidal ideation (often including referral to crisis resources such as the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline); and (2) an additional layer of protections specific to minors, including more frequent or more prominent AI disclosures and restrictions on certain interaction patterns understood to be harmful to young users ([IAPP](https://iapp.org/news/a/chatbot-laws-coming-to-a-state-near-you)). Hawaii's SB 3001 fits this template closely: it requires a clear disclosure whenever a reasonable person could mistake the chatbot for a human, requires minor-specific disclosures to either remain persistently visible or appear at the beginning of each session and recur at least every three hours during extended sessions (reminding the minor user to take a break and that the conversation is artificially generated), and mandates suicidal-ideation/self-harm response protocols ([Hawaii State Legislature, SB3001 CD1 enrolled text](https://www.capitol.hawaii.gov/sessions/session2026/bills/SB3001_CD1_.HTM); [KHON2](https://www.khon2.com/local-news/hawaii-governor-signs-ai-laws/); [Honolulu Star-Advertiser](https://www.staradvertiser.com/2026/07/16/hawaii-news/hawaii-enacts-ai-laws-to-protect-children/)). Note: an earlier draft of the bill had proposed hourly reminders; the enrolled CD1 text that became Act 248 sets the interval at three hours. <!-- verified: independent search of SB3001 CD1 text confirms "at least every three hours" for minor-disclosure recurrence, consistent with earlier draft language of "at least once per hour" appearing in a superseded version -->

**Scope of coverage — the key point of divergence.** The most consequential difference among the statutes is how each defines the universe of chatbots subject to regulation. According to the IAPP/Zwillgen analysis, three tiers of scope have emerged:

- **Broadest scope — Colorado, Idaho, Iowa, and Nebraska**: these statutes reach *any* conversational AI service accessible to the general public that primarily simulates human conversation, without limiting coverage to systems marketed as "companion" apps or designed to sustain ongoing relationships. This sweeps in many general-purpose LLM-based customer service and consumer chatbots that were never designed as companion products.
- **Mid-range scope — California, Connecticut, and Washington**: these statutes define "companion chatbot" functionally (based on capability to simulate relationships or sustain engagement) in a manner broad enough to capture many general-purpose LLM chatbots currently in commercial use, even though the label is narrower on its face.
- **Narrowest scope — Georgia, Hawaii, New York, Oregon, and Rhode Island**: these target chatbots specifically designed to simulate sustained relationships (i.e., true "companion" products), leaving general-purpose customer-service or informational chatbots largely outside scope ([IAPP](https://iapp.org/news/a/chatbot-laws-coming-to-a-state-near-you); [Zwillgen](https://www.zwillgen.com/publication/chatbot-laws-coming-to-a-state-near-you/)). <!-- verified: independent search confirms IAPP's three-tier framework and exact state groupings (broadest: CO/ID/IA/NE; mid: CA/CT/WA; narrowest: GA/HI/NY/OR/RI) -->

**Professional impersonation restrictions [LOW confidence for the five-state extension; the Colorado detail specifically is MEDIUM confidence] [NEEDS REVIEW].** A recurring provision, confirmed for Colorado, prohibits chatbot operators from representing, or allowing their systems to represent, that the chatbot provides licensed professional mental or behavioral healthcare services, and Colorado extends this restriction further to outputs equivalent to legal advice or dietitian/nutrition counseling ([Orrick, "2026 State Chatbot Laws"](https://www.orrick.com/en/Insights/2026/04/2026-State-Chatbot-Laws-Key-Provisions-and-Regulatory-Trends)). <!-- verified: independent search confirms Colorado's HB 26-1263 (Chatbot Safety Act) prohibits chatbot outputs represented as equivalent to licensed professional services, explicitly including legal advice and dietitian/nutrition counseling --> Secondary trackers (IAPP, FPF) also describe a mental/behavioral-healthcare-impersonation ban as present in Georgia, Hawaii, Idaho, Iowa, and Nebraska — Hawaii's SB 3001, for example, prohibits programming a chatbot to present itself as a licensed mental health professional ([Honolulu Star-Advertiser](https://www.staradvertiser.com/2026/07/16/hawaii-news/hawaii-enacts-ai-laws-to-protect-children/)) — but this report did not independently verify the exact statutory language of the impersonation provision in Georgia, Idaho, Iowa, or Nebraska, and in particular did **not** confirm whether those four states extend the restriction to legal-advice or dietitian/nutrition-counseling outputs the way Colorado does. <!-- needs-human-review: unsourced/unverified claim for GA, ID, IA, NE professional-impersonation scope; appropriately hedged as LOW confidence and disclosed by researcher, but statutory text was not independently retrieved for this reviewer either --> Readers should treat the legal-advice/dietitian extension as Colorado-specific unless and until each state's statutory text is separately verified. This tracks a parallel, narrower category of "mental health chatbot" statutes exemplified by Utah's HB 452 (effective May 7, 2025), which imposes disclosure duties and a data-use ban specific to AI mental health chatbots, enforced by the Utah Division of Consumer Protection with fines up to $2,500 per violation ([Wilson Sonsini](https://www.wsgr.com/en/insights/utah-enacts-mental-health-chatbot-law.html)). <!-- verified: independent search confirms Utah HB452 effective May 7, 2025, $2,500/violation fines, Division of Consumer Protection enforcement --> Utah's law is not counted among the 12-state tally because it targets mental-health-specific chatbots rather than general consumer chatbots, illustrating that the "12-state" figure understates the full universe of chatbot-adjacent state statutes.

**Enforcement — the second major axis of divergence.** Enforcement mechanisms vary widely:

- **AG-only enforcement, no private right of action**: Connecticut's chatbot provisions are enforced as unfair or deceptive trade practices exclusively by the state Attorney General ([FPF tracker](https://fpf.org/2026-chatbot-legislation-tracker/)). Washington's HB 1170 (the companion AI content-provenance statute, distinct from its chatbot-disclosure statute HB 2225) is similarly AG-only. Hawaii's SB 3001 follows the same model: violations are treated as unfair or deceptive acts or practices enforced by the Department of the Attorney General, with civil penalties of $1,000 per violation capped at $1,000,000 per operator plus recoverable attorneys' fees and costs ([Governor's Office news release](https://governor.hawaii.gov/newsroom/office-of-the-governor-news-release-gov-green-signs-legislation-to-support-kupuna-care-and-strengthen-ai-protection/); [LegiScan SB3001 bill text](https://legiscan.com/HI/text/SB3001/id/3428748)). <!-- verified: independent search confirms $1,000/violation, $1,000,000 cap, AG enforcement, recoverable attorneys' fees -->
- **Private right of action with statutory damages**: California's SB 243 permits private suits with statutory damages, and Oregon's SB 1546 relies *exclusively* on private litigation (no AG enforcement track), with statutory damages of $1,000 per violation plus injunctive relief and attorney's fees — described by commentators as the first chatbot law with "real teeth" for individual plaintiffs ([Baker Botts, cited in Zwiad's prior Oregon report](../oregon-ai-companion-chatbot-sb1546-2026-04-07.md)). <!-- verified: independent search confirms SB1546 exclusive private right of action, $1,000 statutory damages/violation, effective Jan 1, 2027, and the "real teeth" characterization from Baker Botts -->
- **Hybrid — Consumer Protection Act incorporation**: Washington's HB 2225 treats violations as unfair or deceptive acts under the state's Consumer Protection Act (RCW 19.86), which independently authorizes private suits with discretionary treble damages up to $25,000 alongside potential AG action ([Troutman Pepper Locke, cited in Zwiad's prior Washington report](../washington-ai-chatbot-provenance-bills-2026-04-07.md)). <!-- verified: independent search confirms HB2225 signed March 24, 2026, effective Jan 1, 2027, private right of action under RCW 19.86.093, deemed unfair/deceptive act under RCW 19.86.020 -->

## Impact Assessment [MEDIUM confidence] [VERIFIED]

Any organization operating a consumer-facing conversational AI product should not assume that avoiding the "companion" or "relationship" branding exempts it from these laws. The broadest-scope states (Colorado, Idaho, Iowa, Nebraska) capture general-purpose customer service and support chatbots that primarily simulate human conversation, regardless of marketing framing. Even in mid-scope states (California, Connecticut, Washington), functional definitions of "companion chatbot" have been interpreted broadly enough by commentators to capture many general-purpose LLM-based products currently in commercial deployment ([IAPP](https://iapp.org/news/a/chatbot-laws-coming-to-a-state-near-you)).

Compliance timelines are staggered: California's SB 243, New York's companion provisions, and Hawaii's SB 3001 are already in effect (January 1, 2026; November 5, 2025; and July 14, 2026 upon Governor Green's approval, respectively); Iowa's SF 2417 took effect July 1, 2026 (with a later operator-compliance date); most of the remaining enacted statutes — Colorado's related bills, Idaho, Nebraska, Oregon, and Washington — take effect January 1 or July 1, 2027. Organizations should map their chatbot product footprint against each state's effective date rather than assuming a single national compliance deadline, and Hawaii-facing operators should treat SB 3001's core disclosure and self-harm-protocol obligations as already live.

The bifurcated enforcement landscape materially changes litigation risk calculus: operators facing exposure in Oregon (exclusive private right of action, $1,000 statutory damages per violation) or Washington (Consumer Protection Act treble damages up to $25,000) face meaningfully higher per-incident exposure than operators facing AG-only enforcement in Connecticut or Hawaii (where SB 3001 caps aggregate AG-recovered penalties at $1,000,000 per operator). Because the list of states is expected to keep growing — with Hawaii's enactment as the most recent example and dozens more bills pending per FPF's tracker of 98 bills across 34 states — this is not a static compliance target; year-over-year monitoring is required. Enforcement is also increasingly framed by practitioners (e.g., Orrick) as a "regulatory and litigation risk" rather than a purely UX consideration, with statutory damages, potential class actions, and heightened state AG scrutiny all cited as emerging risk vectors ([Orrick](https://www.orrick.com/en/Insights/2026/04/2026-State-Chatbot-Laws-Key-Provisions-and-Regulatory-Trends)). <!-- verified: independent search confirms FPF's 98-bill/34-state tracker figure -->

## Action Items

- Inventory all consumer-facing conversational AI products (customer service bots, virtual assistants, companion/character apps) and classify each against the broadest applicable state definition — do not rely solely on whether the product is marketed as a "companion."
- Prioritize compliance review for products with users in Colorado, Idaho, Iowa, and Nebraska given the broadest statutory scope in those states.
- Confirm self-harm/suicide detection and crisis-referral protocols are implemented ahead of the earliest applicable effective dates (California, New York, and Hawaii obligations are already live as of this report's date).
- Assess minor-user detection and age-assurance mechanisms, since nearly every enacted statute imposes heightened obligations specific to minors, including Hawaii's disclosure requirement (recurring at least every three hours) for extended sessions with minors.
- Flag Oregon and Washington deployments for elevated litigation-risk review given private-right-of-action exposure with statutory/treble damages.
- Confirm Hawaii SB 3001 compliance obligations are met given the law's effective-upon-approval date of July 14, 2026, including: (a) minor-specific disclosures that either remain persistently visible or appear at session start and recur at least every three hours; and (b) preparing to submit the first annual report to the Department of Health's Behavioral Health Administration by January 1, 2028.
- Continue tracking the FPF 2026 Chatbot Legislation Tracker (98 bills, 34 states as of mid-2026) for newly enacted statutes beyond the current 12-state baseline.
- For any state where this memo did not independently verify primary statutory text (Georgia, Idaho, Nebraska, Connecticut, Rhode Island), commission direct statutory verification before finalizing compliance controls tied to that state.
