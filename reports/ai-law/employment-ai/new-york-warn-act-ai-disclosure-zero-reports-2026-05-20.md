# New York WARN Act AI Disclosure After One Year: Zero Reported AI-Related Layoffs Expose Structural Gaps

**Jurisdiction:** New York | **Category:** ai-law | **Date:** 2026-05-20

## Executive Summary [HIGH confidence]

In January 2025, Governor Kathy Hochul directed the New York Department of Labor (NYS DOL) to update its Worker Adjustment and Retraining Notification (WARN) Act filing system to require employers to disclose whether artificial intelligence, automation, or related technologies contributed to mass layoffs. The updated form — with a new AI-disclosure checkbox — went live in March 2025, making New York the first U.S. state to collect this data. After approximately one year of the requirement being in effect, not a single one of more than 160 companies that filed WARN notices attributed any layoffs to AI or automation. The zero-disclosure outcome is widely attributed to the absence of statutory mandate, undefined key terms, and the lack of regulatory guidance — rather than to an absence of AI-driven workforce reduction in the state. The finding has reinvigorated legislative efforts to place the disclosure requirement on a firmer statutory footing and has surfaced a fundamental challenge for policymakers seeking to quantify the human cost of AI adoption.

## Background [HIGH confidence]

The New York WARN Act requires private employers with 50 or more full-time employees to provide 90 days' notice before a plant closing, mass layoff, or substantial reduction in hours affecting 25 or more workers. New York's law is materially stricter than the federal WARN Act of 1988, which covers employers with 100 or more employees and requires only 60 days' notice. Historically, WARN filings have captured the scale and timing of layoffs and associated economic causes — but not the specific technologies driving workforce restructuring.

Growing public concern about AI-driven automation eliminating jobs prompted a policy response. During her 2025 State of the State Address on January 14, 2025, [Governor Hochul directed the NYS DOL](https://natlawreview.com/article/new-yorks-impending-warn-notice-requirement-artificial-intelligence-related-layoffs) to begin collecting AI-related layoff data from WARN filers. No legislation was required for this initial step because the DOL retained discretion over the content of its WARN filing forms. The NYS DOL updated the WARN system by March 2025, adding a checkbox and supplemental fields to each filing.

This administrative action positioned New York as the first U.S. state to formally track AI's contribution to mass layoffs — a significant milestone in regulatory transparency, but one with notable structural limitations from the outset.

## Detailed Analysis [MEDIUM confidence]

### The Form Fields and Disclosure Mechanism

The updated WARN notice form presents covered employers with a checkbox asking whether "technological innovation or automation" was a contributing factor to the reported layoffs. If the employer marks the box affirmatively, a follow-up prompt asks the employer to specify which technology was deployed — with AI, robotic process automation, and general automation listed as possible categories.

The disclosure mechanism is explicitly framed as asking whether AI was a contributing factor "in whole or in part," a deliberately broad standard designed to capture partial AI causation rather than requiring AI to be the sole or primary driver of cuts.

However, as [Hunton Andrews Kurth LLP observed](https://www.hunton.com/hunton-employment-labor-perspectives/new-york-warn-act-no-ai-related-layoffs-reported-in-first-year-of-adding-ai-related-disclosure-to-the-system), the statutory language of New York's WARN Act was never amended to reflect the new disclosure requirement. The requirement exists only as an administrative update to the form — which creates ambiguity as to whether the disclosure is legally mandatory or effectively voluntary. No enforcement mechanism or penalty has been attached to non-disclosure in the absence of a statutory amendment.

### The First-Year Outcome: Zero Reports

Between March 2025 and early 2026, [162 companies filed WARN notices with the NYS DOL](https://natlawreview.com/article/new-york-warn-act-no-ai-related-layoffs-reported-first-year-adding-ai-related). Not one of those filers checked the AI/automation disclosure box or attributed any layoffs to technological displacement.

This finding acquires additional significance when placed alongside national data. According to [Challenger, Gray and Christmas](https://www.challengergray.com/blog/2025-year-end-challenger-report-highest-q4-layoffs-since-2008-lowest-ytd-hiring-since-2010/), AI or automation was cited as a contributing reason for more than 54,000 planned job cuts nationally in 2025 — the second-most frequently cited reason for layoffs across all industries. That national figure stands in direct contrast to the zero figure recorded in New York's WARN system.

The gap is not plausibly explained by a New York-specific absence of AI-related restructuring. Bloomberg Law reported that [Amazon filed 660 New York WARN notices](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/daily-labor-report/ai-related-layoffs-test-new-yorks-ability-to-track-job-losses) during this period attributing cuts to "economic conditions" — even as Amazon's CEO Andy Jassy had publicly linked the company's accelerating AI adoption to future workforce changes. Similarly, Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley both attributed New York filings to economic reasons in formal WARN notices while investor communications simultaneously referenced AI productivity gains as a driver of headcount efficiency.

### Why Employers Are Not Disclosing

Practitioners and legal commentators have identified several interacting reasons for systematic non-disclosure:

**1. Legal ambiguity about mandatory versus optional disclosure.** Because the WARN Act statute itself was not amended, the disclosure requirement rests solely on administrative form design. [Kaufman Dolowich attorneys](https://www.kaufmandolowich.com/news-resources/new-york-amends-warn-act-to-require-disclosure-of-ai-related-layoffs-by-keith-j-gutstein-esq-and-shiddhartha-uddin-esq-8-4-2025/) and others have advised clients that, without a statutory basis and without an enforcement mechanism, the disclosure may be treated as optional. Absent penalty exposure, the rational incentive for many employers — especially those with complex multi-factor restructurings — is non-disclosure.

**2. Undefined key terms.** The NYS DOL has not published formal guidance defining "artificial intelligence," "robotic process," or "automation" for purposes of the WARN form. This definitional vacuum makes it genuinely difficult for employers operating AI-augmented workflows to determine whether their specific deployments trigger the checkbox. Legal counsel conservatively advised clients to decline to check a box whose scope they cannot reliably assess.

**3. Causal attribution complexity.** Modern AI-driven restructurings rarely result in a one-to-one replacement of workers by machines. More commonly, productivity gains from AI tools allow companies to achieve the same output with fewer people during natural attrition cycles, or to consolidate roles over time. Employers argue — with some justification — that categorizing such reductions as "AI-related" requires a causal inference that existing guidance does not support.

**4. Reputational and litigation risk from disclosure.** Checking the AI box may expose employers to heightened scrutiny in WARN Act litigation, discrimination claims, or political backlash. Until the disclosure framework is settled and normalized, disclosure is perceived as creating asymmetric risk.

**5. Structural mismatch between WARN and gradual AI displacement.** Policy experts have noted that the WARN Act was designed for the factory-closing era of the 1970s and 1980s — abrupt, geographically concentrated mass layoffs — and is an imperfect vessel for capturing the gradual, distributed, and often ambiguous workforce impacts of AI.

### Legislative Proposals to Strengthen the Framework

The zero-disclosure outcome has produced two significant legislative responses in the 2025-2026 session:

**S8928 — Artificial Intelligence Workforce Impact Transparency Act:** Introduced by Senator Cooney, [S8928](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/bills/2025/S8928) would amend the Labor Law to place the AI disclosure requirement on a statutory footing. The bill requires that each WARN notice include a statement as to whether the employment losses described are the result, in whole or in part, of "the introduction, expansion, or adoption of artificial intelligence systems, automation technologies, or machine-based processes that have replaced or materially altered the duties of affected employees." The bill also establishes a two-year pilot program — the AI Innovation and Workforce Tracking Initiative — requiring the DOL to maintain a database of disclosed AI-related layoffs and publish quarterly summaries to inform retraining programs and workforce policy. As of January 2026, S8928 had been referred to the Senate Committee on Labor.

**Automation Displacement Protection Act (pending):** A separate pending measure would require employers with 50 or more full-time employees to provide advance notice when employees are affected by a "technological displacement," with implications for severance and retraining support. This bill would operate parallel to, rather than within, the WARN Act framework.

## Impact Assessment [MEDIUM confidence]

### Who Is Affected

All New York private employers with 50 or more full-time employees who are subject to the NY WARN Act are nominally covered by the existing administrative disclosure requirement. This includes technology companies, financial services firms, professional services organizations, and manufacturers — all sectors that have publicly acknowledged AI-driven workforce restructuring in parallel channels.

If S8928 or the Automation Displacement Protection Act is enacted, the statutory scope and enforcement weight would increase materially, and the current administrative ambiguity would be resolved.

### Compliance Outlook

Under current administrative-only form requirements, enforcement risk is low because no statutory penalty has been attached to non-disclosure. However, the legal landscape is shifting rapidly. A statutory amendment would change the calculus entirely, and the pilot program contemplated by S8928 — with quarterly public summaries — would increase transparency and likely generate political and reputational pressure that functions as a soft enforcement mechanism.

The "contributing factor" standard embedded in both the current form and S8928 is broad. If enacted as statute, employers will need to assess their AI adoption programs against WARN Act obligations proactively — not merely at the point of announcing layoffs.

### Broader Policy Implications

New York's experience illustrates a recurring challenge in AI accountability regulation: disclosure requirements without clear definitions, enforcement mechanisms, and causal standards are unlikely to generate reliable data. The zero-disclosure result, while predictable given the structural gaps in the current framework, is politically significant because it publicly demonstrates the inadequacy of voluntary or ambiguously mandatory disclosure and provides justification for the stronger statutory approach in S8928.

The experience also provides a cautionary data point for other states considering similar AI-related workforce disclosure requirements. Without clear definitions of "AI" and "automation," mandatory legal footing, and a coherent causal attribution standard, similar initiatives are likely to produce similar zero-disclosure outcomes.

## What To Watch [LOW confidence]

- **S8928 legislative progress:** Committee votes and floor action in the 2026 legislative session are the primary near-term indicator of whether New York will convert the administrative disclosure into an enforceable statutory obligation. The bill's AI Innovation and Workforce Tracking Initiative pilot program depends on passage.
- **NYS DOL guidance:** Whether the DOL issues formal definitions of "AI," "automation," and "robotic process" without waiting for legislation will determine whether voluntary compliance improves in 2026 filings.
- **Enforcement posture:** Any DOL attempt to treat the current administrative checkbox as mandatory — even absent statutory amendment — would likely prompt legal challenge, clarifying the precise legal footing of the requirement.
- **National replication:** Other states watching New York's experience may condition any similar initiative on statutory mandate rather than administrative action alone, informed by the zero-report outcome.
- **Annual employer report proposals:** Separate legislative proposals would require large New York employers (100+ employees or publicly traded) to file annual reports with the DOL on AI's impact on hiring, workforce reductions, and unfilled positions — a more robust data-collection mechanism than the current WARN Act checkbox.

## Action Items

- New York employers subject to the WARN Act should immediately confirm whether their WARN filing procedures include the AI/automation checkbox, and should seek legal counsel on how to assess whether pending layoffs involve AI-contributing factors, given the current definitional ambiguity.
- Compliance teams should monitor S8928 and the Automation Displacement Protection Act for committee votes and floor action in the 2026 session; passage of either bill would convert the current ambiguous administrative requirement into an enforceable statutory obligation.
- Employers undertaking AI-driven workforce restructuring should document their internal analysis of whether AI is a contributing factor to any headcount reductions, to support defensible future WARN Act disclosures and to prepare for stronger requirements if S8928 is enacted.
- HR and legal teams should flag the "contributing factor" standard as materially lower than a "sole cause" standard — meaning any meaningful role AI plays in reducing headcount, even as one of several reasons, may trigger disclosure once statutory requirements are in place.
- Policy teams should track the NYS DOL's rollout of the AI Innovation and Workforce Tracking Initiative pilot program if S8928 advances, as quarterly public summaries will create reputational transparency that functions as a de facto enforcement mechanism.
