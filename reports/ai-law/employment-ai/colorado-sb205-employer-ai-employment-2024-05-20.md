# Colorado SB 24-205: Employer Liability for AI Algorithmic Discrimination in Employment Decisions

**Jurisdiction:** Colorado | **Category:** AI Law — Employment AI | **Date:** May 20, 2024

> **Supplemental report:** This memo focuses specifically on the employment law implications of Colorado's AI Act (SB 24-205) — statutory tort liability for employer use of AI tools in hiring, performance management, and workforce decisions, compliance obligations for HR teams, and the interplay with federal employment anti-discrimination law. For the general framework overview of SB 24-205, see [reports/ai-law/state-legislation/colorado-sb205-ai-act-2024-05-20.md](../state-legislation/colorado-sb205-ai-act-2024-05-20.md).

## Executive Summary [HIGH confidence]

Colorado [Senate Bill 24-205](https://leg.colorado.gov/bills/sb24-205), signed by Governor Jared Polis on May 17, 2024, makes Colorado the first U.S. state to impose statutory compliance obligations — including tort-style negligence liability — on employers that use AI tools in employment decisions. Employers that use AI for candidate screening, hiring, performance evaluation, promotions, compensation, or termination are classified as "deployers" of a high-risk AI system and must exercise "reasonable care" to prevent algorithmic discrimination against Colorado workers. This standard diverges sharply from federal employment discrimination law: rather than targeting intentional discriminatory conduct (as Title VII does), Colorado's law creates liability for negligence even absent discriminatory intent. The law does not create a private right of action; enforcement rests with the Colorado Attorney General. Penalties may reach $20,000 per violation under the [Colorado Consumer Protection Act](https://coag.gov/resources/consumer-protection/). The effective date is June 30, 2026, following a delayed special session in August 2025. As analyzed by [Littler Mendelson PC](https://www.littler.com/publication-press/publication/colorados-landmark-ai-legislation-would-create-significant-compliance), SB 24-205 "would create significant compliance burden for employers using AI tools."

## Background [HIGH confidence]

### Colorado's Role as the First Mover

Prior to SB 24-205, no U.S. state had enacted a comprehensive framework imposing anti-discrimination compliance obligations on employers specifically in their capacity as AI system users. Existing law addressed employer discrimination through Title VII of the Civil Rights Act (intentional discrimination and disparate impact in employment), the EEOC's April 2023 guidance on AI in employment, and narrow state laws in Illinois (Artificial Intelligence Video Interview Act), Maryland (automated employment decision tools in hiring), and New York City (Local Law 144, requiring bias audits for automated employment decision tools). Colorado's SB 24-205 goes further: it imposes a proactive risk management and disclosure regime on all employers using covered AI in any consequential employment decision, not merely in hiring.

The bill was introduced by Senator Robert Rodriguez, passed the Senate on May 3, 2024, the House on May 8, 2024, and was signed into law on May 17, 2024. Governor Polis simultaneously issued a signing letter expressing concern that the negligence standard "deviates from the traditional method of addressing discrimination," which requires proof of intentional conduct. Full signed text is available at the [Colorado General Assembly](https://content.leg.colorado.gov/sites/default/files/2024a_205_signed.pdf).

### EEOC Context

The federal Equal Employment Opportunity Commission published [guidance in April 2023](https://www.eeoc.gov/laws/guidance/questions-and-answers-clarify-and-provide-a-common-interpretation-uniform-guidelines) making clear that existing Title VII disparate impact doctrine applies to employer use of AI in employment decisions — meaning employers can face liability under federal law if their AI tools produce discriminatory outcomes even without discriminatory intent. Colorado's SB 24-205 reinforces and extends this posture at the state level, but operationalizes it differently: instead of relying on enforcement after a discriminatory outcome is proven, the Act requires affirmative pre-deployment risk assessment, ongoing monitoring, and documented mitigation — independent of whether discrimination actually occurs.

However, the federal direction shifted materially in 2025. President Trump's [Executive Order 14281](https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2025/04/restoring-equality-of-opportunity-and-meritocracy/) (April 2025) directed federal agencies to abandon disparate impact analysis in rulemaking and enforcement. This creates a direct conflict between the federal approach — which now limits liability to intentional discrimination — and Colorado's CAIA, which expressly creates obligations based on disparate impact outcomes. Employers operating nationally must navigate both regimes simultaneously.

## Detailed Analysis [HIGH confidence]

### Employment as a High-Risk AI Domain

Under [SB 24-205 Section 6-1-1702](https://content.leg.colorado.gov/sites/default/files/2024a_205_signed.pdf), a "consequential decision" includes any determination with material legal or similarly significant effect on a consumer's access to, or cost/terms of, **employment, employment opportunities, or employment-related benefits**. An AI system that makes or is a substantial factor in making such decisions is a "high-risk artificial intelligence system."

Covered employment AI tools include, but are not limited to:

- **Applicant tracking systems (ATS)** that screen, rank, or filter resumes and applications
- **Interview analysis tools** using video, audio, or behavioral cues to score candidates
- **Performance evaluation AI** that rates or scores employee output, productivity, or conduct
- **Workforce management systems** that make or influence scheduling, compensation, or benefit eligibility decisions
- **Promotion and succession AI** that surfaces or de-prioritizes candidates for advancement
- **Termination risk tools** that flag employees for performance improvement plans or reduction-in-force decisions

As [Ogletree Deakins](https://ogletree.com/insights-resources/blog-posts/colorados-artificial-intelligence-act-what-employers-need-to-know/) notes, virtually any AI system used in the employment lifecycle — from sourcing through separation — is a candidate for high-risk classification if it makes or substantially influences a decision with material employment consequences.

### Employers as Deployers: The Core Compliance Obligation

Employers using these systems are **deployers** under SB 24-205 — distinct from the developers (typically technology vendors) that build the systems. The law places compliance obligations on both. As a deployer, an employer must:

**1. Implement a Risk Management Policy and Program**
Employers must adopt a written AI governance program aligned with a recognized framework — specifically [NIST AI RMF](https://www.nist.gov/system/files/documents/2023/01/26/AI%20RMF%201.0.pdf), [ISO/IEC 42001](https://www.iso.org/standard/81230.html), or a substantially equivalent framework — governing how the organization identifies, monitors, and mitigates algorithmic discrimination risks from high-risk AI systems. The policy must address the full lifecycle of any covered AI system in use.

**2. Conduct Impact Assessments**
The law requires a written impact assessment:
- Before initial deployment of any high-risk AI system
- At least annually for each covered system in active use
- Within 90 days of any intentional and substantial modification to the system

The assessment must evaluate whether the system creates a risk of algorithmic discrimination, describe steps taken or planned to mitigate that risk, and identify the categories of decisions the system makes or influences. As [Adams & Reese](https://www.adamsandreese.com/insights/colorados-ai-law-is-here-what-employers-need-to-know-about-the-colorado-artificial-intelligence-act-caia) notes, the bill does not require employers to halt deployment pending risk mitigation — but the assessed risks and mitigation steps must be documented.

**3. Provide Adverse-Action Notices to Employees and Applicants**
When a high-risk AI system makes or substantially influences an adverse consequential employment decision — a rejection, demotion, termination, or denial of a benefit — the employer must:
- Notify the affected individual that AI made or substantially influenced the decision
- Explain the basis for the decision in a manner the individual can understand
- Inform the individual how to correct inaccurate personal data used by the system
- Describe the process to appeal or seek human review

This creates a new category of adverse-action notice obligation for employers, analogous to but distinct from the adverse action notices required under the Fair Credit Reporting Act (FCRA) for background checks.

**4. Provide Human Review on Appeal**
Individuals adversely affected by a high-risk AI employment decision must have a meaningful opportunity to appeal and request human review. Employers must maintain documented appeal procedures.

**5. Publish a Website Notice**
Employers must post a publicly accessible notice identifying each high-risk AI system in use, the types of decisions it informs, and how individuals may seek information about those decisions.

**6. Report Algorithmic Discrimination Incidents**
If an employer discovers or receives a credible report that a high-risk AI system has caused algorithmic discrimination, it must notify the Colorado Attorney General within 90 days. This incident-reporting obligation runs to the AG, not directly to affected employees or applicants.

### The Negligence Standard: A Significant Departure from Employment Law

The legal architecture of SB 24-205 is materially different from federal employment discrimination law, a point emphasized by [Littler Mendelson](https://www.littler.com/publication-press/publication/colorados-landmark-ai-legislation-would-create-significant-compliance) and noted by Governor Polis in his signing statement.

**Title VII (federal)** imposes liability for:
- Intentional discrimination (disparate treatment) — requires proof of discriminatory motive
- Disparate impact — requires plaintiff to show the challenged practice causes adverse statistical impact, after which the employer can defend by showing business necessity; plaintiff can then show a less discriminatory alternative exists

**Colorado SB 24-205** imposes liability for:
- Failure to exercise **reasonable care** to prevent algorithmic discrimination — a negligence standard
- No proof of discriminatory intent is required
- No statistical showing by a plaintiff is required (since there is no private right of action)
- The AG must show the deployer failed to use reasonable care; the deployer can rebut with evidence of compliance

This means an employer that cannot demonstrate it followed the Act's compliance steps — risk management policy, impact assessment, adverse-action notice, appeal process — is potentially liable even if no actual discrimination occurred, simply because it did not take reasonable precautions. Conversely, an employer that follows all compliance steps gains a **rebuttable presumption of reasonable care**, protecting against AG enforcement.

As [Regulatory Oversight](https://www.regulatoryoversight.com/2025/09/what-businesses-need-to-know-colorados-first-in-the-nation-ai-act-diverges-from-federal-approach-to-disparate-impact/) summarizes: Colorado has "tied liability to disparate impact and made recurring impact assessments the centerpiece of compliance," while the Trump administration has directed federal agencies to "abandon disparate impact analysis." Employers with Colorado operations face both regimes simultaneously.

### Enforcement: AG-Only, No Private Right of Action

Enforcement rests exclusively with the [Colorado Attorney General](https://coag.gov/ai/) and district attorneys. There is no private right of action under SB 24-205. This is a significant limitation on employee and applicant recourse under the statute itself.

However, the absence of a private right of action does not fully insulate employers:
- A successful AG investigation and findings of algorithmic discrimination could generate follow-on litigation under Title VII, the Colorado Anti-Discrimination Act (CADA), or other theories
- The discovery obligations in AG proceedings could surface documentary evidence (impact assessments, risk management policies, incident reports) that is usable in related civil litigation
- Civil penalties of up to **$20,000 per violation** accumulate per individual affected decision, making systematic failures in high-volume hiring AI potentially catastrophic

### Small Employer Exemption

Employers with fewer than 50 full-time equivalent employees are **partially exempt** from the most burdensome deployer obligations — specifically, the risk management policy, impact assessment, and website notice requirements — **only if all three conditions are met**:

1. The employer does not use its own proprietary data to train or fine-tune the AI system
2. The employer uses the system solely for purposes previously disclosed by the developer
3. The employer makes available to consumers (employees/applicants) any impact assessment completed by the developer

Even with this exemption, small employers must still comply with: the duty of reasonable care, pre-decision notices, adverse-action explanations, appeal procedures, and AG incident reporting. [Ogletree Deakins](https://ogletree.com/insights-resources/blog-posts/colorados-artificial-intelligence-act-what-employers-need-to-know/) notes the exemption is narrow and conditions-based — employers who customize or fine-tune AI tools with their own data do not qualify.

### Safe Harbor for Good-Faith Compliance

A deployer employer that discovers and self-reports a violation through:
- Voluntary internal review
- Adversarial testing or red-teaming
- Encouraged user feedback mechanisms

...and that remedies the violation before a formal complaint is filed, may assert an **affirmative defense** to AG enforcement. This incentivizes employers to maintain ongoing monitoring programs and document their testing activities.

## Impact Assessment [MEDIUM confidence]

### Scope of Employer Impact

Any Colorado employer — or out-of-state employer with Colorado employees — using AI tools in employment decisions is potentially within scope. This includes:

- Large enterprises using enterprise HR platforms with AI components (Workday, SAP SuccessFactors, Oracle HCM, and similar products often include AI scoring and recommendation features)
- Mid-size employers using commercial ATS products (Greenhouse, Lever, iCIMS, and similar)
- Employers using AI for performance management, workforce analytics, or compensation benchmarking
- Staffing agencies and temp firms using AI to match and place workers

The law applies to decisions affecting "Colorado residents" — meaning remote employees who are Colorado residents are covered even if the employer is headquartered elsewhere.

### Vendor Contract Implications

Because developers (AI vendors) have parallel obligations — including providing deployers with documentation of training data bias evaluation, intended uses, and risk mitigation steps — employers should review vendor contracts for:

- Representations and warranties about bias testing and documentation
- Indemnification provisions if the vendor's system is found to cause algorithmic discrimination
- Data sharing provisions relevant to impact assessment requirements
- Contractual commitments to provide developer impact assessments (which small employers may need to rely upon for the exemption)

[Foley & Lardner](https://www.foley.com/insights/publications/2024/05/colorado-artificial-intelligence-act-human-resources-employers/) advises that employers should negotiate for robust vendor documentation as a compliance prerequisite before or at the time of initial deployment.

### Federal-State Tension

The clearest long-term compliance challenge is navigating the divergence between Colorado's proactive disparate-impact-focused framework and the Trump administration's federal posture abandoning disparate impact analysis. Specifically:

- EEOC guidance (under the prior administration) encouraged employers to test AI for disparate impact; the current administration's EO 14281 discourages this
- Colorado requires ongoing disparate impact assessments regardless of federal signals
- The Commerce Department is reviewing Colorado's CAIA under the December 2025 AI EO as a potential "onerous" state law; if listed, DOJ litigation challenging preemption may follow
- Pending federal preemption, Colorado's requirements remain in full force and employers must comply

Employers with operations in multiple states should also track California, Illinois, and New York City — each of which has distinct, partially overlapping employer AI compliance requirements.

### Effective Date and Compliance Window

The effective date is **June 30, 2026**. Following the failure of the August 2025 special session to substantively amend the law, employers have a defined window to:
- Complete AI system inventories
- Complete initial impact assessments
- Implement risk management programs
- Establish adverse-action notice and appeal workflows
- Update vendor contracts
- Train HR and talent acquisition teams

## Action Items

- **Conduct an AI inventory:** Identify every AI system used in employment decisions affecting Colorado workers — from resume screening through performance management and termination — and determine whether each qualifies as a high-risk AI system under the consequential-decisions definition.
- **Complete pre-deployment impact assessments:** For every identified high-risk AI system, complete a written impact assessment before June 30, 2026 (or before next deployment if not yet deployed). Document discrimination risk analysis and planned mitigations.
- **Implement a risk management program:** Adopt a written AI governance program aligned with NIST AI RMF or ISO 42001. Assign accountability for ongoing monitoring, annual assessment cycles, and incident response.
- **Design adverse-action notice and appeal workflows:** For each high-risk AI system, design and test the process for notifying employees and applicants of adverse AI-influenced decisions, explaining the basis, and providing access to human review.
- **Review and renegotiate vendor contracts:** Request developer documentation packages (bias testing data, intended uses, risk mitigation steps) from AI vendors. Ensure contracts allocate responsibility appropriately for developer-tier failures.
- **Evaluate small employer exemption eligibility:** If the employer has fewer than 50 FTEs, assess whether all three exemption conditions are met. If the employer fine-tunes AI tools with its own data, the exemption does not apply.
- **Update job application disclosures:** Notify applicants when AI is used in hiring decisions. Ensure the public website notice is ready for the effective date.
- **Monitor federal preemption developments:** Track Commerce Department review of Colorado's CAIA under the December 2025 AI EO and any resulting DOJ challenge. The outcome could affect whether full compliance is required by June 30, 2026.
- **Track Colorado AG rulemaking:** The [Colorado AG's AI rulemaking page](https://coag.gov/ai/) will publish guidance and regulations interpreting SB 24-205 before the effective date; monitor for rules that clarify impact assessment standards, safe harbor conditions, and covered AI system definitions.
