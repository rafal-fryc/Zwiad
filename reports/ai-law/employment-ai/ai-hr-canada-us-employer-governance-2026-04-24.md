# AI for HR in Canada and the US: 2026 Regulatory Developments and Employer Governance Obligations

**Jurisdiction:** Federal, Multi-state (US), Canada | **Category:** AI Law — Employment AI | **Date:** April 24, 2026

## Executive Summary [MEDIUM confidence]

A cluster of AI employment regulations took effect on January 1, 2026 across North America, creating simultaneous compliance obligations for employers operating in the US and Canada. [Baker McKenzie attorneys](https://iapp.org/news/a/ai-for-hr-in-canada-and-the-us-what-s-new-for-2026-and-what-employers-are-doing) identified four principal 2026 developments: Ontario's AI disclosure mandate under the Working for Workers Four Act (effective January 1, 2026), Illinois's prohibition on discriminatory AI in employment under the amended Human Rights Act (effective January 1, 2026), California's finalized Automated Decisionmaking Technology regulations (effective January 1, 2027 for core obligations), and the approaching effective date for Colorado SB 24-205 (June 30, 2026). New York City's Local Law 144 AEDT regime, which has been in enforcement since July 2023, entered a new phase following a critical audit that found widespread enforcement failures. Canada's federal AIDA bill died with Parliament's prorogation in January 2025, leaving PIPEDA and provincial laws — Ontario, Quebec (Law 25), Alberta, and British Columbia — as the operative frameworks. No jurisdiction currently prohibits AI use in HR outright; all regulate the manner of use and impose disclosure, notification, and governance requirements. Employers with operations in multiple jurisdictions must build layered governance frameworks that satisfy the most demanding requirements in each jurisdiction.

## Background [HIGH confidence]

### Why 2026 Is a Pivot Year for AI in HR

The use of AI in human resources functions — resume screening, candidate ranking, interview analysis, performance management, and workforce planning — has become widespread. [Baker McKenzie's April 2026 IAPP article](https://iapp.org/news/a/ai-for-hr-in-canada-and-the-us-what-s-new-for-2026-and-what-employers-are-doing) notes that AI tools are now deployed throughout the HR stack: ranking resumes, matching candidates to open positions, conducting initial interviews, generating interview questions, summarizing interviews, and producing interview scores and red-flag reports for HR teams.

Legislatures and regulators in both the US and Canada have responded. The key US developments — NYC Local Law 144 (2023), Colorado SB 24-205 (signed 2024, effective 2026), Illinois HB 3773 (signed 2024, effective 2026), and California's ADMT regulations (finalized 2025, phased implementation through 2027) — form an emerging patchwork. On the Canadian side, Ontario's job-posting AI disclosure requirement is the first provincial measure specifically targeting AI in hiring. Quebec's Law 25 automated decision requirements are fully in effect since September 2024.

The federal picture is divergent on both sides of the border. The Trump administration rescinded EEOC AI-in-hiring guidance in January 2025, pulling back the federal floor in the US while state requirements proliferate. In Canada, AIDA died in Parliament in January 2025, leaving no federal AI framework — though new federal privacy legislation is expected to be reintroduced.

## Detailed Analysis [HIGH confidence]

### United States

#### New York City Local Law 144 (AEDT Bias Audit Mandate)

[NYC Local Law 144](https://rules.cityofnewyork.us/rule/automated-employment-decision-tools-updated/), enacted in 2021 and in enforcement since July 5, 2023, requires employers using automated employment decision tools (AEDTs) for hiring or promotion decisions in New York City to: (1) commission an annual independent bias audit, with results publicly posted; (2) notify candidates and employees at least ten business days before using an AEDT, including details of the characteristics evaluated and data used; and (3) provide a process for requesting an alternative assessment.

In December 2025, the [New York State Comptroller released an audit](https://www.osc.ny.gov/state-agencies/audits/2025/12/02/enforcement-local-law-144-automated-employment-decision-tools) finding severe enforcement failures: 75% of test calls to the NYC 311 hotline about AEDT issues were misrouted and never reached the Department of Consumer and Worker Protection (DCWP). The Comptroller found at least 17 potential violations among 32 sampled companies — the same 32 that DCWP had found to have only one violation. DCWP has committed to remediation. Employers should expect more rigorous investigations and increased penalty exposure going forward. Penalties start at $500 per violation and escalate to $1,500 per day for continuing violations.

#### Colorado SB 24-205 (Consumer Protections for AI — Employment Deployer Obligations)

[Colorado Senate Bill 24-205](https://leg.colorado.gov/bills/sb24-205), signed by Governor Polis on May 17, 2024, makes Colorado the first state to impose broad AI regulation covering employment decisions. Employers using AI systems that "contribute directly or significantly" to hiring, promotion, compensation, discipline, or termination decisions are classified as "deployers" of a high-risk AI system. Under the law, deployers must:

- Establish and maintain a risk management policy aligned with a recognized framework (such as NIST AI RMF);
- Complete an impact assessment for each deployed high-risk AI system at least annually, and within 90 days of any intentional and substantial modification to the system ([CRS § 6-1-1703](https://law.justia.com/codes/colorado/title-6/fair-trade-and-restraint-of-trade/article-1/part-17/section-6-1-1703/));
- Notify employees when AI is used in a consequential employment decision and provide a right to appeal;
- Disclose AI use to the Colorado Attorney General upon request.

The original February 1, 2026 effective date was delayed by SB 25B-004 to **June 30, 2026**. Enforcement rests with the Colorado AG; penalties can reach $20,000 per violation under the Colorado Consumer Protection Act.

#### Illinois HB 3773 / Public Act 103-0804 (IHRA AI Amendment)

[Illinois House Bill 3773](https://www.ilga.gov/legislation/publicacts/fulltext.asp?Name=103-0804), signed August 9, 2024 and effective **January 1, 2026**, amends the [Illinois Human Rights Act](https://www.ilga.gov/legislation/ilcs/ilcs5.asp?ActID=2266) to: (1) expressly prohibit employers from using AI that has the effect of discriminating against employees or applicants based on protected characteristics (including using zip codes as proxies); and (2) require employers to notify employees and applicants when AI is used in covered employment decisions.

Draft implementing rules (Subpart J) issued by the Illinois Department of Human Rights require:
- Notice to prospective employees in job postings when AI is used to screen or evaluate candidates;
- Annual notice to current employees, plus notice within 30 days of adopting or substantially updating an AI system;
- Four-year record retention of notices provided.

Civil penalties under the IHRA follow a tiered structure: up to $16,000 per aggrieved party for a first violation, up to $42,500 for a second violation within five years, and up to $70,000 for two or more violations within seven years. Enforcement runs through the IDHR and Illinois Human Rights Commission.

As reported in [National Law Review](https://natlawreview.com/article/illinois-anti-discrimination-law-address-ai-goes-effect-1-january-2026), the law covers recruitment, hiring, promotion, renewal of employment, selection for training or apprenticeship, discharge, discipline, tenure, and terms and conditions of employment.

#### California ADMT Regulations (CPPA / CCPA)

The [California Privacy Protection Agency](https://cppa.ca.gov/regulations/ccpa_updates.html) finalized regulations under the CCPA/CPRA covering Automated Decisionmaking Technology (ADMT) in September 2025. For employment purposes, the regulations treat decisions on hiring, assignment of work, compensation, promotion and demotion, and termination as "significant decisions" triggering key obligations, including:

- A **pre-use risk assessment** evaluating privacy risks against benefits;
- **Prior notice** to California residents before ADMT is used for a significant decision;
- An **opt-out right** (subject to exceptions) from ADMT use;
- A **right to access** information about how the ADMT was used in decisions affecting the individual.

Core employer compliance is due **January 1, 2027**, allowing approximately one year of preparation after the regulations' January 1, 2026 effective date. [Akin Gump](https://www.akingump.com/en/insights/alerts/new-california-regulations-regarding-employer-use-of-automated-decision-making-technology-compliance-required-by-january-1-2027) notes these regulations impose "the most stringent requirements in the United States on employers' use of artificial intelligence and other automated tools in employment decisionmaking."

#### Federal Level: EEOC Guidance Rollback

The EEOC withdrew its May 2023 [Title VII guidance on AI in employment selection](https://www.eeoc.gov/newsroom/eeoc-launches-initiative-artificial-intelligence-and-algorithmic-fairness) on January 27, 2025, following President Trump's executive order reducing federal AI oversight. As [Husch Blackwell notes](https://www.huschblackwell.com/newsandinsights/ai-and-workplace-discrimination-what-employers-need-to-know-after-the-eeoc-and-dol-rollbacks), the withdrawal does not alter the underlying statutory obligations under Title VII and the ADA; it simply removes agency interpretive guidance. State-level requirements therefore represent the operative compliance frontier for employers in covered jurisdictions.

### Canada

#### Ontario: Working for Workers Four Act / Employment Standards Act Amendment

[Ontario's Working for Workers Four Act, 2024](https://www.ontario.ca/laws/statute/s24003) amended the Employment Standards Act, 2000 effective **January 1, 2026**. Employers with 25 or more employees must disclose in any publicly advertised job posting whether AI is used to screen, assess, or select applicants. The required disclosure is a specific statement: "This employer uses artificial intelligence technology to assist in screening, assessing, or selecting applicants for this position." The same disclosure must appear in any associated application form.

As [Osler's analysis](https://www.osler.com/en/insights/blogs/employment-and-labour-law-blog/ai-in-hiring-ontario-employers-grappling-with-new-job-posting-disclosure-requirement/) notes, the scope of "AI" under this provision is broad — any automated tool contributing to applicant screening, assessment, or selection triggers the requirement. Internal postings and postings by employers with fewer than 25 employees are exempt. The act also introduced mandatory pay transparency and vacancy status disclosures.

[Littler's Canadian alert](https://www.littler.com/news-analysis/asap/canada-new-ontario-job-posting-requirements-force-january-1-2026) confirms the effective date and notes ongoing uncertainty about what constitutes "AI" for purposes of the provision.

#### Quebec: Law 25 (Act Respecting the Protection of Personal Information in the Private Sector)

Quebec's [Law 25](https://vidcruiter.com/blog/quebecs-law-25-and-ai-in-hiring/) (Bill 64, fully in effect since September 2024) imposes requirements specific to automated decision-making in Section 12.1: when an organization uses a system to make a decision based exclusively on automated processing that produces legal or significant effects for an individual, the organization must: (1) inform the individual before making the decision; (2) provide the rationale behind the decision on request; and (3) provide an opportunity to present observations and request correction of personal information used.

In 2025, the [Quebec Commission d'accès à l'information (CAI)](https://www.torys.com/our-latest-thinking/publications/2025/06/quebec-privacy-regulator-identifies-best-practices-for-the-use-of-ai-in-the-workplace) issued best practices for AI in the workplace, recommending that employers maintain a written AI policy documenting tools used, their purposes, the personal information involved, risk mitigation measures, and audit results — and that employees be notified well in advance of any AI system being used in employment decisions about them.

#### Federal Canada: PIPEDA and the Death of AIDA

The federal [Personal Information Protection and Electronic Documents Act (PIPEDA)](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/P-8.6/index.html) applies to private-sector employers in federally regulated industries and to cross-border data flows. It does not contain AI-specific provisions, but its requirements for notice, consent, and openness regarding personal information collection apply to HR AI systems. Alberta and British Columbia have substantially similar provincial laws.

Canada's Artificial Intelligence and Data Act (AIDA), contained in [Bill C-27](https://www.parl.ca/legisinfo/en/bill/44-1/c-27), died with the prorogation of Parliament in January 2025. As [McInnes Cooper notes](https://www.mcinnescooper.com/publications/the-demise-of-the-artificial-intelligence-and-data-act-aida-5-key-lessons/), Canada now has no federal AI framework. New federal privacy legislation is expected but its form and timeline remain uncertain as of April 2026.

## Impact Assessment [MEDIUM confidence]

### Who Is Affected

- **Multi-jurisdictional employers**: Any employer operating across the US and Canada, or across multiple US states, faces simultaneous and partially overlapping obligations. The jurisdictions with active requirements as of 2026 are: New York City, Illinois, Ontario, and Quebec (fully in force); Colorado (effective June 30, 2026); and California (core obligations January 1, 2027).
- **HR technology vendors**: Software providers whose tools are used by employers for hiring, promotion, or performance management are subject to obligations in some jurisdictions (e.g., as "developers" under Colorado SB 24-205) and face audit and documentation demands.
- **Mid-size employers**: Ontario's threshold (25+ employees for AI disclosure) and the Illinois IHRA's broad applicability to any employer mean smaller employers are not exempt from all requirements.

### Governance Framework Implications

[Baker McKenzie's analysis](https://connectontech.bakermckenzie.com/the-legal-playbook-for-ai-in-hr-five-practical-steps-to-help-mitigate-your-risk/) identifies a common governance thread across all jurisdictions: alignment between an employer's internal AI policies, privacy notices (or job posting disclosures), candidate and employee notifications, and the AI tools actually deployed. Regulatory requirements across jurisdictions collectively demand:

1. **Inventory of AI tools** used in HR processes — what they do, what data they use, and who makes the final decision;
2. **Discrimination risk assessments** on a recurring basis (Colorado annually for deployed systems, NYC each 12 months, California before use);
3. **Disclosure and notice programs** tailored by jurisdiction — job posting disclosures (Ontario, Illinois), pre-use candidate notices (NYC, California, Colorado), and employee notices (Illinois, Quebec);
4. **Appeals and human review mechanisms** — Colorado requires a right to appeal AI-influenced employment decisions; California provides an opt-out right; NYC mandates an alternative assessment process;
5. **Policy and handbook alignment** — employee handbooks, codes of conduct, and AI governance policies must accurately reflect actual AI practices to avoid creating liability through inconsistency.

## Action Items

- **Audit current AI tool inventory**: Identify every HR system, vendor tool, or algorithm used in recruiting, screening, performance management, promotion, or termination. Map each tool against jurisdiction-specific definitions (AEDT under NYC LL 144, "high-risk AI system" under Colorado SB 24-205, ADMT under California CCPA regs, AI used for screening/assessment under Ontario's ESA, AI influencing employment decisions under Illinois IHRA).
- **Update Ontario job postings now (deadline passed)**: If operating in Ontario with 25+ employees, ensure all public job postings include the required AI disclosure. This obligation became effective January 1, 2026; any posting since that date that used AI in screening without disclosure represents a live violation.
- **Implement Illinois notices before next hiring cycle**: Illinois HB 3773 requires notice to applicants in job postings and annual notice to current employees. Ensure HR has distributed the initial employee notice and that job templates include AI disclosure where AI is used.
- **Complete NYC bias audits**: Confirm that all AEDTs deployed for NYC hiring have a current (within 12 months) independent bias audit with results posted publicly. The December 2025 Comptroller audit signals DCWP will increase enforcement scrutiny.
- **Begin Colorado SB 24-205 preparation for June 30, 2026**: Develop a risk management policy aligned with NIST AI RMF or ISO 31000, identify all "deployer" obligations applicable to your AI use, and plan for the initial impact assessment. Time for remediation before the effective date is limited.
- **Begin California ADMT compliance preparation for January 2027**: Map HR decisions to the "significant decisions" definition, draft pre-use notices, design opt-out processes, and scope risk assessment requirements. Begin vendor due diligence on what information HR technology suppliers can provide for risk assessments.
- **Align governance documents**: Review and update employee handbooks, codes of conduct, privacy notices, and AI governance policies to ensure they accurately reflect actual AI use. Inconsistency between written policy and actual practice creates independent liability exposure.
- **Monitor Quebec CAI guidance and Canadian federal developments**: The CAI's 2025 AI workplace best practices represent the direction of enforcement. New federal Canadian privacy and AI legislation is expected; track its introduction and scope.
