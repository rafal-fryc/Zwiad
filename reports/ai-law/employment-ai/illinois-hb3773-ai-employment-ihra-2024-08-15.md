# Illinois HB 3773: AI in Employment Discrimination Prohibited Under Amended Human Rights Act

**Jurisdiction:** Illinois | **Category:** AI Law — Employment AI | **Date:** August 15, 2024

## Executive Summary [HIGH confidence]

On August 9, 2024, Illinois Governor JB Pritzker signed [House Bill 3773](https://www.ilga.gov/legislation/publicacts/fulltext.asp?Name=103-0804) (enacted as [Public Act 103-0804](https://www.ilga.gov/Legislation/publicacts/view/103-0804)) into law, amending the [Illinois Human Rights Act (IHRA)](https://www.ilga.gov/legislation/ilcs/ilcs5.asp?ActID=2266) to regulate employer use of artificial intelligence in covered employment decisions. The law makes Illinois the second U.S. state — after Colorado — to impose statewide requirements on how private-sector employers may deploy AI systems affecting workers and job applicants. Two core obligations take effect January 1, 2026: (1) a prohibition on using AI that has a discriminatory effect on employees or applicants based on protected classes, including using zip codes as a proxy for protected characteristics; and (2) a duty to notify employees when AI is being used in covered employment decisions. Enforcement runs through the Illinois Department of Human Rights (IDHR) and the Illinois Human Rights Commission (IHRC), with civil penalties reaching $70,000 per aggrieved party for repeat violations. As of late 2025, IDHR issued draft implementing rules (Subpart J) detailing notice content and four-year recordkeeping obligations; those rules remained in draft form as of April 2026.

## Background [HIGH confidence]

### Legislative History

Illinois HB 3773 was introduced in the 103rd General Assembly as an amendment to Article 2 of the Illinois Human Rights Act, which covers employment discrimination. The bill was carried through the General Assembly and sent to Governor Pritzker, who signed it on August 9, 2024. Upon enactment it became [Public Act 103-0804](https://www.ilga.gov/Legislation/publicacts/view/103-0804).

The IHRA, codified at [775 ILCS 5/](https://www.ilga.gov/legislation/ilcs/ilcs5.asp?ActID=2266), is the state's foundational civil rights statute. Prior to HB 3773, the IHRA prohibited discrimination in employment on the basis of race, color, religion, sex, national origin, ancestry, age, disability, marital status, and other protected characteristics, but did not specifically address the use of automated or AI-assisted decision-making tools. HB 3773 added explicit language making it a civil rights violation to use AI in ways that produce discriminatory outcomes, even absent discriminatory intent.

### National Context: The Patchwork Landscape

HB 3773 was enacted amid growing state-level activity on AI employment regulation:

- **Colorado SB 24-205** (signed May 2024): Colorado became the first state to enact broad algorithmic discrimination protections, imposing "reasonable care" obligations on deployers of high-risk AI systems, including HR tools. Illinois joined Colorado as the only states with statewide private-sector AI employment laws at the time of enactment. See [reports/ai-law/employment-ai/colorado-sb205-employer-ai-employment-2024-05-20.md](./colorado-sb205-employer-ai-employment-2024-05-20.md).

- **NYC Local Law 144** (effective July 2023): New York City preceded both states with its Automated Employment Decision Tools (AEDT) law requiring third-party bias audits. See [reports/ai-law/employment-ai/nyc-local-law-144-aedt-bias-audit-2024-05-23.md](./nyc-local-law-144-aedt-bias-audit-2024-05-23.md).

- **EU AI Act** (adopted 2024): Illinois's definition of "artificial intelligence" was drafted to align closely with EU AI Act terminology, signaling an intent to harmonize with international standards.

As noted by [Jones Day](https://www.jonesday.com/en/insights/2024/10/illinois-becomes-second-state-to-pass-broad-legislation-on-the-use-of-ai-in-employment-decisions), Illinois's approach is notably narrower than Colorado's: while Colorado creates a broad "high-risk AI" framework with impact assessment requirements, Illinois focuses specifically on employment discrimination and notice obligations already familiar to IHRA practitioners.

## Detailed Analysis [HIGH confidence]

### Scope: Covered Employment Decisions

HB 3773 covers AI use in any of the following employment contexts, which mirror existing IHRA Article 2 protected activities:

- Recruitment
- Hiring
- Promotion
- Renewal of employment
- Selection for training or apprenticeship
- Discharge
- Discipline
- Tenure
- Terms, privileges, or conditions of employment

This enumeration is comprehensive — it reaches the full employment lifecycle from initial recruitment through separation, meaning employers cannot limit compliance to hiring processes alone.

### Definition of Artificial Intelligence [HIGH confidence]

The law defines "artificial intelligence" to mean "a machine-based system that, for explicit or implicit objectives, infers, from the input it receives, how to generate outputs such as predictions, content, recommendations, or decisions that can influence physical or virtual environments." This definition explicitly encompasses generative AI systems.

The definition is intentionally broad and closely tracks language in the EU AI Act and [Colorado SB 24-205](https://leg.colorado.gov/bills/sb24-205). It covers:

- Predictive analytics tools (e.g., resume screening algorithms)
- AI-assisted candidate ranking and scoring systems
- Generative AI tools used in the recruitment or evaluation process
- Machine learning models used in performance management or discipline
- Any system producing recommendations that influence covered employment decisions

### Prohibition on Discriminatory AI Use [HIGH confidence]

Section 2-102 of the IHRA, as amended, makes it a civil rights violation for an employer to:

1. **Use AI with a discriminatory effect**: Use artificial intelligence that has the effect of subjecting employees or applicants to discrimination on the basis of a protected class identified under the IHRA. This is an **effects-based** standard — discriminatory intent is not required.

2. **Use zip codes as a proxy**: Specifically prohibit use of zip codes as a proxy for protected classes. This provision directly targets a well-documented discriminatory practice in predictive analytics, where geographic data (zip codes correlating with race or national origin) is fed into models in ways that replicate historical discrimination.

The effects standard is significant because it eliminates the traditional intent inquiry from federal employment discrimination law (Title VII). An employer cannot escape liability by demonstrating good faith or absence of discriminatory purpose — if the AI system produces discriminatory outcomes on the basis of a protected characteristic, that is itself a civil rights violation.

### Notice Obligation [HIGH confidence]

HB 3773 makes it a separate civil rights violation for an employer to **fail to provide notice** to an employee or applicant that the employer is using AI for covered employment decisions. This is an independent obligation — notice failures are actionable even if the AI system itself does not produce discriminatory outcomes.

The statute delegates the details of the notice requirement to IDHR rulemaking: the department is directed to adopt "any rules necessary for the implementation and enforcement" of the AI amendments, specifically including:

- The circumstances and conditions that require notice
- The time period for providing notice
- The means for providing notice

#### IDHR Draft Rules (Subpart J) [MEDIUM confidence]

In late 2025, IDHR circulated draft implementing rules, designated "Subpart J: Use of Artificial Intelligence in Employment," to stakeholders. According to analysis by [Ogletree Deakins](https://ogletree.com/insights-resources/blog-posts/illinois-unveils-draft-notice-rules-on-ai-use-in-employment-ahead-of-discrimination-ban/) and [Epstein Becker Green](https://www.workforcebulletin.com/your-ai-in-hr-must-do-list-navigating-illinois-draft-ai-notice-regulations), the draft rules would:

- Trigger notice obligations when an employer "uses" AI "to influence or facilitate" any covered employment decision
- Require notice content to specify: the AI product's name, the employment decisions it affects, its purpose, the data it collects, targeted job positions, and employer contact information for inquiries
- Mandate recordkeeping: employers must preserve notices, postings, and disclosures regarding AI use, and maintain records of such use for **four years**

As of April 2026, the draft rules had not been formally published in the Illinois Register for public comment. According to [Hinshaw & Culbertson](https://www.hinshawlaw.com/en/insights/blogs/employment-law-observer/illinois-adopts-new-ai-in-employment-regulations-what-employers-need-to-know-for-2026), significant changes to the draft rules before publication were considered unlikely, but the finalization timeline remains uncertain.

### Comparison to Colorado and NYC Frameworks [MEDIUM confidence]

| Feature | Illinois HB 3773 | Colorado SB 24-205 | NYC Local Law 144 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Scope | Employment decisions (IHRA-covered activities) | High-risk AI systems broadly; deployer obligations | Automated employment decision tools only |
| Standard | Discriminatory effects; notice obligation | Reasonable care to avoid algorithmic discrimination | Bias audit requirement; result disclosure |
| Intent required | No | No | N/A (audit-based) |
| Private right of action | No (IDHR/IHRC enforcement) | No (AG enforcement) | Yes (limited) |
| Max penalty | $70,000 per aggrieved party | $20,000 per violation | $1,500 per violation |
| Effective date | January 1, 2026 | June 30, 2026 | July 5, 2023 |
| Impact assessment | Not required | Required | Bias audit (third-party) |
| Notice to employees | Yes (IDHR rules pending) | Yes (employees and AG) | Yes (limited) |

As analyzed by [Mayer Brown](https://www.mayerbrown.com/en/insights/publications/2024/09/illinois-passes-artificial-intelligence-ai-law-regulating-employment-use-cases), Illinois's law is less operationally demanding than Colorado's — it does not require separate impact assessments, deployer risk management programs, or annual reporting. However, its focus on discriminatory effects and its integration with the IHRA enforcement machinery (with per-aggrieved-party penalties) makes it potentially more costly for employers in high-volume hiring scenarios.

## Impact Assessment [HIGH confidence]

### Who Is Affected

The IHRA's employer coverage provisions apply broadly. All Illinois private-sector employers using AI tools in any covered employment decision are subject to HB 3773. There is no minimum employee threshold explicitly added by HB 3773 — coverage tracks existing IHRA coverage (generally employers with one or more employees for most provisions). Staffing firms, third-party AI vendors that provide employment decision tools, and employers headquartered outside Illinois but making employment decisions affecting Illinois workers are also implicated.

### Compliance Requirements

**Effective January 1, 2026**, employers must:

1. **Inventory AI tools**: Identify all AI systems used in recruitment, hiring, performance management, discipline, or any other IHRA-covered employment decision.

2. **Assess for discriminatory effects**: Evaluate whether any AI tool in use produces outcomes that adversely impact protected classes — including disparate impact on the basis of race, sex, national origin, disability, and other IHRA-protected characteristics. The effects standard means employers cannot rely on vendor assurances of non-bias without their own assessment.

3. **Eliminate zip code proxy use**: Audit AI systems for use of zip codes as input variables or as proxies for geographic or demographic characteristics.

4. **Provide employee notice**: Notify employees and applicants that AI is being used in covered employment decisions. Until IDHR finalizes Subpart J rules, the precise form, timing, and content of notice remain uncertain — but the obligation itself is effective January 1, 2026.

5. **Implement recordkeeping**: Even before final rules, employers should preserve documentation of AI use in employment decisions. The draft Subpart J rules contemplate a four-year retention period.

### Penalties and Enforcement [HIGH confidence]

HB 3773 violations are enforced through the existing IHRA framework:

1. A complainant files a charge with the IDHR.
2. IDHR investigates and attempts conciliation.
3. If unresolved, the matter proceeds to administrative hearing before the IHRC or to circuit court.

Civil penalties under the IHRA (as amended by separate civil penalties legislation) are assessed per violation per aggrieved party:

- **First violation**: Up to $16,000
- **Second violation within 5 years**: Up to $42,500
- **Two or more violations within 7 years**: Up to $70,000

Because the penalties apply per aggrieved party, an AI system used in high-volume hiring that produces discriminatory results affecting dozens or hundreds of applicants can generate aggregate liability vastly exceeding these nominal per-violation caps. There is no private right of action — all claims proceed through IDHR/IHRC.

The [Illinois Attorney General's office](https://www.illinoisattorneygeneral.gov/news/story/attorney-general-raoul-and-illinois-department-of-human-rights-applaud-passage-of-legislation-to-enhance-civil-rights-protections) applauded passage of the legislation to enhance civil rights protections, signaling enforcement interest.

## Action Items

- **Immediately**: Conduct a complete inventory of all AI systems, algorithms, and automated tools used in any Illinois employment decision — recruitment software, ATS resume screening, skills assessments, performance scoring, discipline flagging, scheduling tools.
- **Before January 1, 2026 (already passed)**: Assess each identified AI tool for discriminatory effects using disparate impact analysis across IHRA-protected characteristics; eliminate or remediate any zip code proxy inputs.
- **Notice program**: Implement an employee and applicant notice procedure covering the use of AI in covered employment decisions. Because IDHR Subpart J rules are not yet finalized, draft notices should include the information anticipated in the draft rules (AI product name, purpose, data used, positions affected, employer contact).
- **Vendor contracts**: Review agreements with HR technology vendors to ensure contractual representations about non-discrimination, access to disparate impact data, and indemnification terms commensurate with IHRA liability exposure.
- **Recordkeeping**: Begin preserving records of AI tool use, notices issued, and impact assessments now — the draft rules contemplate a four-year retention period from the date of each covered employment decision.
- **Monitor IDHR rulemaking**: Track IDHR's publication of the Subpart J rules in the Illinois Register and participate in the public comment process. The final rules will govern notice content, timing, and the specific means of delivery.
- **Federal preemption watch**: Monitor federal AI legislation and executive actions (including the Trump AI executive order on state preemption) that could affect the landscape of state AI employment laws. See [reports/ai-law/trump-ai-executive-order-state-preemption-2026-04-12.md](../trump-ai-executive-order-state-preemption-2026-04-12.md).
