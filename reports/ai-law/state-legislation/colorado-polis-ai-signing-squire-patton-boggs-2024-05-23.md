# All Eyes on AI: Colorado Governor Signs Nation's First Comprehensive AI Law Amid Flurry of Federal AI Activity (Week of May 13–23, 2024)

**Jurisdiction:** Colorado (primary); Federal (secondary) | **Category:** AI Law | **Date:** May 23, 2024

> **Scope note:** This memo takes the analytical frame of the [Squire Patton Boggs "All Eyes on AI" client alert](https://www.privacyworld.blog/2024/05/all-eyes-on-ai-colorado-governor-throws-down-the-gauntlet-on-ai-regulation-after-colorado-general-assembly-passes-the-nations-first-ai-law/) (published May 21–23, 2024), which characterized the week of May 13–23, 2024, as a pivotal inflection point in U.S. AI regulation. The three simultaneous developments — Colorado's Governor signing SB 24-205, the Senate AI Working Group's bipartisan roadmap, and the Department of Labor's AI Worker Well-Being Principles — each have separate in-depth reports in this knowledge base; this memo synthesizes the context and interplay between them. See the Related Reports section for links to the detailed analyses.

## Executive Summary [HIGH confidence]

The week of May 13–23, 2024, produced three major U.S. AI regulatory developments in rapid succession, together representing what [Squire Patton Boggs](https://www.privacyworld.blog/2024/05/all-eyes-on-ai-colorado-governor-throws-down-the-gauntlet-on-ai-regulation-after-colorado-general-assembly-passes-the-nations-first-ai-law/) called the moment when the U.S. AI governance landscape shifted from theoretical debate to binding law. On May 17, Colorado Governor Jared Polis signed [Senate Bill 24-205](https://leg.colorado.gov/bills/sb24-205) — the first comprehensive state AI regulation in the United States — while simultaneously issuing a signed reservations letter inviting Congress to preempt the law with a national framework. Two days before the signing, on May 15, the [Bipartisan Senate AI Working Group](https://www.schumer.senate.gov/imo/media/doc/Roadmap_Electronic1.32pm.pdf) released a 31-page roadmap calling for $32 billion in annual non-defense AI R&D spending and a committee-by-committee legislative approach. In between, on May 16, the [U.S. Department of Labor](https://www.dol.gov/newsroom/releases/osec/osec20240516) published the first federal guidance directly addressing worker rights in AI-assisted workplaces: "Artificial Intelligence and Worker Well-Being: Principles for Developers and Employers." Taken together, these three actions signaled a new phase in which AI regulation was no longer a future prospect but an active compliance obligation, with employers caught between a pioneering state mandate and the federal government's preference for voluntary frameworks.

## Background [HIGH confidence]

### The Pre-2024 Federal Vacuum

By early 2024, Congress had not enacted comprehensive AI legislation. Federal AI governance existed primarily through: President Biden's October 2023 Executive Order on Safe, Secure, and Trustworthy AI (directing agencies to develop guidance but imposing no private-sector requirements); the [NIST AI Risk Management Framework 1.0](https://www.nist.gov/artificial-intelligence) (voluntary guidance published January 2023); the FTC's monitoring of AI-related unfair or deceptive practices; and the EEOC's April 2023 technical guidance on AI in employment decisions. None of these measures created statutory compliance obligations on private businesses.

State-level AI legislation, prior to Colorado, addressed narrow subjects: Illinois's Artificial Intelligence Video Interview Act (chatbot disclosure in hiring), New York City Local Law 144 (bias audits for automated employment decision tools), Maryland's restrictions on automated hiring screens. No state had enacted a cross-sector AI framework imposing general duties of care.

### Colorado's Path to Enactment

Senator Robert Rodriguez introduced [SB 24-205](https://leg.colorado.gov/bills/sb24-205) on April 10, 2024. The bill, titled "Concerning Consumer Protections in Interactions with Artificial Intelligence Systems," passed the Colorado Senate on May 3 and the House on May 8. Governor Polis signed it on May 17, making it effective February 1, 2026 (later delayed to June 30, 2026 by [SB 25B-004](https://leg.colorado.gov/bills/sb25b-004), signed August 28, 2025).

For comprehensive statutory analysis — developer obligations, deployer obligations, exemptions, algorithmic discrimination definition, enforcement by the Colorado Attorney General — see the companion report: [Colorado Becomes the First State to Pass Comprehensive AI Legislation (SB 24-205)](../../reports/ai-law/state-legislation/colorado-ai-act-sb24-205-passage-2024-05-14.md).

## Detailed Analysis [HIGH confidence]

### Colorado SB 24-205: Governor's "Gauntlet" Framing

Squire Patton Boggs framed Governor Polis's action as "throwing down the gauntlet" — not in the sense of issuing a challenge to AI companies, but in the sense of forcing a national policy choice. By signing the law while simultaneously inviting Congress to preempt it, Polis created a dynamic in which the effective timeline for compliance is uncertain: either companies comply with Colorado's framework by June 30, 2026, or Congress acts first and supersedes it.

Governor Polis's formal [signing letter](https://reason.org/commentary/governor-polis-signed-colorados-restrictive-ai-law-but-supports-a-federal-moratorium-on-similar-legislation/) expressed concern that SB 24-205 "could inadvertently stifle innovation" and declared that "the important work of protecting consumers from discrimination and other unintended consequences of nascent AI technologies is better considered and applied by the federal government to limit and preempt varied compliance burdens on innovators." He called on the legislature to "significantly improve" the law before the effective date and urged Congress to pass federal preemptive legislation.

This posture — signing a consumer protection bill while publicly lobbying against it — reflects the political dynamics of the 2024 AI regulatory cycle: state legislators who had built momentum for AI accountability wanted binding rules; governors, sensitive to tech industry concerns, wanted to preserve innovation; and businesses wanted federal clarity over a patchwork of state laws. As [Reason](https://reason.org/commentary/governor-polis-signed-colorados-restrictive-ai-law-but-supports-a-federal-moratorium-on-similar-legislation/) noted, Polis simultaneously supported a federal moratorium on additional state AI laws — a position that would effectively neutralize the very law he was signing.

### Senate AI Working Group Roadmap: Congress Declines to Move Fast

The [Senate AI Working Group Roadmap](https://www.schumer.senate.gov/imo/media/doc/Roadmap_Electronic1.32pm.pdf) released May 15, 2024 — two days before Polis signed SB 24-205 — made explicit what Colorado's action was responding to: Congress was not going to move quickly. Senate Majority Leader Chuck Schumer, joined by Senators Mike Rounds, Todd Young, and Martin Heinrich, released a 31-page document directed at committee chairs to develop legislation sector by sector, but provided no timeline, no draft legislation, and no commitment to any particular framework.

Key aspects of the roadmap:
- **$32 billion annual R&D target:** Calls for emergency appropriations to reach non-defense AI R&D spending of $32 billion per year, tracking the 2021 National Security Commission on Artificial Intelligence recommendation.
- **No omnibus bill:** Senator Schumer stated explicitly that the Senate would not wait for a single AI framework bill; rather, committees of jurisdiction would develop legislation in their respective areas.
- **Eight bipartisan priority areas:** Transparency, accountability, election integrity, national security, intellectual property, privacy, workforce, healthcare.
- **Voluntary emphasis:** The roadmap's overall posture favors voluntary standards and innovation investment over mandatory requirements.

As [Mayer Brown observed](https://www.mayerbrown.com/en/insights/publications/2024/05/senate-ai-working-group-releases-roadmap-for-artificial-intelligence-policy), the roadmap "lacks binding commitments, specific bills, or timelines" and primarily serves as a "call to action for relevant Senate committees." This meant that Governor Polis's invitation to Congress to preempt Colorado's law was, in the short term, an invitation no one was prepared to accept.

For the detailed analysis of the Senate AI Working Group Roadmap, see: [Bipartisan Senate AI Working Group Releases Policy Roadmap](../../reports/ai-law/federal-regulation/federal-senate-ai-working-group-roadmap-2024-05-15.md).

### DOL AI Worker Well-Being Principles: Federal Guidance Enters the Employment Domain [MEDIUM confidence]

On May 16, 2024, the [U.S. Department of Labor](https://www.dol.gov/newsroom/releases/osec/osec20240516) published "Artificial Intelligence and Worker Well-Being: Principles for Developers and Employers" — the first federal guidance document specifically addressing the rights of workers in AI-assisted workplaces.

The DOL principles were issued pursuant to President Biden's October 2023 AI Executive Order, which directed the DOL to develop best practices for employers, agencies, and federal contractors using AI. The guidance is voluntary and does not create legally enforceable obligations. However, it represents the federal government's first substantive statement on how existing labor law applies in AI contexts and signals what future DOL enforcement may look like under any future administration that chooses to pursue AI accountability in employment.

**The eight DOL AI principles:**

1. **Worker and worker-representative engagement** — Workers and their representatives should be genuinely informed of, and have real input into, the design, development, testing, training, use, and oversight of AI systems deployed in their workplace.
2. **Ethical AI development and deployment** — AI systems should be developed and deployed consistent with ethical frameworks that protect workers from potential AI harms.
3. **AI governance and human oversight** — Organizations should establish AI governance structures that maintain meaningful human oversight of high-stakes AI decisions affecting workers.
4. **Transparent AI use** — Workers should be notified when AI systems are used to make or inform decisions that affect them.
5. **Worker protection from AI harms** — AI systems must not violate or undermine workers' rights to organize, wage and hour protections, health and safety protections, or anti-discrimination and anti-retaliation protections.
6. **AI to enhance job quality** — AI systems should be designed and deployed to augment workers' capabilities and improve job quality rather than to increase worker surveillance, control, or deskilling.
7. **Support for workers displaced by AI** — Employers and developers should support workers impacted by AI through training, transition assistance, and advancement opportunities.
8. **Responsible data use** — Workers' data collected, used, or created by AI systems should be limited in scope, used only for legitimate business purposes, and protected and handled responsibly.

The principles are notable for their breadth: they go beyond the narrow "bias audit" frame common in state employment AI laws and address questions of worker agency, surveillance, and data rights. As [Littler Mendelson](https://www.littler.com/news-analysis/asap/dol-issues-artificial-intelligence-principles) noted at the time, the principles "do not create new legal obligations" but employers using AI in the workplace should consider them as a potential roadmap for future DOL enforcement guidance and contractor requirements.

The direct tension between the DOL's voluntary principles and Colorado's mandatory regime is significant: where Colorado requires binding impact assessments, consumer notices, and human review rights for all deployers of high-risk AI systems, the federal government simultaneously advocated voluntary principles with no enforcement mechanism. Employers operating in Colorado faced the unusual position of mandatory state obligations that went further, in concrete legal terms, than anything the federal government was willing to require.

## Impact Assessment [MEDIUM confidence]

### The Compliance Landscape for Employers in May 2024

For employers, the May 2024 regulatory week meant the following:

- **Colorado deployer obligations would become binding law** with a February 1, 2026 effective date (now June 30, 2026): impact assessments for high-risk AI, risk management programs aligned with NIST AI RMF or ISO 42001, consumer notices, human review rights, and AG enforcement with $20,000 per violation penalties.
- **Federal guidance remained voluntary** with no enforcement timeline: the DOL principles provided a policy frame but no penalties, no private right of action, no audit requirement.
- **Congress was not going to act quickly**: the Senate roadmap confirmed a multi-year, committee-by-committee approach with no binding legislation on any timeline.

The practical implication, as [Squire Patton Boggs](https://natlawreview.com/article/all-eyes-ai-colorado-governor-throws-down-gauntlet-ai-regulation-after-colorado) analyzed, is that Colorado's law would function as the de facto national standard for employers until either Congress preempts it or more states enact analogous legislation. Companies with any significant operations in Colorado — or companies planning AI product sales to Colorado employers — needed to initiate compliance work regardless of the Governor's reservations or federal ambivalence.

### Industry Opposition and Governor's Political Positioning

Squire Patton Boggs noted that the Governor's position was unusual: a Democratic governor signing a consumer protection bill while lobbying against it and calling on Congress to preempt it. The political context was that Colorado had emerged as an unexpected laboratory for comprehensive AI regulation — not because it was the most tech-heavy state (that distinction belongs to California), but because its legislative coalition built momentum before industry opposition could organize effectively. Governor Polis's response reflected the tension: he did not want to veto a consumer protection bill, but he also wanted Colorado's tech and startup community to understand that he viewed the law as imperfect and improvable.

The [U.S. Chamber of Commerce](https://www.seyfarth.com/news-insights/colorado-governor-signs-broad-ai-bill-regulating-employment-decisions.html), Chamber of Progress, and Consumer Technology Association had urged a veto. Their objections centered on the difficulty of tracing discriminatory outcomes in multi-model AI pipelines and the potential for over-broad compliance obligations on companies that used — but did not develop — AI tools.

### Post-2024 Developments

The tension identified in May 2024 played out as follows:
- The Colorado legislature's 2025 regular session produced no amendments to SB 24-205.
- An August 2025 special session called by Governor Polis collapsed without substantive changes and produced only a six-month delay ([SB 25B-004](https://leg.colorado.gov/bills/sb25b-004)).
- President Trump's December 11, 2025 executive order directing Commerce to identify "onerous" state AI laws and the DOJ to develop litigation strategies named Colorado's CAIA as a primary target.
- Enforcement remains scheduled for June 30, 2026, absent further legislative action or federal preemption.

For the current enforcement status, see [Colorado AI Act Enforcement Delayed to June 30, 2026](../../reports/ai-law/state-legislation/colorado-ai-act-enforcement-delayed-2026-04-13.md).

## Action Items

- **Employers using AI for employment decisions in Colorado:** Review the DOL AI Worker Well-Being Principles as a voluntary compliance benchmark; even though they are non-binding, they signal what federal enforcement priorities may look like and they track the types of protections that Colorado's SB 24-205 mandates through law.
- **Compliance teams:** The May 2024 regulatory confluence confirmed that federal preemption of Colorado's law was not imminent as of that date. Plan for June 30, 2026 compliance unless and until Congress acts or a court enjoins enforcement.
- **AI developers selling to employers:** Prepare developer documentation packages for deployer customers as required by SB 24-205, covering intended uses, harmful uses, training data bias evaluation, and risk mitigation measures.
- **Monitor the DOL AI guidance update track:** The DOL published updated best practices in October 2024; organizations should track whether future updates create more specific obligations for federal contractors or recipients.
