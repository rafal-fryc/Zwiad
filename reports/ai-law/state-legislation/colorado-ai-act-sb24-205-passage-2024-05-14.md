# Colorado Becomes the First State to Pass Comprehensive AI Legislation (SB 24-205)

**Jurisdiction:** Colorado | **Category:** AI Law | **Date:** May 14, 2024

## Executive Summary [HIGH confidence]

On May 17, 2024, Colorado Governor Jared Polis signed [Senate Bill 24-205](https://leg.colorado.gov/bills/sb24-205) — the Colorado Artificial Intelligence Act (CAIA) — making Colorado the first state in the nation to enact comprehensive AI regulation. The law imposes a duty of reasonable care on both **developers** and **deployers** of "high-risk" artificial intelligence systems to protect consumers from algorithmic discrimination in consequential decisions affecting employment, education, health care, housing, financial services, insurance, legal services, and essential government services. Enforcement authority rests exclusively with the Colorado Attorney General, with civil penalties of up to $20,000 per violation under the Colorado Consumer Protection Act. Governor Polis signed with reservations, urging the legislature to "significantly improve" the law before its effective date; those concerns — along with industry opposition — ultimately produced an enforcement delay and a 2025 special session that further postponed the law's enforcement to June 30, 2026.

## Background [HIGH confidence]

### The Federal Vacuum

By 2024, the United States Congress had not enacted comprehensive AI legislation, despite multiple legislative proposals and significant federal regulatory activity. The FTC had issued guidance on AI and dark patterns; the NIST AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF 1.0) was published in January 2023; and the Biden administration issued an AI Executive Order in October 2023 directing agencies to develop standards. None of these measures imposed binding legal duties on private-sector AI developers or deployers.

In the absence of federal legislation, several states — including California, Connecticut, Illinois, and Texas — introduced AI-focused bills in 2023 and early 2024. Most addressed narrow topics: chatbot disclosure, employment AI, or deepfakes. No state had enacted a comprehensive cross-sector regulatory framework.

### EU AI Act Influence

Colorado SB 24-205 is widely described as inspired in part by the [EU AI Act](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32024R1689), which the European Parliament passed in March 2024 (formally entering into force August 2024). The EU Act adopts a tiered, risk-based approach categorizing AI systems as unacceptable-risk, high-risk, limited-risk, or minimal-risk. Colorado's law, however, differs in scope: it is narrower than the EU Act, requiring "reasonable care" and creating an affirmative defense for organizations following recognized risk management frameworks such as NIST AI RMF or ISO 42001, rather than imposing the EU Act's more prescriptive product-safety requirements.

### Colorado Legislative History

Senator Robert Rodriguez introduced SB 24-205 on April 10, 2024. Additional Senate sponsors included Senators Cutter, Michaelson Jenet, Priola, and Winter F.; House sponsors were Representatives Brianna Titone, Manny Rutinel, and Duran. The Senate passed the bill on May 3, 2024. The House passed it on May 8, 2024. Governor Polis signed the bill on May 17, 2024, accompanied by a formal [reluctance letter](https://www.seyfarth.com/news-insights/colorado-governor-signs-broad-ai-bill-regulating-employment-decisions.html) urging the legislature to "significantly improve" the law's approach before the February 1, 2026 effective date, and calling on Congress to enact preemptive federal legislation.

## Detailed Analysis [HIGH confidence]

### Scope and Definitions

SB 24-205 applies to Colorado-connected conduct involving "high-risk artificial intelligence systems." A system is "high-risk" when it makes, or is a "substantial factor" in making, a **consequential decision** — defined as a decision with a material legal or similarly significant effect on a consumer's access to, cost of, or terms of:

- Employment, employment opportunities, or employment-related benefits
- Education enrollment or opportunity
- Financial or lending services
- Essential government services
- Health care services
- Housing
- Insurance
- Legal services

**Developer** means a person doing business in Colorado who develops or intentionally and substantially modifies a high-risk AI system offered commercially. **Deployer** means a person doing business in Colorado who deploys a high-risk AI system to make, or be a substantial factor in making, consequential decisions about Colorado consumers.

**Algorithmic discrimination** is defined as any condition in which an AI system results in unlawful differential treatment or impact that disfavors an individual or group based on actual or perceived race, color, ethnicity, sex, religion, age, national origin, limited English proficiency, disability, veteran status, genetic information, or reproductive health.

### Developer Obligations

Developers must exercise reasonable care to protect Colorado consumers from known or reasonably foreseeable risks of algorithmic discrimination. Specific duties include:

1. **Documentation to deployers:** Developers must provide deployers with documentation describing the AI system's intended uses, known harmful uses, how training data was evaluated for bias, and what risk mitigation measures were taken.
2. **Public disclosure:** Developers must maintain a publicly accessible statement summarizing the types of high-risk AI systems they have developed, made available to third parties, and how they manage foreseeable algorithmic discrimination risks.
3. **Incident reporting:** Upon discovering algorithmic discrimination, developers must notify the Colorado Attorney General and all known Colorado deployers within 90 days.

### Deployer Obligations

Deployers must exercise reasonable care to prevent algorithmic discrimination. A deployer obtains a **rebuttable presumption** of reasonable care if it:

1. **Implements a risk management policy and program** governing the deployment of each high-risk AI system, incorporating the NIST AI RMF, ISO 42001, or a substantially equivalent framework;
2. **Conducts impact assessments** on each high-risk AI system — (a) prior to deployment, (b) annually, and (c) within 90 days of any intentional and substantial modification;
3. **Reviews deployment annually** to ensure systems are not causing algorithmic discrimination;
4. **Notifies consumers** when a high-risk AI system makes or substantially influences a consequential decision about that consumer, including an explanation of the decision and how to correct data errors;
5. **Provides appeal/human review:** Deployers must give consumers a meaningful opportunity to appeal adverse consequential decisions and to have a human review any consequential decision made by a high-risk AI system; and
6. **Publishes a summary** of all high-risk AI systems deployed.

### Exemptions

SB 24-205 carves out several categories:

- **Federally regulated AI:** Systems approved or cleared by the FDA, FAA, or Federal Housing Finance Agency are exempt. Systems deployed under contracts with the Department of Commerce, Department of Defense, or NASA are exempt unless the decision affects employment or housing.
- **Financial institutions:** Banks, credit unions, and affiliates that are subject to AI examination by a state or federal prudential regulator under published guidance meeting the law's criteria are deemed in compliance.
- **Insurance:** Insurers and developers for insurers are in compliance if subject to Colorado's insurance algorithmic-regulation statutes.
- **HIPAA-covered entities:** Certain health care entities in compliance with HIPAA AI-related guidance are partially exempt.

### Enforcement

The Colorado Attorney General has **exclusive** enforcement authority. There is no private right of action. Violations constitute **unfair or deceptive trade practices** under the [Colorado Consumer Protection Act (CCPA)](https://coag.gov/resources/consumer-protection/), and civil penalties may reach **$20,000 per violation**. The AG may also seek injunctive relief and restitution.

### Governor's Signing Statement

Governor Polis's reluctance letter is significant. While signing the bill into law, he expressed concern that the law "could inadvertently stifle innovation" and asked the legislature to address several issues before February 1, 2026: potential undue burdens on small AI developers and deployers, definitional uncertainties, and the need for alignment with federal AI governance. He simultaneously called on Congress to enact federal legislation that would preempt SB 24-205 with "a needed cohesive federal approach." This ambivalence foreshadowed the 2025 special session and enforcement delay.

## Impact Assessment [MEDIUM confidence]

### Who Is Affected

**Employers** are among the most directly affected parties. Any Colorado employer using an AI tool to assist with hiring, promotion, compensation, or termination decisions — whether the employer developed the tool or purchased it from a vendor — is a "deployer" of a high-risk AI system. This encompasses virtually all mid-to-large employers using automated resume screening, performance scoring, or scheduling systems. Impact assessment and human-review requirements create compliance infrastructure obligations comparable to GDPR's DPIA process.

**AI developers** (including SaaS vendors, HR-tech companies, LLM API providers, and in-house AI teams) face documentation and disclosure obligations to their enterprise customers. Developers whose models are used in high-risk contexts — whether or not they control the deployment — bear notice and incident-reporting obligations.

**Financial services and healthcare** entities with active prudential-regulator AI oversight may qualify for the financial-institution or HIPAA exemptions, but must confirm their regulators' guidance meets the statutory criteria.

**Insurance companies** operating under Colorado's algorithmic-insurance statutes are fully exempt.

### The Developer-Deployer Dynamic

A distinctive feature of the CAIA is its simultaneous regulation of both ends of the AI supply chain. A software vendor selling an AI hiring tool is a "developer" with obligations to document risk and disclose to its enterprise customer/deployer. The enterprise that deploys that tool is the "deployer" with independent impact assessment, notice, and appeal obligations. Where deployers rely on developer documentation, the developer's failure to disclose risks flows downstream into deployer liability exposure. [Wilson Sonsini analysis](https://www.wsgr.com/en/insights/colorado-passes-first-in-nation-artificial-intelligence-act.html) highlighted this dual-obligation structure as one of the most operationally complex aspects of the law.

### Influence on State AI Legislative Activity

Colorado's passage of SB 24-205 accelerated state AI legislative activity nationwide. [Georgia and Illinois introduced bills](https://www.naag.org/attorney-general-journal/a-deep-dive-into-colorados-artificial-intelligence-act/) closely modeled on the CAIA's developer-deployer framework; neither advanced to enactment in 2024. California Governor Gavin Newsom subsequently faced a similar AI legislative debate and slowed California's own SB 1047 (frontier models bill) in part due to concerns echoing those raised in Colorado. Connecticut's own AI bill failed in 2024 amid a veto threat from Governor Ned Lamont. Colorado's experience demonstrated both the political feasibility and the industry-opposition dynamics that shaped the rest of the 2024-2025 state AI legislative wave.

### Industry Opposition

The U.S. Chamber of Commerce, [Chamber of Progress](https://www.seyfarth.com/news-insights/colorado-governor-signs-broad-ai-bill-regulating-employment-decisions.html), and Consumer Technology Association each urged Governor Polis to veto SB 24-205. Their core objections:

- Overly broad scope that burdens companies who are not the source of discriminatory outcomes
- Difficulty determining where discriminatory outcomes originate in multi-model AI pipelines
- Compliance costs for small businesses that use — but do not develop — AI tools
- Risk of deterring AI innovation and deployment in Colorado

These concerns were partially validated when the 2025 regular session produced no amendment and the August 2025 special session collapsed without substantive changes, ultimately producing only a delay.

## Action Items

- **AI vendors and developers** selling into Colorado enterprise markets should audit their products to determine whether any downstream use case meets the "consequential decision" threshold, and prepare developer documentation packages for deployer customers as required by the Act.
- **Employers and other deployers** of AI-assisted employment, benefits, lending, and healthcare decision tools should conduct a gap assessment against NIST AI RMF impact assessment requirements to determine scope of compliance work needed.
- **Legal and compliance teams** should map which AI systems in use qualify as "high-risk" under the consequential decisions definition, and identify which vendors bear developer obligations.
- **Monitor enforcement delay legislation** (see companion report) — the June 30, 2026 enforcement date is current as of April 2026; further amendments remain possible.
- **Track federal preemption** — President Trump's December 2025 executive order directs federal agencies to identify "onerous" state AI laws; Colorado's CAIA is a primary target.
