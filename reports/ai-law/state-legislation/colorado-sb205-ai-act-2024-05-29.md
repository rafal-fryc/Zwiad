# The Colorado AI Act: America's First Comprehensive AI Law (SB 24-205)

**Jurisdiction:** Colorado | **Category:** AI Law | **Date:** May 29, 2024

> **Note:** This report covers Colorado SB 24-205 as reported by Herzog Fox & Neeman via Lexology on May 29, 2024, placing the law in comparative context with the EU AI Act. For the primary enactment analysis from May 14, 2024, see [reports/ai-law/state-legislation/colorado-ai-act-sb24-205-passage-2024-05-14.md](reports/ai-law/state-legislation/colorado-ai-act-sb24-205-passage-2024-05-14.md). For the enforcement delay and post-2024 developments, see [reports/ai-law/state-legislation/colorado-ai-act-enforcement-delayed-2026-04-13.md](reports/ai-law/state-legislation/colorado-ai-act-enforcement-delayed-2026-04-13.md).

## Executive Summary [HIGH confidence]

On May 17, 2024, Colorado Governor Jared Polis signed [Senate Bill 24-205](https://leg.colorado.gov/bills/sb24-205) into law, making Colorado the first US state to enact a comprehensive artificial intelligence regulatory framework. The law is formally titled "An Act Concerning Consumer Protections for Interactions with Artificial Intelligence" — commonly abbreviated as "CAIA" or referred to as the "Colorado AI Act," though "CAIA" is a colloquial shorthand rather than a statutory short title. The legislation arrived in the same month that the European Union AI Act received its final parliamentary vote, marking a convergence of AI regulatory activity on both sides of the Atlantic. The Colorado AI Act adopts a risk-based approach modeled in part on the EU AI Act's framework, imposing a duty of reasonable care on both **developers** and **deployers** of "high-risk" AI systems to prevent algorithmic discrimination in consequential decisions affecting Colorado consumers. Enforcement rests exclusively with the Colorado Attorney General, with civil penalties of up to $20,000 per violation. Governor Polis signed with reservations, and the law's effective date was subsequently pushed from February 1, 2026, to June 30, 2026, after a failed special legislative session in August 2025.

## Background [HIGH confidence]

### The Transatlantic AI Regulatory Moment

Colorado's enactment of SB 24-205 coincided directly with the completion of the EU AI Act's legislative process. The [EU AI Act](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32024R1689), which the European Parliament passed in March 2024 and which formally entered into force in August 2024, is the world's first binding comprehensive AI regulatory framework. As law firm Herzog Fox & Neeman observed in their May 29, 2024 client alert via Lexology, Colorado's passage occurred precisely while the EU Act was "about to be officially published and enter into force."

This parallelism is more than coincidental. Colorado's legislative drafters — led by Senate Majority Leader Robert Rodriguez — drew on EU AI Act concepts, particularly its tiered, risk-based architecture that concentrates regulatory obligations on "high-risk" AI uses. The two regimes emerged from a shared global conversation about AI governance, though they differ substantially in scope, enforcement mechanisms, and the legal standards they impose.

### Federal Vacuum and State Legislative Activity

By the spring of 2024, Congress had not enacted comprehensive AI legislation. Federal activity consisted of:

- The [NIST AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF 1.0)](https://www.nist.gov/system/files/documents/2023/01/26/AI%20RMF%201.0.pdf), published in January 2023, which established voluntary guidance for AI risk management;
- The Biden administration's [AI Executive Order of October 2023](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2023/11/01/2023-24283/safe-secure-and-trustworthy-development-and-use-of-artificial-intelligence), directing federal agencies to develop standards but imposing no private-sector mandates;
- FTC guidance on AI and deceptive practices; and
- Multiple Congressional bills that had not advanced.

In this federal vacuum, states began filling the gap. A [multi-state AI legislative workgroup](https://www.bytebacklaw.com/2024/05/colorado-legislature-passes-first-in-nation-artificial-intelligence-bill/) organized through the Future of Privacy Forum, led by Connecticut Senator James Maroney and Colorado Senator Rodriguez, met seven times and coordinated drafting across nearly thirty states. Colorado and Connecticut introduced companion bills in 2024 (Connecticut's effort ultimately failed). Colorado's bill became the nation's first to pass and be signed into law.

### Colorado Legislative History

Senator Rodriguez introduced [SB 24-205](https://leg.colorado.gov/bills/sb24-205) on April 10, 2024. The bill was co-sponsored in the Senate by Senators Cutter, Michaelson Jenet, Priola, Winter F., and Fenberg, and in the House by Representatives Brianna Titone, Manny Rutinel, and Duran. The [Colorado Senate passed the bill on May 3, 2024](https://leg.colorado.gov/bills/sb24-205), and the House passed it on May 8, 2024. Governor Polis signed the bill on May 17, 2024, accompanied by a formal letter expressing reservations and calling on the legislature to "significantly improve" the law before its original February 1, 2026 effective date.

The official signed text is available from the [Colorado General Assembly](https://content.leg.colorado.gov/sites/default/files/2024a_205_signed.pdf).

## Detailed Analysis [HIGH confidence]

### Framework: Risk-Based, Duty-of-Care Approach

Unlike the EU AI Act's prescriptive product-safety model — which imposes pre-market conformity assessments, CE-marking equivalents, and outright prohibitions on certain AI uses — Colorado's Colorado AI Act adopts a negligence-based "reasonable care" standard rooted in American tort law. The law does not prohibit any category of AI outright. Instead, it creates an affirmative duty for developers and deployers to exercise reasonable care to prevent algorithmic discrimination when their AI systems are used in high-stakes contexts.

A key structural feature borrowed from the EU framework is the **tiered scope**: only "high-risk" AI systems are subject to the full compliance regime. This concept — that regulatory intensity should track actual risk — appears in both the EU AI Act and the NIST AI RMF, and Colorado's adoption of it marked a deliberate choice to focus compliance obligations where societal harm is most likely.

### Scope: High-Risk AI Systems and Consequential Decisions [HIGH confidence]

The Colorado AI Act defines a **high-risk artificial intelligence system** as any AI system that makes, or is a "substantial factor" in making, a **consequential decision** — defined as a decision with a material legal or similarly significant effect on a consumer's access to, cost of, or terms of:

- Employment, employment opportunities, or employment-related benefits
- Education enrollment or opportunity
- Financial or lending services
- Essential government services
- Health care services
- Housing
- Insurance
- Legal services

**Algorithmic discrimination** — the harm targeted by the law — means any condition in which an AI system results in unlawful differential treatment or impact that disfavors an individual or group based on actual or perceived race, color, ethnicity, sex, religion, age, national origin, limited English proficiency, disability, veteran status, genetic information, or reproductive health.

**Developer** is defined as a person doing business in Colorado who develops or intentionally and substantially modifies a high-risk AI system offered commercially. **Deployer** means a person doing business in Colorado who deploys a high-risk AI system in a product or service to make, or be a substantial factor in making, consequential decisions about Colorado consumers.

### Developer Obligations [HIGH confidence]

Under the [signed statute](https://content.leg.colorado.gov/sites/default/files/2024a_205_signed.pdf), developers of high-risk AI systems must exercise reasonable care to protect Colorado consumers from known or reasonably foreseeable risks of algorithmic discrimination. Specific duties include:

1. **Documentation to deployers:** Developers must supply deployers with materials describing the AI system's intended uses, known harmful or inappropriate uses, how training data was evaluated for bias, and what risk mitigation measures were taken.
2. **Public disclosure:** Developers must maintain a publicly accessible statement summarizing the types of high-risk AI systems they have developed, made commercially available, and how they manage foreseeable algorithmic discrimination risks.
3. **Incident reporting:** Within 90 days of discovering or receiving a credible report that a system has caused or is reasonably likely to have caused algorithmic discrimination, developers must notify the Colorado Attorney General and all known Colorado deployers.

### Deployer Obligations [HIGH confidence]

A deployer obtains a **rebuttable presumption of reasonable care** — the statute's central safe harbor — if it satisfies the following requirements:

1. **Risk management policy:** Implement a risk management policy and program for each high-risk AI system, incorporating the [NIST AI Risk Management Framework](https://www.nist.gov/artificial-intelligence/executive-order-safe-secure-and-trustworthy-artificial-intelligence), ISO 42001, or a substantially equivalent framework.
2. **Impact assessments:** Conduct impact assessments prior to deployment, annually thereafter, and within 90 days of any intentional and substantial modification. Assessments must cover the types of data processed, performance metrics, known limitations, and transparency measures.
3. **Consumer notice:** When a high-risk AI system makes or substantially influences a consequential decision about a consumer, notify that consumer — in plain language, in the consumer's language, and in accessible formats — explaining the decision and how to correct inaccurate data.
4. **Human review and appeal:** Provide consumers a meaningful opportunity to appeal adverse consequential decisions and, when technically feasible, to obtain human review of the AI-influenced decision.
5. **Website summary:** Publish a summary of all high-risk AI systems deployed.

An additional **voluntary safe harbor** applies to organizations that discover violations through their own voluntary review and remediate them before any complaint is filed.

### Consumer-Facing AI Disclosure (Broader than High-Risk) [HIGH confidence]

A provision that extends SB 24-205's reach well beyond the "high-risk" framework is its **general AI labeling requirement**: any person doing business in Colorado that deploys or makes available an AI system intended to interact with consumers must disclose to each consumer that they are interacting with an AI system. This obligation applies to all consumer-facing AI deployments — chatbots, virtual assistants, AI recommendation tools — regardless of whether the system makes consequential decisions. As analyzed by [Holland & Knight](https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2024/05/colorado-legislature-approves-ai-bill-targeting-highrisk-systems-and), this makes the labeling requirement the single broadest provision of the law, reaching companies that would otherwise conclude the high-risk AI framework does not apply to them.

### Exemptions [HIGH confidence]

The Colorado AI Act carves out several categories from high-risk obligations:

- **Federally regulated AI:** Systems approved, cleared, or authorized by the FDA, FAA, or Federal Housing Finance Agency are exempt. Systems deployed under contracts with the Department of Commerce, Department of Defense, or NASA are exempt unless the decision affects employment or housing.
- **Financial institutions:** Banks, credit unions (state-chartered or federal), and their affiliates subject to AI examination by a state or federal prudential regulator under published guidance meeting the law's criteria are deemed compliant.
- **Insurance:** Insurers and developers for insurers operating under Colorado's algorithmic-insurance statutes.
- **HIPAA-covered entities:** Certain health care entities compliant with HHS AI-related guidance are partially exempt.
- **Small business exception:** Deployers with fewer than 50 full-time employees are exempt from certain requirements if they do not train the AI system using their own data, **and** they limit their use of the system to uses disclosed by the developer, **and** they provide consumers access to the impact assessment completed by the developer.

### Enforcement [HIGH confidence]

The Colorado Attorney General has **exclusive** enforcement authority; there is no private right of action. Violations constitute **unfair or deceptive trade practices** under the [Colorado Consumer Protection Act](https://coag.gov/resources/consumer-protection/), with civil penalties reaching **$20,000 per violation**. The AG may also pursue injunctive relief and restitution. The AG has rulemaking authority to implement the Act's requirements. The [Colorado AG's official AI page](https://coag.gov/ai/) serves as the rulemaking hub.

### Comparison to the EU AI Act [MEDIUM confidence]

The near-simultaneous arrival of Colorado's Colorado AI Act and the EU AI Act invites comparison. As analyzed by [Herzog Fox & Neeman](https://www.lexology.com/r/UE7wCMm/f51ba1d131/XXcN) and the [National Association of Attorneys General](https://www.naag.org/attorney-general-journal/a-deep-dive-into-colorados-artificial-intelligence-act/), the two frameworks share a risk-tiered architecture but differ substantially:

| Feature | Colorado AI Act | EU AI Act |
|---|---|---|
| Prohibited uses | None | Yes (unacceptable-risk category) |
| High-risk standard | Duty of care + reasonable care safe harbor | Prescriptive conformity assessment |
| Enforcement model | State AG exclusive, CCPA penalties | Multi-level: national authorities + EU AI Office |
| Private right of action | None | Varies by member state |
| Scope of application | Consequential decisions (defined categories) | Tiered by risk classification across all AI uses |
| Approach to compliance | Framework-based (NIST AI RMF, ISO 42001) | CE-marking, technical documentation, third-party audit for highest risk |

Colorado's approach is notably more flexible than the EU Act. The "reasonable care" standard and framework-based safe harbor reflect a distinctly American preference for outcome-based, negligence-style liability rather than prescriptive pre-market authorization.

### Governor Polis's Signing Statement [HIGH confidence]

Governor Polis signed SB 24-205 while simultaneously expressing reservations in a formal letter. He criticized the law for creating "a complex compliance regime" and cautioned that it could "inadvertently stifle innovation." He urged the General Assembly to "significantly improve" the law's approach before the February 1, 2026 effective date, specifically requesting attention to definitional uncertainties, burdens on small AI developers, and alignment with any emerging federal framework. He simultaneously called on Congress to enact comprehensive federal AI legislation that would preempt SB 24-205. As reported by [Seyfarth Shaw](https://www.seyfarth.com/news-insights/colorado-governor-signs-broad-ai-bill-regulating-employment-decisions.html), this signing-with-reservations approach was unusual and helped set the stage for subsequent amendment attempts.

## Impact Assessment [MEDIUM confidence]

### Industry Affected

The law's practical scope is broader than its "high-risk" label suggests:

**Employers** are among the most directly affected. Any Colorado employer using AI tools to assist with hiring, promotion, compensation, or termination decisions — whether the employer built the tool or purchased it from a vendor — qualifies as a "deployer" of a high-risk AI system. Impact assessment and human-review requirements impose compliance infrastructure obligations comparable in structure (if not scale) to GDPR Data Protection Impact Assessments.

**AI developers and software vendors** selling AI tools used in employment, lending, health care, education, or housing face documentation and incident-reporting obligations. Developers whose AI is used in high-risk contexts — whether or not they control the deployment — bear notice and 90-day incident-reporting duties.

**Financial services and healthcare entities** with active prudential-regulator AI oversight may qualify for exemptions, but must verify that their regulator's guidance meets the statutory criteria.

**Retailers, financial services firms, and any entity deploying consumer-facing AI** — even if not "high-risk" — face the general AI labeling requirement.

### Industry Opposition

The U.S. Chamber of Commerce, [Chamber of Progress](https://www.securityindustry.org/2024/05/28/misgivings-cloud-first-in-nation-colorado-ai-law-implications-and-considerations-for-the-security-industry/), and Consumer Technology Association each urged Governor Polis to veto SB 24-205. Core objections centered on:

- Overly broad scope creating liability for companies that do not originate discriminatory outcomes
- Difficulty attributing discriminatory outputs in multi-model AI pipelines
- Annual impact assessment costs disproportionate for small and mid-sized businesses
- Risk of deterring AI innovation and deployment in Colorado
- The "patchwork" compliance burden if other states enact similar but non-identical laws

### Catalytic Effect on State AI Legislation

Colorado's passage of SB 24-205 accelerated state AI legislative activity nationwide. [Georgia and Illinois introduced bills](https://www.naag.org/attorney-general-journal/a-deep-dive-into-colorados-artificial-intelligence-act/) closely modeled on the Colorado AI Act's developer-deployer framework. California's AI legislative debate intensified. Connecticut's companion bill failed amid a veto threat from Governor Lamont. Colorado's experience demonstrated both the political feasibility of comprehensive state AI legislation and the industry-opposition dynamics that would shape the rest of the 2024-2025 state AI legislative wave.

### Post-Enactment Timeline

Subsequent developments after the May 2024 enactment (outside this report's primary scope):

- **August 2025:** A special legislative session failed to produce substantive amendments. The resulting legislation, [SB 25B-004](https://leg.colorado.gov/bills/sb25b-004), signed by Governor Polis on August 28, 2025, merely delayed the effective date from February 1, 2026 to **June 30, 2026**.
- **December 2025:** President Trump signed an [executive order](https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2025/12/eliminating-state-law-obstruction-of-national-artificial-intelligence-policy/) directing federal agencies to identify "onerous" state AI laws for potential federal preemption challenge; Colorado's Colorado AI Act is widely expected to be targeted.
- **March 2026:** Governor Polis's AI Policy Working Group reached unanimous consensus on a [proposed ADMT (Automated Decision-Making Technology) framework](https://governorsoffice.colorado.gov/governor/news/colorado-artificial-intelligence-policy-workgroup-delivers-unanimous-support-revised-policy) to replace SB 24-205, using data privacy law terminology. The existing SB 24-205 remains on track for its **June 30, 2026** effective date; the proposed ADMT replacement bill, if enacted, would instead take effect **January 1, 2027**, with new AG rulemaking due by December 31, 2026. A replacement bill is expected in the 2026 legislative session.
- **Current status (April 2026):** The June 30, 2026 effective date for the existing SB 24-205 stands. A repeal-and-replace bill targeting a January 1, 2027 effective date is under active consideration.

## Action Items

- **Audit consumer-facing AI:** Any AI system deployed to interact with Colorado consumers triggers the general disclosure requirement regardless of "high-risk" classification. Implement disclosures by June 30, 2026.
- **Map high-risk AI deployments:** Determine which AI systems in current use make or substantially influence employment, lending, health care, education, housing, insurance, or legal services decisions affecting Colorado consumers.
- **Developer documentation readiness:** AI vendors must prepare documentation packages for deployer customers: intended uses, harmful uses, training data bias evaluation, risk mitigation measures. Establish 90-day incident-reporting workflows to the Colorado AG.
- **Deploy risk management frameworks:** Deployers should implement NIST AI RMF or ISO 42001 governance programs to qualify for the rebuttable presumption of reasonable care.
- **Conduct pre-deployment impact assessments:** Required for all high-risk systems before June 30, 2026; design repeatable processes for annual reassessment and post-modification review.
- **Track the 2026 legislative session:** A repeal-and-replace bill incorporating the ADMT framework proposed by the Governor's working group is expected; if enacted, the core obligations may shift materially and the effective date would move to January 1, 2027.
- **Monitor federal preemption developments:** The Trump administration's AI executive order signals potential DOJ litigation against SB 24-205; track Commerce Department guidance and any resulting federal challenge.
