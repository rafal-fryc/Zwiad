# Utah AI Disclosure Law: Healthcare and Regulated Occupation Compliance Challenges (Supplementary Analysis)

**Jurisdiction:** Utah | **Category:** AI Law | **Date:** May 15, 2024

> **Note:** This is a supplementary research memo focused on healthcare and regulated-occupation compliance dimensions of Utah's Artificial Intelligence Policy Act (UAIPA / SB 149). A comprehensive primary report covering the full scope of the law is at [reports/ai-law/state-legislation/utah-uaipa-sb149-ai-disclosure-2024-05-15.md](utah-uaipa-sb149-ai-disclosure-2024-05-15.md). This memo draws primarily on the [Cozen O'Connor client alert](https://www.cozen.com/news-resources/publications/2024/utah-to-require-disclosure-of-artificial-intelligence) and healthcare-sector analyses published around the law's May 1, 2024 effective date.

## Executive Summary [HIGH confidence]

The [Utah Artificial Intelligence Policy Act (UAIPA), enacted via SB 149](https://le.utah.gov/~2024/bills/static/SB0149.html), took effect May 1, 2024, imposing a bifurcated AI disclosure framework on Utah businesses. The law's proactive, pre-interaction disclosure requirement bears most heavily on "regulated occupations" — a category that encompasses more than 30 licensed healthcare professions under the Utah Department of Commerce, including physicians, surgeons, dentists, nurses, pharmacists, midwives, and others. Cozen O'Connor's analysis at the law's effective date highlights that healthcare organizations face particularly acute implementation challenges: the statute is silent on disclosure frequency (per-patient, per-session, or per-encounter), offers no clear guidance on when licensed professional activity begins and ends in complex care settings, and gives covered entities a narrow two-month runway between enactment (March 13, 2024) and effectiveness (May 1, 2024). The 2025 legislative amendments have since modified the proactive disclosure threshold to apply only to "high-risk" AI interactions, but the fundamental ambiguities for healthcare providers remain relevant to compliance programs.

## Background [HIGH confidence]

Utah Governor Spencer Cox signed [SB 149](https://le.utah.gov/~2024/bills/static/SB0149.html) on March 13, 2024. The law amends Utah's consumer protection statutes — principally the Utah Consumer Sales Practices Act — rather than creating standalone AI legislation. The Utah Division of Consumer Protection (UDCP) received primary enforcement authority.

The UAIPA reflects a deliberate design choice: rather than building new AI regulatory machinery, the legislature grafted AI-specific disclosure obligations onto existing consumer protection enforcement infrastructure. This approach carries implications for regulated professions because Utah's existing professional licensing framework — administered by the Department of Commerce — serves as the definitional anchor for the law's most demanding disclosure tier.

The law was promulgated against a backdrop of no federal AI disclosure mandate, giving Utah a first-mover position. [Cozen O'Connor noted](https://www.cozen.com/news-resources/publications/2024/utah-to-require-disclosure-of-artificial-intelligence) the brief compliance window as a key concern: entities had fewer than two months from signing to operationalize disclosure mechanisms across consumer-facing AI tools.

## Detailed Analysis [HIGH confidence]

### The Bifurcated Disclosure Framework

The UAIPA creates two distinct compliance tracks:

**Tier 1 — Regulated Occupations (Proactive Disclosure):** Entities in occupations regulated by the Department of Commerce and requiring licensure or state certification must "prominently" disclose that a consumer is interacting with generative AI at the outset of any AI-assisted service interaction:
- Oral/audible interactions: verbal disclosure at the start of the conversation.
- Electronic/text interactions: written disclosure before the exchange begins.

**Tier 2 — General Consumer Protection Activities (On-Request Disclosure):** Any person who uses generative AI to interact with a consumer must clearly and conspicuously disclose that the consumer is interacting with AI — but only when asked or prompted by the consumer.

The law expressly prohibits using AI authorship as a defense to consumer protection liability: a business cannot escape an enforcement action by arguing that the AI, rather than a human, made the violating statement.

### Healthcare as the Primary Regulated Occupation Vector [HIGH confidence]

[McDermott Will & Emery's analysis](https://www.mcdermottlaw.com/insights/how-the-utah-artificial-intelligence-policy-act-impacts-health-professionals/) confirms that "healthcare organizations may bear the brunt of this law" because "regulated occupations" encompasses over 30 distinct healthcare professions regulated by the Utah Department of Commerce. The list includes:

- Physicians and surgeons
- Dentists and dental hygienists
- Registered and licensed practical nurses
- Pharmacists
- Midwives
- Dieticians and nutritionists
- Physical and occupational therapists
- Radiology technologists
- Genetic counselors
- Health facility administrators

[Bass, Berry & Sims](https://www.bassberry.com/news/utah-ai-acts-impact-on-healthcare-and-other-regulated-occupations/) emphasizes the "bifurcated approach" the law takes: holding regulated professions to a stricter standard than general commercial entities reflects legislative recognition that professional-patient relationships carry heightened trust expectations.

### Key Implementation Ambiguities for Healthcare [MEDIUM confidence]

Multiple law firm analyses identified implementation gaps left by the statute's text. These ambiguities were highlighted in the Cozen O'Connor alert and corroborated by other firm analyses:

**1. Disclosure Frequency.** The statute does not specify whether AI disclosure must occur before each individual instance of AI use, once per patient encounter, once per provider-patient relationship, or on some other cadence. Where a healthcare organization uses AI for multiple tasks within a single patient visit (e.g., scheduling assistance, symptom triage chatbot, clinical documentation AI, and medication reminders), it is unclear whether each AI-mediated touchpoint requires its own disclosure or whether a single encounter-level disclosure suffices.

**2. Scope of "Provision of Regulated Services."** The disclosure obligation is triggered when generative AI is used "in the provision of regulated services." For clinical care (e.g., a physician using an AI diagnostic assistant during patient consultations), the trigger is clear. However, [McDermott](https://www.mcdermottlaw.com/insights/how-the-utah-artificial-intelligence-policy-act-impacts-health-professionals/) identifies value-based care operations as a gray area: care coordination, patient record management, population health analytics, and administrative AI tools are increasingly common but their connection to licensed professional practice — and thus whether they constitute "regulated services" — is legally unsettled under the UAIPA.

**3. Operator vs. Individual Practitioner Liability.** In healthcare settings, AI tools are typically deployed by hospital systems or health technology vendors rather than by individual licensed practitioners. The statute targets those who "use, prompt, or otherwise cause" generative AI to interact with consumers. Where a hospital system deploys an AI tool used by a physician, it is unclear whether the hospital, the physician, or both bear the disclosure obligation.

**4. Patient vs. Consumer Framing.** The UAIPA's framework is structured around consumer protection law, not healthcare law. "Consumers" in the consumer protection context map imperfectly onto "patients" in the clinical context, creating interpretive uncertainty about whether the law applies to purely clinical AI tools or only to consumer-facing health products.

### Enforcement Mechanism [HIGH confidence]

[Cozen O'Connor confirmed](https://www.cozen.com/news-resources/publications/2024/utah-to-require-disclosure-of-artificial-intelligence) that the UDCP holds sole enforcement authority under the UAIPA, with the power to:
- Impose administrative fines of up to **$2,500 per violation**.
- Seek injunctive relief in UDCP-initiated proceedings.
- Pursue disgorgement of funds obtained through violative conduct.

The Utah Attorney General may independently seek civil penalties of up to **$5,000 per violation** of an existing administrative or court order. No private right of action exists — enforcement is exclusively governmental.

### The AI Learning Laboratory: A Compliance Safety Valve [MEDIUM confidence]

The UAIPA simultaneously creates the Office of Artificial Intelligence Policy and an AI Learning Laboratory Program. Entities participating in the Laboratory may apply for regulatory mitigation — including reduced fines and pre-penalty cure periods — while testing AI systems. [Bass, Berry & Sims](https://www.bassberry.com/news/utah-ai-acts-impact-on-healthcare-and-other-regulated-occupations/) notes this is "particularly important given the fast-changing nature of AI and the uncertainty around each new use case." For healthcare entities operating in ambiguous UAIPA territory, Laboratory participation may offer a structured way to demonstrate good-faith compliance efforts while the legal framework matures.

### 2025 Amendments: Impact on Healthcare [HIGH confidence]

In March 2025, the Utah legislature enacted SB 226, effective May 7, 2025, which substantially modified the proactive disclosure requirement. Under the amended law, proactive disclosure at the outset of an interaction is only required when the AI interaction is "high-risk" — defined as one involving:
1. Collection of sensitive personal information (financial, health, or biometric data); AND
2. Provision of personalized recommendations or advice that could reasonably be relied upon for "significant personal decisions" (including medical advice).

For healthcare providers, this high-risk definition is likely to cover clinical AI tools that collect health data and provide patient-specific recommendations — meaning the disclosure obligation for the core clinical use case remains largely intact, even if narrowed at the margins. Non-clinical healthcare AI (administrative tools, operational analytics) may fall outside the high-risk threshold and revert to the lighter on-request disclosure standard.

[Sheppard Mullin](https://www.sheppardhealthlaw.com/2024/06/articles/utah-providers-are-you-complying-with-the-ai-policy-act/) has specifically advised Utah healthcare providers to assess their AI portfolios against the amended high-risk criteria and update disclosure workflows accordingly.

## Impact Assessment [MEDIUM confidence]

### Who Bears the Greatest Compliance Burden

Under the original UAIPA framework (May 2024 effective date), the entities facing the most immediate and operationally complex compliance obligations were:

- **Health systems and hospital networks** deploying AI tools across clinical, administrative, and patient-facing touchpoints.
- **Physician groups and specialty practices** using AI-assisted clinical decision support, telehealth, or patient communication tools.
- **Digital health platforms** operating in Utah and offering AI-powered symptom checkers, care coordination tools, or medication management systems.
- **Licensed professionals in non-healthcare regulated occupations**: attorneys, accountants, engineers, architects, and other licensed practitioners using generative AI in client-facing work.

The 2025 amendments reduce the proactive disclosure burden for regulated entities not operating in "high-risk" territory — but the ambiguities around what constitutes "high-risk" for healthcare create ongoing compliance uncertainty.

### Compliance Implementation Challenges

Healthcare-specific compliance actions identified across the law firm analyses include:

- **AI inventory audit**: Catalog all generative AI tools in use across patient-facing and professionally licensed contexts; determine which meet the UAIPA "generative AI" definition (trained on data, generates non-scripted human-like outputs, interacts via text/audio/visual communication).
- **Disclosure mechanism design**: Build verbal disclosure scripts for patient phone interactions and written disclosure banners for patient portal/chat interfaces. Decisions about placement, timing, and language must be made without UDCP guidance on adequacy.
- **Frequency policy**: Adopt an internal disclosure frequency policy (e.g., once-per-encounter vs. once-per-tool-use) pending regulatory clarification; document the policy decision and its legal rationale.
- **Vendor contract review**: Ensure AI vendors have configured systems to identify themselves as AI in response to user requests; include UAIPA compliance representations in vendor agreements.
- **AI Laboratory registration**: Assess eligibility for the Utah AI Learning Laboratory Program as a risk-mitigation mechanism during the early enforcement period.

### Enforcement Risk Outlook

No significant public UDCP enforcement actions under the UAIPA have been publicly identified through the date of this writing. The enforcement program is nascent. Given the lack of a private right of action and the $2,500-per-violation fine cap, enforcement risk for most healthcare entities is primarily reputational and injunctive. However, disgorgement remains a meaningful remedy for telehealth or digital health businesses that profited from AI-mediated consumer interactions conducted without required disclosures.

## Action Items

- Conduct an AI inventory audit covering all consumer-facing and clinician-facing AI tools deployed in Utah contexts; flag any tool that generates non-scripted human-like outputs via text, audio, or visual communication.
- Implement proactive disclosure procedures for healthcare AI that qualifies as "high-risk" under the 2025 amendments (tools collecting health data and providing personalized clinical recommendations).
- Ensure all AI systems deployed for patient-facing interactions can affirmatively identify themselves as AI in response to a clear patient request.
- Develop an internal policy on disclosure frequency (per-encounter, per-tool-use, or per-relationship) and document the legal basis for the chosen approach.
- Review vendor agreements to include UAIPA compliance representations and confirm AI systems are configured for on-request disclosure.
- Consider applying to the Utah AI Learning Laboratory Program as a compliance risk-mitigation mechanism for novel or ambiguous use cases.
- Monitor UDCP enforcement actions and any regulatory guidance from the Utah Office of Artificial Intelligence Policy on disclosure adequacy standards.
- Track the UAIPA's July 1, 2027 sunset date and legislative activity before that date that may further modify the healthcare disclosure framework.
