# Texas Responsible AI Governance Act (TRAIGA): From Broad Proposal to Enacted Law

**Jurisdiction:** Texas | **Category:** AI Law | **Date:** 2025-01-15

## Executive Summary [HIGH confidence]

On December 23, 2024, Texas State Representative Giovanni Capriglione (R-Tarrant County) filed House Bill 1709, the Texas Responsible AI Governance Act (TRAIGA), a sweeping proposal that would have imposed comprehensive governance obligations on developers and deployers of high-risk AI systems across healthcare, employment, finance, and education sectors. The original bill drew significant industry opposition for its broad scope and obligations on private employers. After substantial revision, Capriglione introduced a new, separate bill — HB 149 — in March 2025, which was signed into law by Governor Greg Abbott on June 22, 2025, making Texas the third US state — after Colorado and Utah — to enact comprehensive AI governance legislation. The enacted version significantly narrows the original proposal — eliminating most private-sector compliance burdens and focusing primarily on government agency AI use and targeted prohibitions on harmful AI behaviors. The law took effect on January 1, 2026.

## Background [HIGH confidence]

### Texas AI Policy Trajectory

Texas had been actively developing AI policy prior to TRAIGA. In 2024, the Texas House Speaker created a [Select Committee on Artificial Intelligence and Emerging Technologies](https://capitol.texas.gov/tlodocs/89R/billtext/html/HB01709I.htm) to study and develop recommendations for AI governance in the state. This committee work laid the groundwork for the legislative effort that culminated in TRAIGA.

At the federal level, the absence of comprehensive federal AI legislation created space for states to act. Colorado became the first state to pass broad AI governance legislation with [SB 24-205 (the Colorado AI Act)](https://leg.colorado.gov/bills/sb24-205) in May 2024, establishing requirements for developers and deployers of "high-risk" AI systems used in consequential decisions. TRAIGA was positioned as a response to and evolution of the Colorado model, with Capriglione seeking to craft a Texas-specific framework.

### Filing of HB 1709 (TRAIGA 1.0)

Representative Capriglione filed the [original HB 1709](https://capitol.texas.gov/tlodocs/89R/billtext/html/HB01709I.htm) on December 23, 2024, as part of the Texas Legislature's 89th Regular Session. At introduction, the bill was described as among the most comprehensive AI regulatory proposals in the United States, exceeding even Colorado's law in scope. The [K&L Gates analysis from January 2025](https://www.klgates.com/The-Texas-Responsible-AI-Governance-Act-and-Its-Potential-Impact-on-Employers-1-13-2025) noted that the original bill would have significant implications for private employers using AI in hiring and employment decisions.

Industry groups and technology advocates responded with [significant opposition](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/us-law-week/overbroad-texas-ai-bill-threatens-innovation-and-economic-growth), characterizing the bill as overbroad and a threat to innovation and economic growth. This feedback drove the subsequent revisions.

## Detailed Analysis [HIGH confidence]

### The Original Proposal: HB 1709 (TRAIGA 1.0)

The original [43-page HB 1709 bill text](https://capitol.texas.gov/tlodocs/89R/billtext/pdf/HB01709I.pdf) centered on "high-risk artificial intelligence systems" — defined as AI systems that play a substantive role in a "consequential decision." Consequential decisions explicitly included:

- Employment or employment opportunities (hiring, firing, compensation, promotion)
- Education and educational opportunities
- Financial services and lending
- Housing
- Insurance
- Healthcare
- Government and public services

**Developer obligations under the original bill** included conducting impact assessments, maintaining detailed risk management documentation, and providing transparency disclosures. **Deployer obligations** included producing a Risk Identification and Management Policy, ensuring AI systems were fit for purpose, and disclosing AI use to affected individuals. Both developers and deployers faced potential civil penalties of up to **$200,000 per violation**, plus **$40,000 per day** for continuing violations, enforced by the Texas Attorney General.

The original bill also proposed an Artificial Intelligence Council with rulemaking authority, a regulatory sandbox program, and a Workforce Development Grant Program — making it a comprehensive institutional AI governance framework.

### The Revision Process

[Fortune reported in January 2025](https://fortune.com/2025/01/08/texas-ai-bill-traiga-hb-1709-capriglione-colorado-ai-regulation/) that HB 1709 was positioned as "the next battleground for U.S. AI policy." Following public comment and industry engagement, Representative Capriglione substantially revised the bill. On March 14, 2025, Capriglione introduced a new, separate bill — [HB 149](https://capitol.texas.gov/tlodocs/89R/billtext/pdf/HB00149F.pdf) — colloquially called "TRAIGA 2.0." HB 149 carried a new bill number and was not a formal amendment to HB 1709, though it incorporated the substantive revisions developed in response to industry feedback on the original proposal.

The [Transparency Coalition's analysis](https://www.transparencycoalition.ai/news/analysis-whats-in-traiga-the-texas-responsible-ai-governance-act) characterized the revision as a significant scaling back of private-sector obligations, while the [Texas Tribune reported](https://www.texastribune.org/2025/05/23/texas-ai-bill-legislation-regulation/) on the political dynamics driving the changes.

### The Enacted Law: HB 149 (TRAIGA 2.0)

Governor Abbott signed [HB 149](https://capitol.texas.gov/tlodocs/89R/billtext/pdf/HB00149F.pdf) on June 22, 2025. The enacted law makes Texas the third US state — after Colorado and Utah — to enact comprehensive AI governance legislation. (Utah enacted its AI Policy Act, SB 149, in 2024 as the second such law; Illinois has sector-specific AI statutes but not a single comprehensive AI governance framework.)

**Scope and Applicability**

TRAIGA applies to any party that: (1) develops or deploys an AI system in Texas; (2) produces a product or service used by Texas residents; or (3) promotes, advertises, or conducts business in the state. The definition of "artificial intelligence system" is broad: "any machine-based system that, for any explicit or implicit objective, infers from the inputs the system receives how to generate outputs including content, decisions, predictions, or recommendations, that can influence physical or virtual environments."

Notably, unlike the original bill's focus on "high-risk" AI in specific sectors, the enacted law **does not define a category of "high-risk AI"** for private-sector purposes.

**Government Entity Requirements**

The primary compliance burden under TRAIGA falls on **government entities**, not private businesses. State agencies and governmental bodies must:

- Disclose to individuals when they are interacting with an AI system
- Refrain from deploying AI systems that assign a "social score" used to cause detrimental treatment of individuals (a prohibition targeting China-style social credit systems)
- Ensure AI use does not violate constitutional rights

**Prohibited Uses (All Entities)**

For both government and private actors, TRAIGA prohibits:

- Developing or deploying AI systems "with the intent to unlawfully discriminate against a protected class" under federal or state law
- Using AI to manipulate human behavior in a manner designed to incite self-harm, violence, or criminal behavior
- Using AI to generate child sexual abuse material
- Developing or deploying AI systems with the "sole intent" of impairing any person's rights under the US Constitution

**The Intent Standard and Disparate Impact**

A critical provision for employers: TRAIGA's discrimination prohibition requires proof of **intent** to unlawfully discriminate. [As K&L Gates and multiple law firms have analyzed](https://www.klgates.com/Pared-Back-Version-of-the-Texas-Responsible-Artificial-Intelligence-Governance-Act-Signed-Into-Law-6-24-2025), disparate impact alone — where an AI system produces statistically unequal outcomes across protected classes without discriminatory intent — does not constitute a violation of TRAIGA. This is a significant departure from the original bill and from the Colorado AI Act's "reasonable care" standard.

**Safe Harbors**

The enacted law provides meaningful safe harbors. A party is not liable under TRAIGA if:

- A third party misuses the AI system in a manner that violates TRAIGA (without the developer's/deployer's knowledge)
- The violation is discovered through testing or a good-faith audit and is then remediated
- The party substantially complies with recognized standards such as the **NIST AI Risk Management Framework**

**AI Advisory Council and Regulatory Sandbox**

TRAIGA creates a **seven-member AI Advisory Council**, but unlike the original bill's proposal, the Council has **no rulemaking authority**. Its mandate is advisory: conducting AI training programs for state agencies and local governments, issuing reports on AI ethics, data privacy, security, and legal compliance. The Council cannot promulgate binding rules.

The law also establishes a **36-month regulatory sandbox** administered by the Texas Department of Information Resources (DIR), allowing entities to test AI innovations in a controlled environment with reduced regulatory burden during the experimentation period.

**Penalties and Enforcement**

Enforcement authority rests exclusively with the **Texas Attorney General**; there is no private right of action under TRAIGA. Before filing suit, the AG must provide written notice of an alleged violation and allow the party **60 days to cure**. If the violation is curable and cured within 60 days, no penalty applies. If not cured, the AG may pursue civil penalties. [According to multiple law firm analyses](https://www.gtlaw.com/en/insights/2025/6/traiga-key-provisions-of-texas-new-artificial-intelligence-governance-act), civil penalty ranges are:

- $10,000–$12,000 per curable violation not timely cured
- $80,000–$200,000 per uncurable violation
- $2,000–$40,000 per day for continuing violations

If the AG finds a violation by a licensed professional, it may also refer the matter to the applicable licensing agency for potential suspension, probation, or revocation of licensure.

**Companion Healthcare Legislation**

Separate companion legislation, [SB 1188](https://www.hchlawyers.com/blog/2026/february/new-texas-laws-require-ai-disclosure-in-healthca/), was enacted alongside TRAIGA to address AI in medical diagnosis. SB 1188 requires healthcare providers to disclose AI use in treatment contexts and mandates provider review of AI-generated medical records under Texas Medical Board standards.

## Impact Assessment [HIGH confidence]

### Impact on Private Employers

The enacted TRAIGA is substantially less burdensome for private-sector employers than the original HB 1709 proposal. [As Littler Mendelson analyzed](https://www.littler.com/news-analysis/asap/texas-joins-fray-and-enacts-ai-legislation), the final law eliminates:

- Mandatory impact assessments for AI systems used in employment decisions
- Affirmative disclosure obligations to job applicants about AI use
- Vendor accountability requirements for HR technology providers
- Risk Identification and Management Policy mandates

The primary remaining compliance concern for employers is the prohibition on AI systems deployed "with the intent to unlawfully discriminate." [Berkshire Associates noted](https://www.berkshireassociates.com/blog/texas-enacts-new-law-for-employers-using-artificial-intelligence) that while this sets a high bar (intent rather than impact), employers should still audit AI-assisted hiring and employment tools and maintain documentation to demonstrate absence of discriminatory intent.

[K&L Gates recommends](https://www.klgates.com/Pared-Back-Version-of-the-Texas-Responsible-Artificial-Intelligence-Governance-Act-Signed-Into-Law-6-24-2025) employers: (1) implement AI risk management policies; (2) train HR and management teams on AI governance; (3) audit AI systems used in employment decisions; and (4) obtain vendor representations that their AI tools do not intentionally discriminate.

### Comparison to Colorado AI Act

[Miller Nash's comparative analysis](https://www.millernash.com/industry-news/from-colorado-to-texas-how-states-are-rewriting-ai-laws) highlights the fundamental difference in regulatory philosophy:

| Feature | Colorado AI Act (SB 24-205) | Texas TRAIGA (HB 149) |
|---|---|---|
| Discrimination standard | "Reasonable care" to prevent algorithmic discrimination (disparate impact actionable) | Intent required; disparate impact alone insufficient |
| Primary targets | Private developers and deployers of high-risk AI | Government entities + targeted prohibitions on harmful AI |
| Impact assessments | Required for high-risk AI deployers | Not required |
| Consumer disclosures | Required for high-risk AI interactions | Required for government entities only |
| Enforcement | State AG | State AG only (no private right of action) |
| Effective date | June 30, 2026 (delayed from February 1, 2026) | January 1, 2026 |

[WilmerHale's analysis](https://www.wilmerhale.com/en/insights/blogs/wilmerhale-privacy-and-cybersecurity-law/20250721-texas-enacts-new-ai-law) characterizes TRAIGA as representing "a distinctive approach to AI regulation" that prioritizes prohibition of specific harms over affirmative compliance mandates — a model more aligned with Texas's generally business-friendly regulatory posture.

### Broader Policy Significance

TRAIGA's enactment as a scaled-back version of the original proposal illustrates a pattern in state AI legislation: broad initial proposals getting moderated through the legislative process in response to industry concerns. The [Goodwin Law analysis](https://www.goodwinlaw.com/en/insights/publications/2025/07/alerts-practices-dpc-with-traiga-lone-star-state-leans) notes that TRAIGA nonetheless represents a meaningful floor — particularly the social scoring prohibition and behavior manipulation ban, which address specific, emerging AI harms.

Federal preemption remains a background consideration. The Trump Administration's [AI Executive Order of December 2025](https://www.lw.com/en/insights/texas-signs-responsible-ai-governance-act-into-law) directed federal agencies to preempt state AI laws inconsistent with federal policy, creating potential conflict with TRAIGA's government disclosure and social scoring provisions. The extent of federal preemption of state AI laws remains unresolved as of early 2026.

## Action Items

- **Government entities operating in Texas:** Immediately audit all AI systems deployed in public-facing contexts for compliance with TRAIGA's disclosure requirements and social scoring prohibition. Effective date was January 1, 2026.
- **Private employers:** Conduct a documented audit of all AI-assisted employment decision tools (recruiting, performance management, compensation) to confirm they lack intentional discriminatory design; obtain vendor compliance representations.
- **AI developers and deployers:** Consider aligning with NIST AI Risk Management Framework practices to qualify for TRAIGA's safe harbor, even though not strictly required for private actors.
- **HR technology vendors:** Review product documentation and terms of service to ensure representations about non-discriminatory design are supportable and accurately conveyed to Texas-based clients.
- **Monitor federal preemption:** Track Commerce Department and DOJ AI preemption actions; federal guidance could limit enforcement of certain TRAIGA provisions.
- **Watch for AG guidance:** The Texas AG has yet to publish enforcement guidance or FAQs on TRAIGA implementation. Initial guidance may clarify cure period procedures and penalty calculation methodology.
