# Colorado AI Act: Hogan Lovells Data Chronicles Practitioner Analysis (SB 24-205)

**Jurisdiction:** Colorado | **Category:** AI Law | **Date:** June 6, 2024

## Executive Summary [MEDIUM confidence]

On May 17, 2024, Colorado Governor Jared Polis signed [Senate Bill 24-205](https://leg.colorado.gov/bills/sb24-205), making Colorado the first US state to enact a broadly applicable, cross-sectoral AI governance law. Hogan Lovells' global Privacy and Cybersecurity practice — through their podcast and video series ["The Data Chronicles"](https://www.hoganlovells.com/en/publications/the-data-chronicles-podcast-and-video-series) — analyzed the Colorado Artificial Intelligence Act (CAIA) in an episode hosted by Scott Loughlin with senior associate Sophie Baum. The episode, titled "Rocky Mountain High-Risk AI: Unveiling Colorado's AI Act," examined the law's risk-based framework, developer and deployer obligations, enforcement architecture, and practical preparation steps for companies ahead of the February 2026 (now June 30, 2026) effective date. Hogan Lovells characterized the CAIA as the first cross-sectoral AI governance law in the US, drawing comparisons to the EU AI Act's risk-tiered model. The episode emphasized that the law's rise reflects broader policymaker concern about consumer-facing AI, particularly generative AI interactions that "day-to-day folks and normal consumers interact with." The law's effective date was subsequently delayed from February 1, 2026 to June 30, 2026 by SB 25B-004, signed August 28, 2025.

## Background [HIGH confidence]

### Colorado's Regulatory First

Colorado enacted [SB 24-205](https://leg.colorado.gov/bills/sb24-205) as the first cross-sectoral AI governance statute in the United States. Unlike prior state AI laws that addressed narrow use cases — chatbot disclosure in California, employment screening audits in New York City, deepfake prohibitions — the Colorado Artificial Intelligence Act (CAIA) creates a comprehensive duty of care applicable across employment, education, healthcare, housing, financial services, insurance, legal services, and essential government services.

Governor Polis signed the bill on May 17, 2024, while simultaneously publishing a letter expressing reservations about the law's compliance complexity and calling for federal preemptive legislation. Despite those reservations, the bill's enactment triggered immediate law firm commentary across the US regulatory bar.

## Detailed Analysis [MEDIUM confidence]

### Risk-Based Framework and EU AI Act Comparison

Hogan Lovells' analysis — consistent with other major law firm commentaries — placed the CAIA's structure within the broader international context of risk-based AI governance. The firm characterized the CAIA as similar in architecture to the EU AI Act: both instruments apply differentiated obligations to AI systems used in high-stakes contexts, and both distinguish between developers (who build AI systems) and deployers (who use AI systems to make consequential decisions).

The CAIA imposes an overarching duty of "reasonable care" on both developers and deployers of "high-risk AI systems" to protect consumers from **algorithmic discrimination** — unlawful differential treatment or impact based on protected characteristics — in consequential decisions. A "high-risk AI system" is one that makes or is a "substantial factor" in making a **consequential decision**: a decision with material legal or similarly significant effect on a consumer's access to employment, education, healthcare, housing, financial services, insurance, legal services, or essential government services.

### Developer and Deployer Obligations

The Hogan Lovells analysis highlighted the dual-track obligation structure:

**Developers** (those who build or substantially modify high-risk AI systems) must:
- Use reasonable care to avoid algorithmic discrimination in their systems
- Disclose specified information to deployers, including intended uses, known limitations, bias evaluation approaches, and risk mitigation steps
- Publish public summaries of risk management practices
- Report discovered algorithmic discrimination to the Colorado Attorney General and affected deployers within 90 days

**Deployers** (those who use high-risk AI systems to make consequential decisions about Colorado consumers) bear the more operationally intensive obligations:
- Implement a risk management program aligned with recognized frameworks such as the [NIST AI Risk Management Framework](https://www.nist.gov/artificial-intelligence) or ISO/IEC 42001
- Conduct impact assessments prior to deployment, annually, and within 90 days of substantial modifications
- Provide consumer notices when a high-risk AI system makes or substantially factors into a consequential decision
- Offer consumers a mechanism to appeal adverse consequential decisions and request human review
- Publish public summaries of deployed high-risk AI systems

Hogan Lovells' practitioner framing on the podcast emphasized that companies should begin preparation ahead of the effective date — a message delivered in June 2024 when the February 2026 deadline was still 20 months away.

### Generative AI and the Consumer-Facing Trigger

A notable framing in Hogan Lovells' analysis was the explicit invocation of generative AI as a catalyst for the legislation. The firm noted that the rise of consumer-facing generative AI products — tools that "day-to-day folks and normal consumers interact with" — spurred legislators to act. This context explains the CAIA's separate general disclosure requirement: any business deploying an AI system intended to interact with consumers must disclose that the consumer is interacting with AI rather than a human, regardless of whether the system qualifies as "high-risk." This general disclosure provision extends the law's reach beyond the high-risk AI compliance track to all consumer-facing AI deployments.

### Enforcement Architecture

The Colorado Attorney General holds exclusive enforcement authority under the CAIA; there is no private right of action. The [Colorado AG's AI page](https://coag.gov/ai/) serves as the rulemaking and enforcement hub. Violations constitute unfair trade practices under the Colorado Consumer Protection Act, exposing violators to civil penalties of up to $20,000 per violation. The AG also has authority to seek injunctive relief and restitution.

## Implementation Timeline and Post-Enactment Developments [HIGH confidence]

The podcast episode was recorded and published in June 2024, referencing the then-current February 1, 2026 effective date. Subsequent legislative developments materially altered the timeline:

- **August 2025 Special Session:** Industry opposition led to a special legislative session. The session produced no substantive amendments — four competing bills were introduced but collapsed after technology company objections.
- **SB 25B-004 (August 28, 2025):** Governor Polis signed this bill, delaying the effective date from February 1, 2026 to **June 30, 2026**. All substantive obligations remain unchanged.
- **March 2026 — ADMT Framework Proposal:** Colorado's AI Policy Work Group unanimously approved a proposal to replace SB 24-205 entirely with an "Automated Decision-Making Technology (ADMT) Framework," targeting a January 1, 2027 effective date. Hogan Lovells separately analyzed this development in ["Mile-High Machine Learning: New Policy Framework Would Significantly Alter Colorado AI Act"](https://www.hoganlovells.com/en/publications/milehigh-machine-learning-new-policy-framework-would-significantly-alter-colorado-ai-act) and ["Updates to Colorado AI Act Expected"](https://www.hoganlovells.com/en/publications/updates-to-colorado-ai-act-expected).
- **Federal Preemption Pressure:** President Trump's December 2025 executive order on AI identified state AI laws as potential obstacles to national policy, with Colorado's CAIA widely cited as a primary target. Hogan Lovells has tracked this development through its ongoing AI Hub practice resource.

## Impact Assessment [MEDIUM confidence]

### Broad Industry Scope

The CAIA's consequential decision categories — employment, healthcare, financial services, education, housing, insurance, legal services, essential government services — capture a wide swath of Colorado-operating businesses. Any company using vendor-supplied AI tools in these contexts qualifies as a "deployer" and faces the full set of deployer obligations; it need not develop or own the AI system.

Hogan Lovells' practitioner framing underscored that organizations should assess their AI system inventories early, categorizing each deployment by whether it makes or substantially influences consequential decisions about Colorado consumers.

### Compliance Preparation Timeline

As of April 2026, two possible effective dates remain in play:
- **June 30, 2026** — if the ADMT Framework replacement does not pass or is not signed before that date, SB 24-205 takes effect in its current form
- **January 1, 2027** — proposed effective date for the ADMT Framework replacement, if enacted

Organizations should maintain parallel compliance preparation for both scenarios, as the substantive obligations under either framework — documented AI governance, consumer disclosures, appeal mechanisms — substantially overlap.

## Action Items

- Inventory all AI systems used in consequential decisions affecting Colorado consumers; determine whether each qualifies as a "high-risk AI system" under the CAIA's cascading definitions.
- Identify whether your organization is a "developer," a "deployer," or both; many organizations deploying third-party AI tools are deployers with the full impact-assessment and risk-management program obligations.
- Implement the CAIA's general AI interaction disclosure for all consumer-facing AI deployments regardless of high-risk status — this obligation applies broadly to any business deploying consumer-facing AI in Colorado.
- Audit AI vendor contracts to ensure developers are providing the documentation deployers need for impact assessments (intended uses, bias evaluation, limitation disclosures).
- Align any risk management program with the NIST AI RMF 1.0 or ISO/IEC 42001; NIST compliance provides an affirmative defense under the CAIA.
- Monitor the 2026 Colorado legislative session for ADMT Framework replacement legislation; build modular compliance programs that can adapt if the law's substance changes before June 30, 2026.
- Monitor Colorado Attorney General rulemaking guidance expected ahead of the enforcement date.
- Consult the [Hogan Lovells AI Hub](https://digital-client-solutions.hoganlovells.com/resources/ai-hub/jurisdiction/us) for ongoing updates, particularly regarding the ADMT Framework and federal preemption developments.
