# Utah and Colorado Pioneer Contrasting State AI Regulatory Models: Disclosure vs. Risk-Based Frameworks

**Jurisdiction:** Utah, Colorado | **Category:** AI Law | **Date:** June 6, 2024

## Executive Summary [HIGH confidence]

In the first half of 2024, Utah and Colorado enacted the United States' first state-level AI-specific statutes, establishing two distinct regulatory templates that other states are expected to follow. Utah's [Artificial Intelligence Policy Act (SB 149)](https://le.utah.gov/~2024/bills/static/SB0149.html), signed March 13, 2024 and effective May 1, 2024, takes a narrow, disclosure-first approach focused on ensuring consumers know when they are interacting with generative AI. Colorado's [Senate Bill 24-205](https://leg.colorado.gov/bills/sb24-205), signed May 17, 2024 and originally set to take effect February 1, 2026 (subsequently delayed to June 30, 2026), is a comprehensive, risk-based framework that imposes affirmative obligations on developers and deployers of "high-risk" AI systems making consequential decisions. As [Cooley LLP observed at the time of enactment](https://cdp.cooley.com/utah-colorado-pave-way-for-ai-specific-state-laws-is-your-company-ready-for-the-impending-regulation-wave/), these two laws "pave the way" for a broader state-level regulation wave and represent divergent but complementary approaches companies must now navigate. The patchwork of state AI regulation has since accelerated substantially, with all 50 states introducing AI-related bills in 2025. **Note (April 2026):** Colorado's risk-based framework faces existential uncertainty — a draft repeal-and-replace bill released in March 2026 would abandon the law's duty-of-care, impact assessment, and risk management obligations in favor of a narrower automated decision-making technology disclosure regime before the June 30, 2026 effective date.

## Background [HIGH confidence]

### Federal Vacuum Drives State Action

By early 2024, the United States lacked any binding federal AI statute applicable to private-sector companies. The Biden administration's October 2023 executive order on AI directed agencies to develop standards, and the [NIST AI Risk Management Framework](https://www.nist.gov/artificial-intelligence) provided voluntary guidance — but neither created enforceable obligations on commercial AI developers or deployers. This federal vacuum pushed states to act independently, following the same trajectory that produced the current US state privacy law patchwork.

Utah acted first. In the 2024 legislative session, the Utah General Assembly passed SB 149, signed by Governor Spencer Cox on March 13, 2024, with an effective date of May 1, 2024. Colorado followed on May 17, 2024 when Governor Jared Polis signed SB 24-205 — the Colorado Artificial Intelligence Act (CAIA) — with a longer runway to compliance (originally February 1, 2026).

### The Two Contrasting Regulatory Models

The two laws represent structurally different philosophies:

- **Utah's model:** Disclosure-centric, consumer protection-integrated, innovation-supportive. Amends existing consumer protection statutes rather than creating new AI-specific law.
- **Colorado's model:** Risk-based, comprehensive, algorithmic discrimination-focused. Draws explicitly from the EU AI Act's tiered risk approach, imposing affirmative substantive obligations on developers and deployers.

Both models were studied by legislative drafters in other states as 2024 and 2025 AI bill waves developed. Over 600 AI-related bills were introduced across US states in 2024, with nearly 100 enacted; that figure grew to 1,200 bills in 2025.

## Detailed Analysis [HIGH confidence]

### Utah Artificial Intelligence Policy Act (SB 149)

**Scope and Coverage.** The UAIPA applies to entities using "generative AI" in consumer interactions. "Generative AI" is defined as a system that: (a) is trained on data; (b) interacts with a person via text, audio, or visual communication; and (c) generates non-scripted outputs similar to human outputs, with limited human oversight. This definition covers large-language-model-based consumer tools and AI-driven customer service but excludes scripted chatbots and traditional rule-based decision support tools.

**Two-Tier Disclosure Framework.** The UAIPA creates differentiated disclosure obligations based on entity type:

1. **Regulated Occupations (Proactive Disclosure).** Entities in occupations requiring a Utah Department of Commerce license or certification — including physicians, nurses, attorneys, accountants, engineers, pharmacists, social workers, and others — must "prominently" disclose at the outset of any AI-assisted service interaction that the consumer is interacting with generative AI. For oral interactions, this means verbal disclosure at the start of the exchange; for electronic interactions, a written notice before the exchange begins.

2. **General Consumer-Facing Businesses (On-Request Disclosure).** Any entity engaging in UDCP-regulated consumer protection activities that uses generative AI in consumer interactions must disclose AI use when "asked or prompted" by the consumer. The statute requires the AI system itself to be capable of making this disclosure on request.

**No "AI Defense."** The UAIPA expressly forecloses the argument that generative AI — rather than the deploying entity — was responsible for a consumer protection violation. Deployers bear full accountability for AI-generated content or conduct.

**Office of Artificial Intelligence Policy and AI Learning Laboratory.** The UAIPA creates the [Office of Artificial Intelligence Policy](https://le.utah.gov/~2024/bills/static/SB0149.html) within the Utah Department of Commerce. The Office administers an AI Learning Laboratory Program — a regulatory sandbox allowing companies to apply for up to two years of "regulatory mitigation," including reduced fines and cure periods, while developing or testing AI systems. This innovation-protective feature reflects Utah's dual goals of consumer protection and attracting AI development.

**Enforcement.** The Utah Division of Consumer Protection (UDCP) may impose administrative fines of up to **$2,500 per violation**; the Utah Attorney General may separately seek civil penalties of up to **$5,000 per violation** of an existing order. No private right of action exists. Courts in UDCP-initiated proceedings may also enjoin violations and order disgorgement.

**2025 Amendments.** In March 2025, the Utah legislature enacted [SB 226](https://le.utah.gov/~2025/bills/static/SB0226.html) (signed March 27, 2025) and SB 332 (signed March 25, 2025), both effective May 7, 2025. These amendments narrowed the proactive disclosure requirement to apply only to "high-risk" AI interactions — defined as those combining collection of sensitive personal information with provision of personalized recommendations or advice that could influence significant personal decisions. SB 332 extended the UAIPA's sunset date to July 1, 2027.

### Colorado Artificial Intelligence Act (SB 24-205)

**Scope and Coverage.** The [CAIA](https://leg.colorado.gov/bills/sb24-205) applies to "developers" and "deployers" of "high-risk AI systems." A high-risk AI system is one that makes or is a substantial factor in making a "consequential decision" — defined to include decisions affecting employment, education, financial services, healthcare, housing, insurance, legal services, or government benefits and services. The law thus covers any AI system embedded in hiring pipelines, loan origination platforms, healthcare decision support, insurance underwriting, or similar high-stakes contexts.

**Developer Obligations.** Developers of high-risk AI systems must:
- Exercise reasonable care to protect against algorithmic discrimination.
- Provide deployers with documentation detailing the system's purpose, foreseeable uses, known risks, training data summaries, and risk mitigation measures.
- Make available a public website summarizing their high-risk AI systems and discrimination risk management approaches.
- Notify the Colorado Attorney General and known deployers within 90 days of discovering that a high-risk system has caused or is likely to cause algorithmic discrimination.

**Deployer Obligations.** Deployers of high-risk AI systems must:
- Implement a written risk management policy and governance framework for each high-risk system.
- Complete an impact assessment before deployment and update it as systems are modified.
- Annually review each deployed high-risk system to verify it is not causing algorithmic discrimination.
- Notify consumers when a high-risk AI system is used to make a consequential decision, including disclosing the system's purpose, the nature of the decision, and the consumer's right to appeal adverse decisions.
- Allow consumers to correct inaccurate personal data used by the AI system.
- Maintain compliance documentation for three years after system discontinuation.

There is a rebuttable presumption that a developer or deployer used reasonable care if they have complied with the listed statutory provisions, effectively establishing a safe harbor for documented compliance programs.

**Enforcement.** The Colorado Attorney General has exclusive enforcement authority under the CAIA, incorporated into the Colorado Consumer Protection Act (CCPA). Civil penalties under the CCPA can reach **$20,000 per violation**. No private right of action exists.

**Implementation Delays and Amendments.** Governor Polis signed SB 24-205 with explicit reservations, publicly urging the legislature to significantly improve the law before its effective date. A Governor's AI Policy Work Group was established and has been meeting to develop consensus amendments, including actively throughout 2025 and into 2026. The 2025 regular legislative session did not produce significant amendments. Instead, on August 28, 2025, Governor Polis signed [SB 25B-004](https://leg.colorado.gov/bills/sb25b-004), which pushed the compliance deadline from February 1, 2026 to June 30, 2026.

**April 2026 Status — Draft Repeal-and-Replace Framework.** As of April 2026, the Colorado AI Act's core risk-based framework faces a serious prospect of replacement before the June 30, 2026 effective date. On March 17, 2026, the [Colorado AI Policy Work Group released a draft repeal-and-replace framework](https://www.coloradopolitics.com/2026/03/17/artificial-intelligence-working-group-agrees-on-framework-to-replace-colorado-law/) with strong backing from Governor Polis. As analyzed by [Mayer Brown](https://www.mayerbrown.com/en/insights/publications/2026/03/the-colorado-ai-policy-work-group-proposes-an-updated-framework-to-replace-the-colorado-ai-act) and [Hogan Lovells](https://www.hoganlovells.com/en/publications/milehigh-machine-learning-new-policy-framework-would-significantly-alter-colorado-ai-act), the draft bill would:

- Replace the "high-risk AI system" / consequential decisions framework with a narrower "Automated Decision-Making Technology" (ADMT) disclosure regime.
- Eliminate the duty of reasonable care, the incident reporting requirement to the Colorado AG, mandatory impact assessments, and risk management governance frameworks.
- Replace those obligations with transparency and notice requirements, including public-facing disclosures when AI is used in consequential decisions and on-request explanations and human review rights for individuals subject to adverse AI-assisted decisions.
- Push the effective date back further, to January 1, 2027.

As [reported by Colorado Officials Push to Repeal and Replace](https://www.insideglobaltech.com/2026/03/27/colorado-officials-push-to-repeal-and-replace-the-colorado-ai-act/), Colorado state officials are actively pushing to enact the replacement legislation before June 30, 2026. The draft bill had not yet been formally introduced in the General Assembly as of late March 2026. The June 30, 2026 effective date of the existing CAIA remains on the books; if the legislature does not act, the original risk-based framework would take effect on that date.

**Compliance Guidance.** Given the uncertainty, [Brownstein Hyatt Farber Schreck advises](https://www.bhfs.com/insight/colorados-landmark-ai-law-coming-online-what-developers-and-deployers-should-know/) that companies should continue compliance preparations for the existing CAIA requirements while monitoring whether the replacement framework passes before June 30, 2026.

### Key Comparative Differences

| Dimension | Utah (SB 149) | Colorado (SB 24-205) |
|---|---|---|
| Regulatory model | Disclosure-first, innovation-supportive | Risk-based, comprehensive (pending replacement) |
| Scope | Consumer-facing generative AI | High-risk AI making consequential decisions |
| Key obligation | Disclose AI use on request or proactively | Risk assessments, impact assessments, anti-discrimination programs |
| Effective date | May 1, 2024 | June 30, 2026 (delayed; replacement may push to Jan 1, 2027) |
| Enforcement | Utah DCP ($2,500/violation) | Colorado AG ($20,000/violation) |
| Private right of action | No | No |
| Regulatory sandbox | Yes (AI Learning Laboratory) | No |
| EU AI Act alignment | Limited | Explicit (risk-tiered structure) |

## Impact Assessment [HIGH confidence]

### Affected Entities

**Utah.** The UAIPA as originally enacted applied to (1) licensed professionals under the Utah Department of Commerce — healthcare providers, attorneys, accountants, engineers, architects, and others — and (2) any business engaging in UDCP-regulated consumer activities that uses consumer-facing generative AI. After the 2025 amendments, the proactive disclosure obligation is narrowed to "high-risk" interactions involving sensitive data and significant personal advice. However, the on-request disclosure duty remains broadly applicable.

**Colorado.** The CAIA as enacted covers any entity that develops or deploys AI systems making consequential decisions affecting Colorado residents in the eight covered domains (employment, education, financial services, healthcare, housing, insurance, legal services, government services). If the draft repeal-and-replace framework is enacted, the ADMT disclosure regime would cover a somewhat narrower set of entities and eliminate the most burdensome compliance obligations — though notice, correction, and human review rights would still apply.

**National Businesses.** Companies operating nationally must assess their obligations under both laws and any other state AI statutes that have since been enacted. The compliance frameworks are materially different, creating genuine multi-state compliance complexity.

### Compliance Requirements and Timelines

For **Utah-only compliance**, the primary obligations are:
- Audit consumer-facing AI tools for UAIPA "generative AI" definition coverage.
- Implement on-request disclosure capability across all AI-consumer interfaces.
- For regulated occupations: implement proactive disclosures for "high-risk" AI interactions (post-2025 amendment standard).
- Remove any AI-as-intervening-cause reasoning from compliance and legal defense playbooks.
- Consider AI Learning Laboratory participation for novel use cases.

For **Colorado compliance**, given the unsettled status:
- Continue mapping AI systems that could constitute "high-risk" systems under the consequential-decision definition (required if the original CAIA takes effect June 30, 2026).
- Simultaneously assess obligations under the draft ADMT framework (notice, correction rights, human review workflows) as the likely replacement regime.
- Monitor legislative developments closely through May and June 2026.
- For developers: maintain readiness on system documentation packages for deployers regardless of which framework prevails.
- Maintain documentation for three years post-deployment.

### Enforcement Outlook [HIGH confidence]

Utah has had an active disclosure framework since May 2024. No significant public UDCP enforcement actions under the UAIPA have been identified, consistent with an enforcement program that remains nascent. Colorado's law does not become operative until June 30, 2026 at the earliest, and the Colorado AG has not yet brought CAIA-specific enforcement actions.

Both laws face an uncertain long-term outlook given two federal developments. First, the Trump administration's December 2025 executive order directed the DOJ to challenge state AI laws that conflict with federal AI policy. Second, and separately, on March 20, 2026, the White House released the [National Policy Framework for Artificial Intelligence](https://www.whitehouse.gov/releases/2026/03/president-donald-j-trump-unveils-national-ai-legislative-framework/) — a legislative recommendations document transmitted to Congress urging preemption of state AI laws that impose "undue burdens" on AI development. As analyzed by [Ropes & Gray](https://www.ropesgray.com/en/insights/alerts/2026/03/the-white-house-legislative-recommendations-national-policy-framework-for-artificial-intelligence-an) and [WilmerHale](https://www.wilmerhale.com/en/insights/blogs/wilmerhale-privacy-and-cybersecurity-law/20260323-white-house-releases-national-policy-framework-for-artificial-intelligence), the Framework is not itself binding law but outlines a proposed federal preemption regime that, if enacted by Congress, would bar states from regulating AI development or imposing undue burdens on lawful AI use. The proposed carveouts would preserve states' authority over child safety, fraud prevention, and consumer protection laws — which could shield Utah's disclosure-centric UAIPA more than Colorado's broader duty-of-care framework. Utah's relatively narrow, disclosure-based framework may face less preemption pressure than Colorado's more comprehensive, substantive framework in any event.

### The Emerging State AI Regulation Wave

As [Cooley LLP observed](https://cdp.cooley.com/utah-colorado-pave-way-for-ai-specific-state-laws-is-your-company-ready-for-the-impending-regulation-wave/) at the time of these enactments, Utah and Colorado were "paving the way" for broader state AI regulation. That prediction has been borne out: over 600 AI-related state bills were introduced in 2024 with nearly 100 enacted; in 2025, all 50 US states introduced AI-related bills for the first time, with 145 enacted. States have generally trended toward narrower, transparency-focused approaches rather than replicating Colorado's comprehensive framework, though requirements vary significantly.

## Action Items

- Determine whether your organization is a "developer" or "deployer" under Colorado SB 24-205, or a regulated or non-regulated entity under Utah's UAIPA, as these characterizations determine the applicable compliance obligations.
- For Colorado-scope entities: do not stand down on compliance preparation — the June 30, 2026 effective date of the existing CAIA remains on the books, and the replacement framework has not yet been formally introduced or enacted. Continue building compliance infrastructure while monitoring the draft ADMT framework's legislative progress.
- For Utah-scope entities: verify that consumer-facing AI systems can disclose AI status on request; for regulated occupations, assess whether any interactions meet the "high-risk" post-2025 amendment threshold requiring proactive disclosure.
- Conduct an enterprise-wide audit of all AI systems to identify which may constitute "high-risk" systems under Colorado's consequential-decision definition, and separately assess whether they would qualify as "automated decision-making technology" under the draft replacement framework.
- Track federal preemption developments: monitor both the DOJ AI litigation posture under the December 2025 executive order and congressional action on the White House's March 2026 National Policy Framework legislative recommendations.
- Assign ongoing monitoring responsibility for new state AI legislation, as the state patchwork continues to grow.
