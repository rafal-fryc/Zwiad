# Tennessee ELVIS Act: First State Law Targeting AI Voice Cloning and Right of Publicity

**Jurisdiction:** Tennessee | **Category:** AI Law | **Date:** June 6, 2024

## Executive Summary [HIGH confidence]

Tennessee Governor Bill Lee signed the Ensuring Likeness, Voice, and Image Security (ELVIS) Act on March 21, 2024, making Tennessee the first state to enact legislation specifically targeting AI-generated voice cloning and deepfakes in the context of right of publicity law. The Act (Tenn. Code Ann. §§ 47-25-1101 et seq.) amends Tennessee's Personal Rights Protection Act of 1984 to add "voice" as a protected attribute alongside name, photograph, and likeness, and introduces three separate causes of civil action, including a novel secondary-liability provision that directly targets providers of AI tools or algorithms whose "primary purpose or function" is to generate unauthorized voice or likeness replicas. The legislation passed both chambers of the Tennessee General Assembly unanimously (93-0 in the House; 30-0 in the Senate) and took effect on July 1, 2024. Criminal enforcement as a Class A misdemeanor — carrying up to 11 months and 29 days incarceration and fines up to $2,500 — supplements civil remedies including injunctive relief, actual damages, profits, and attorney's fees. The ELVIS Act serves as the leading state model for federal proposals including the NO FAKES Act and the No AI FRAUD Act.

## Background [HIGH confidence]

### Origins in Elvis Presley's Estate

Tennessee's Personal Rights Protection Act of 1984 was itself landmark legislation. Enacted primarily to address the postmortem exploitation of [Elvis Presley's name and likeness](https://www.tn.gov/governor/news/2024/1/10/tennessee-first-in-the-nation-to-address-ai-impact-on-music-industry.html) after courts questioned whether the estate could rely solely on copyright, trademark, and false-endorsement claims, the 1984 Act established a statutory, property-based right of publicity in Tennessee that survives death and is descendible to heirs and assigns. For four decades, the statute protected commercial use of an individual's name, photograph, and likeness, but did not explicitly cover voice.

### The AI Voice-Cloning Crisis

By 2023, generative AI voice-cloning technology had matured to the point where near-perfect audio replicas of artists could be produced and distributed at scale without consent. A high-profile incident in April 2023 — an AI-generated song mimicking Drake and The Weeknd that went viral and accrued millions of streams before platforms removed it — crystallized the urgency for the music industry. Nashville-based artists, the Recording Industry Association of America (RIAA), and the Recording Academy lobbied Tennessee officials for updated statutory protections.

Governor Lee announced exploratory work on AI music-industry legislation in [January 2024](https://www.tn.gov/governor/news/2024/1/10/tennessee-first-in-the-nation-to-address-ai-impact-on-music-industry.html), describing Tennessee as the first state to address the AI threat to music. The legislative effort that followed produced the ELVIS Act.

### Legislative History

The bill was introduced in the Tennessee General Assembly's 113th session as [House Bill 2091](https://www.capitol.tn.gov/Bills/113/Bill/HB2091.pdf) by Rep. William Lamberth (R-44) and Senate Bill 2096 by Sen. Jack Johnson (R-27), with bipartisan sponsorship and backing as a Governor's Bill. Key milestones:

- **January 10, 2024**: Governor Lee announces AI music-industry legislation initiative.
- **February 2024**: HB 2091 passes the House Banking and Consumer Affairs Subcommittee.
- **March 4, 2024**: Tennessee House passes HB 2091 by 93-0.
- **March 7, 2024**: Tennessee Senate passes SB 2096 by 30-0.
- **March 21, 2024**: Governor Lee [signs the ELVIS Act](https://www.tn.gov/governor/news/2024/3/21/photos--gov--lee-signs-elvis-act-into-law.html) at Robert's Western World, a Nashville honky-tonk, with artists including Chris Janson and Luke Bryan in attendance.
- **July 1, 2024**: ELVIS Act takes effect.

The RIAA [hailed the signing](https://www.riaa.com/elvis-act-becomes-law-as-tennessee-leads-the-nation/) as a milestone for artist protection and called on Congress to enact similar federal legislation.

## Detailed Analysis [HIGH confidence]

### Renaming and Structural Changes

The ELVIS Act formally renames the "Personal Rights Protection Act of 1984" to the "Ensuring Likeness, Voice, and Image Security Act of 2024." This renaming is substantive: it signals that the statute is no longer a narrow postmortem celebrity-estate protection but a living instrument to address contemporary AI-driven misuse of personal identity.

### Key Definitions

**"Voice"** (new definition): The Act defines voice as "a sound in a medium that is readily identifiable and attributable to a particular individual, regardless of whether the sound contains the actual voice or a simulation of the voice of the individual." ([HB 2091, Section 9](https://www.capitol.tn.gov/Bills/113/Bill/HB2091.pdf))

This definition is technology-neutral and intentionally broad. It captures AI-generated simulations that a listener would attribute to a specific person, even if no actual recordings of that person were used to train the model. It covers musicians, voice actors, podcasters, public figures, and any individual with a sufficiently distinctive voice.

**"Likeness"** (existing, extended): The Act retains the 1984 Act's coverage of name, photograph, and likeness. All references in the statute now add "voice" as the fourth protected attribute, creating a "NIL+V" (Name, Image, Likeness, and Voice) protection framework.

**Authorization requirements**: For living individuals, authorization means consent from the individual. For minors, authorization from a parent or guardian. For deceased individuals, authorization from an executor, heir, or assign of the publicity right. The right is freely assignable and descendible.

### Three Causes of Civil Action

[Section 6 of HB 2091](https://www.capitol.tn.gov/Bills/113/Bill/HB2091.pdf) creates three distinct bases for civil liability under Tenn. Code Ann. § 47-25-1105(a):

**Cause 1 — Commercial use without authorization (§ 47-25-1105(a)(1))**: A person is liable who knowingly uses an individual's name, photograph, voice, or likeness for the purpose of advertising products or services, fundraising, or solicitation, without authorization. This extends the 1984 Act's core prohibition to include voice.

**Cause 2 — Unauthorized distribution of voice or likeness (§ 47-25-1105(a)(2))**: A person is liable who "publishes, performs, distributes, transmits, or otherwise makes available to the public an individual's voice or likeness, with knowledge that use of the voice or likeness was not authorized." Unlike Cause 1, this provision is **not limited to commercial contexts**. Any public distribution of unauthorized voice or likeness content — including posting an AI-generated clip on social media — potentially falls within this cause of action.

**Cause 3 — Distribution of AI tools for unauthorized voice/likeness generation (§ 47-25-1105(a)(3))**: The most novel provision. A person is liable who "distributes, transmits, or otherwise makes available an algorithm, software, tool, or other technology, service, or device, the primary purpose or function of which is the production of an individual's photograph, voice, or likeness without authorization." This creates a form of secondary liability targeting developers and distributors of purpose-built AI voice-cloning and deepfake services.

### Secondary Liability for AI Technology Providers

The third cause of action is the ELVIS Act's most legally innovative — and most debated — provision. As [Latham & Watkins analyzed](https://www.lw.com/admin/upload/SiteAttachments/The-ELVIS-Act-Tennessee-Shakes-Up-Its-Right-of-Publicity-Law-and-Takes-On-Generative-AI.pdf), it imposes liability on AI developers that goes beyond the secondary liability frameworks contemplated by most existing state right-of-publicity statutes.

The operative limiting principle — "primary purpose or function" — is undefined in the statute. [Saul Ewing noted](https://www.saul.com/insights/article/elvis-act-tennessee-law-addresses-ais-impact-music-industry) the ambiguity: a general-purpose text-to-speech tool is unlikely to qualify; a service specifically marketed for "celebrity voice cloning" almost certainly would. Tools that are capable of voice cloning but also serve legitimate use cases — audio editing, accessibility, entertainment production — present the most uncertain exposure and will likely require judicial clarification.

[Davis Wright Tremaine's analysis](https://www.dwt.com/blogs/artificial-intelligence-law-advisor/2024/04/tennessee-elvis-act-ai-voice-replica) raises the additional question of whether Section 230 of the Communications Decency Act shields platforms that host user-generated AI voice content. Because the Act does not explicitly carve out "interactive computer service providers" and the second cause of action covers entities that "transmit or otherwise make available" infringing content, platforms face arguments that Section 230 immunity may not apply to state right-of-publicity claims of this type.

### Knowledge Standard

The Act amends the prior "had knowledge" standard to read "had knowledge or reasonably should have known" (Tenn. Code Ann. § 47-25-1107(c)). This negligence-adjacent standard expands liability relative to the 1984 Act's actual-knowledge requirement, and means that defendants cannot evade liability by willful ignorance of whether authorization was obtained.

### Record Label Enforcement Standing

The Act expressly provides that where an individual has entered into an exclusive recording contract as a recording artist, or granted an exclusive license to distribute sound recordings featuring the individual's voice, **either the individual or the contracting party (i.e., a record label)** may bring an enforcement action. This provision addresses the practical reality that record labels, not individual artists, often bear the primary economic injury from unauthorized AI voice use and have greater litigation resources to pursue enforcement.

### Exemptions and Fair Use Carve-Outs

[Tenn. Code Ann. § 47-25-1107](https://www.capitol.tn.gov/Bills/113/Bill/HB2091.pdf) preserves and modifies several liability exemptions. Uses are not actionable if they are:

- In connection with news, public affairs, or sports broadcast or account;
- For the purpose of comment, criticism, scholarship, satire, or parody;
- A representation in an audiovisual work that does not create a false impression that the work is an authentic recording of that individual's participation;
- Fleeting or incidental use;
- Advertisements for the above-listed permitted uses.

The Act also incorporates the First Amendment as an express limiting principle: to the extent a use of voice or likeness is protected by the First Amendment, it remains non-actionable.

However, [Latham & Watkins and Phelps both observe](https://www.phelps.com/insights/the-elvis-act-protects-artists-voices-but-creates-risks-for-media-companies.html) that the ELVIS Act **narrowed** several prior exemptions relative to the 1984 Act, potentially expanding liability for media companies. The "fleeting or incidental use" exemption is now more tightly scoped. The satire and parody defenses survive but are bounded: a work that creates a "false impression of authenticity" is not protected even if comedic intent is present.

### Criminal Enforcement

[Tenn. Code Ann. § 39-14-152(g)](https://www.capitol.tn.gov/Bills/113/Bill/HB2091.pdf) preserves criminal liability for willful violations. Willful violation of the ELVIS Act constitutes a **Class A misdemeanor**, punishable by:

- Up to **11 months and 29 days** of incarceration;
- Fines up to **$2,500**.

This criminal provision is enforced by the state, not by private plaintiffs, and requires willfulness — a higher standard than the civil "knew or reasonably should have known" formulation.

### Civil Remedies

Civil plaintiffs may seek:

- **Injunctive relief** to prevent or restrain unauthorized use;
- **Impoundment and destruction** of infringing materials;
- **Actual damages** plus any profits attributable to the unauthorized use;
- **Attorney's fees** in appropriate cases.

## Impact Assessment [HIGH confidence]

### Entertainment and Music Industry

The Act primarily targets AI exploitation of musician voices but extends to any individual with a sufficiently distinctive, identifiable voice. Nashville-based artists and record labels were the most visible proponents, and the Act's enforcement-standing provision explicitly accommodates label-initiated enforcement.

For record labels and artists, the Act provides new legal tools to combat AI voice services that train on protected recordings and offer cloning of specific artists. The [RIAA](https://www.riaa.com/elvis-act-becomes-law-as-tennessee-leads-the-nation/) and [Recording Academy](https://www.recordingacademy.com/advocacy/news/tennessee-victory-bill-lee-elvis-act) both positioned the Act as a precedent-setting tool and called for federal action.

### AI Platform Developers and Distributors

Multiple law firms — [Armstrong Teasdale](https://www.armstrongteasdale.com/thought-leadership/artificial-intelligence-and-copyrights-tennessees-elvis-act-becomes-law/), [Skadden](https://www.skadden.com/insights/publications/2024/04/tennessee-law-addresses-proliferation-of-deepfakes), and [ArentFox Schiff](https://www.afslaw.com/perspectives/alerts/elvis-alive-tennessee-first-implement-rights-publicity-protections-against-ai) — note that the Act creates new litigation exposure for AI developers and distributors operating in Tennessee or whose services are accessible in Tennessee.

The territorial reach of the Act is unresolved: because the statute does not specify a geographic nexus, there is uncertainty about whether a California-based AI company with no physical presence in Tennessee could be subject to suit if a Tennessee artist's voice is cloned using the company's tool. This extraterritorial ambiguity mirrors issues encountered under similar state biometric and privacy statutes.

Companies operating voice-synthesis, audio-deepfake, or voice-conversion services should assess whether their tools' "primary purpose or function" could be characterized as producing unauthorized voice replicas, regardless of other use cases the tools support. This analysis cannot wait for judicial clarification, as the Act's civil remedies are available from the July 1, 2024 effective date.

### Media Companies

[Phelps' analysis](https://www.phelps.com/insights/the-elvis-act-protects-artists-voices-but-creates-risks-for-media-companies.html) warns that media companies that republish, broadcast, or host audio or video content may face exposure under Cause 2 (distribution with knowledge of unauthorized use) if they knowingly disseminate AI-generated content. The narrowing of fair use and incidental use exemptions increases this risk for documentary filmmakers, news broadcasters, and satire producers who may incorporate AI-generated elements.

### Legal and Constitutional Challenges

[Latham & Watkins](https://www.lw.com/admin/upload/SiteAttachments/The-ELVIS-Act-Tennessee-Shakes-Up-Its-Right-of-Publicity-Law-and-Takes-On-Generative-AI.pdf) identifies two primary vectors for legal challenge:

**Federal copyright preemption**: The Copyright Act expressly preempts state laws that create rights equivalent to any exclusive rights within the general scope of copyright. Certain applications of the ELVIS Act — particularly to sound recordings — may conflict with federal copyright law, generating preemption defenses for defendants.

**First Amendment**: Broadly read, the Act's Cause 2 (non-commercial distribution of unauthorized voice) could reach political satire, documentary journalism, and artistic expression. The Act's First Amendment carve-out is textual but its scope will require judicial elaboration. Academic critics, including those writing for the [Michigan Journal of Economics](https://sites.lsa.umich.edu/mje/2024/05/13/tennessees-new-elvis-act-free-speech-free-market/), have raised concerns about the Act's breadth and lack of the stringent exceptions typically found in right of privacy statutes.

[IPWatchdog](https://ipwatchdog.com/2024/04/23/briefing-tennessees-elvis-act-attempt-protect-musicians-record-labels-ai-era-enforceable/) has raised the practical enforceability question: the Act targets technologies and behaviors that are often hosted outside Tennessee by companies in other jurisdictions, creating significant challenges for effective enforcement.

### Federal Legislative Influence

The ELVIS Act is explicitly positioned as a model for federal legislation. The bipartisan [NO FAKES Act](https://www.congress.gov/bill/118th-congress/senate-bill/4875) (Senate; discussion draft circulated September 2023; formally introduced as S.4875 in July 2024) would create a federal digital replica right. The [No AI FRAUD Act](https://www.congress.gov/bill/118th-congress/house-bill/6943) (House, introduced January 2024) would establish a federal voice and likeness right. Tennessee's unanimous enactment created political momentum for federal action; neither bill had passed as of the date of this report.

## Action Items

- **AI voice and image service providers**: Audit your product's "primary purpose or function" against Cause 3's secondary-liability provision. If your service is marketed or primarily used for generating identifiable voice or likeness replicas of specific individuals, retain counsel experienced in right-of-publicity law to assess Tennessee exposure immediately — the Act has been in effect since July 1, 2024.
- **Record labels and artist management**: Review existing recording contracts and license agreements to confirm enforcement standing under the ELVIS Act is properly allocated. Both the artist and the label have independent standing; coordinate pre-litigation strategy.
- **Media companies and platforms**: Review content moderation and acquisition policies for AI-generated audio and video content. The "knew or reasonably should have known" standard under Cause 2 is broader than actual-knowledge requirements under prior law — build editorial processes that prevent knowing distribution of unauthorized AI voice content.
- **General-purpose AI developers**: Document legitimate use cases and design choices that distinguish your tool from purpose-built voice-cloning services. Maintain records showing that your product's "primary purpose or function" is not the unauthorized reproduction of identifiable voices. This documentation will be critical in any "primary purpose" defense.
- **Compliance teams**: Track the NO FAKES Act and No AI FRAUD Act federally. A national statute would create a uniform framework and may preempt or complement Tennessee law.
- **Legal teams**: Note the tightening of fair use and incidental use exemptions relative to the 1984 Act. Update clearance and content review checklists accordingly — especially for satire, documentary, and news content that may incorporate AI-generated audio elements.
- **All affected entities**: Monitor first Tennessee court decisions interpreting the "primary purpose or function" standard, the "readily identifiable and attributable" voice definition, and the scope of the First Amendment carve-out. Early judicial decisions will substantially clarify the Act's practical reach.
