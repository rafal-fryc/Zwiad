# Colorado AI Act and Advertising Industry Self-Regulation: What SB 24-205 Means for Consumer-Facing AI

**Jurisdiction:** Colorado | **Category:** AI Law | **Date:** May 30, 2024

## Executive Summary [MEDIUM confidence]

Colorado Governor Jared Polis signed [Senate Bill 24-205](https://leg.colorado.gov/bills/sb24-205) — the Colorado Artificial Intelligence Act (CAIA) — on May 17, 2024, making Colorado the first U.S. state to enact comprehensive AI legislation. While most legal commentary has focused on its employment and financial-services implications, BBB National Programs — the independent nonprofit that administers the National Advertising Division (NAD), the Children's Advertising Review Unit (CARU), and the Digital Advertising Accountability Program (DAAP) — identified a distinct angle: how does SB 24-205 reshape the landscape for businesses using AI in advertising, marketing, and consumer-facing applications, and what role can advertising industry self-regulation play in satisfying the law's requirements?

The Act's core requirement is that developers and deployers of "high-risk" AI systems — those making "consequential decisions" affecting Colorado consumers in enumerated sectors — exercise reasonable care to prevent algorithmic discrimination. Most advertising AI (product recommendations, dynamic pricing, ad targeting) does not fall squarely within SB 24-205's consequential-decision categories. However, AI used in financial advertising, insurance marketing, and hiring-adjacent contexts may trigger full compliance obligations. Beyond that, the law's consumer-disclosure requirements for AI interaction apply broadly to all AI-powered consumer interfaces, not just high-risk systems — a provision directly relevant to chatbots, AI-powered customer service, and interactive marketing tools. BBB National Programs argues that industry self-regulatory programs provide the independent accountability infrastructure that state AI laws assume but do not create, and that companies with strong self-regulatory compliance records are better positioned to demonstrate the "reasonable care" required by SB 24-205.

## Background [HIGH confidence]

### The Federal Void and State Action

By spring 2024, no federal statute had established binding rules for commercial AI systems. The FTC had issued guidance on AI and dark patterns, and the NIST AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF 1.0) was published in January 2023, but neither imposed private-sector legal duties. The Biden administration's October 2023 AI Executive Order directed agencies to develop standards without creating enforceable private-sector obligations.

Into this regulatory vacuum, Colorado enacted SB 24-205 — the first comprehensive state AI consumer protection law in the United States. The bill was introduced by Senator Robert Rodriguez on April 10, 2024, passed both chambers, and was signed May 17, 2024. Governor Polis accompanied his signature with a formal [reluctance letter](https://www.dwt.com/-/media/files/blogs/artificial-intelligence-law-advisor/2024/05/sb24205-signing-statement.pdf?rev=a902184eafe046cfb615bb047484e11c&hash=213F4C6CDFF52A876011290C24406E7F), stating that the law's "disparate impact" standard — regulating the outcomes of AI use regardless of intent — deviated from traditional discrimination law, and urging the legislature to revisit this approach before the law took effect in 2026.

### BBB National Programs as an Industry Self-Regulatory Actor

[BBB National Programs](https://bbbprograms.org/) is an independent nonprofit that administers more than a dozen industry self-regulation programs including:

- **National Advertising Division (NAD):** Reviews national advertising claims for truth and accuracy; companies that decline to participate or comply with decisions are referred to the FTC for enforcement.
- **Children's Advertising Review Unit (CARU):** Sets and enforces standards for child-directed advertising and data collection; in May 2024, issued a [compliance warning](https://assets.bbbprograms.org/docs/default-source/caru/2024_caru_ai-compliance-warning_final.pdf) on AI use in child-directed media.
- **Digital Advertising Accountability Program (DAAP):** Enforces the Digital Advertising Alliance's self-regulatory principles for online behavioral advertising.
- **Center for Industry Self-Regulation (CISR):** Convenes industry stakeholders on emerging issues, including AI governance.

BBB National Programs functions as what regulators call a "credible third party with a track record of providing independent accountability" — exactly the kind of organization SB 24-205's framework implicitly relies upon when it allows deployers to satisfy the law's requirements by following recognized frameworks like the NIST AI RMF. BBB National Programs has published detailed analysis of how self-regulatory programs intersect with state AI law compliance, positioning itself as a complementary accountability mechanism.

### Polis's Signing Statement: Concerns Foreshadowing Legislative Turbulence

Governor Polis's [signing statement](https://www.dwt.com/-/media/files/blogs/artificial-intelligence-law-advisor/2024/05/sb24205-signing-statement.pdf?rev=a902184eafe046cfb615bb047484e11c&hash=213F4C6CDFF52A876011290C24406E7F) signaled that the law as written was not his preferred outcome. Key concerns relevant to advertising and consumer-facing AI businesses:

1. **Disparate impact standard:** Polis wrote that laws prohibiting discrimination "generally focus on prohibiting intentional discriminatory conduct" and that SB 24-205's outcome-based approach — regardless of intent — was a significant departure he urged the legislature to reconsider.
2. **Innovation stifling:** He expressed concern about "the impact this law may have on an industry that is fueling critical technological advancements across our state."
3. **Preference for federal preemption:** Polis argued that "the important work of protecting consumers from discrimination and other unintended consequences of nascent AI technologies is better considered and applied by the federal government to limit and preempt varied compliance burdens on innovators."

These concerns proved prescient: the 2025 regular session produced no substantive amendments, and an August 2025 special session collapsed without meaningful changes. On August 28, 2025, Polis signed [SB 25B-004](https://leg.colorado.gov/bills/sb25b-004), which postponed the effective date from February 1, 2026, to **June 30, 2026**, while leaving all substantive obligations unchanged. As of early 2026, further federal preemption pressure has emerged from President Trump's December 2025 executive order targeting state AI laws.

## Detailed Analysis [MEDIUM confidence]

### What SB 24-205 Actually Covers for Advertising Businesses

The law's core applicability framework centers on "high-risk AI systems" making "consequential decisions." A consequential decision is one with material legal or similarly significant effect on access to, the cost of, or the terms of:

- Employment or employment opportunities
- Education enrollment
- Financial or lending services
- Essential government services
- Health care services
- Housing
- Insurance
- Legal services

Most advertising technology — programmatic ad placement, A/B testing, dynamic creative optimization, product recommendation engines — does not make "consequential decisions" in these enumerated categories and therefore falls outside the high-risk framework's core obligations. A standard ad-targeting algorithm is not covered simply because it personalizes which product a consumer sees.

However, several advertising-adjacent AI applications may cross into covered territory:

- **Insurance advertising with underwriting-linked AI:** If an insurer uses AI to determine which marketing offers to extend to which consumers, and that AI feeds into underwriting or premium decisions, the deployer faces potential high-risk classification — though the law provides an express exemption for insurers operating under Colorado's insurance algorithmic-regulation statutes at [Colo. Rev. Stat. § 10-3-1104.9](https://leg.colorado.gov/bills/sb24-205).
- **Financial services marketing AI:** AI that makes decisions about which credit or lending products to market to which consumers — where those decisions reflect creditworthiness proxies — could qualify as consequential in the financial/lending category.
- **Hiring-adjacent recruitment advertising:** AI tools that determine which job postings to serve to which consumers, if integrated with hiring platforms making downstream employment decisions, may be swept into the developer-deployer chain.

### The Consumer Interaction Disclosure Requirement: Broad Coverage for All Businesses

Critically for advertising businesses, SB 24-205 contains a consumer-disclosure requirement that applies **beyond** high-risk systems. Section 6-1-1704(1) requires any person doing business in Colorado who deploys an AI system **intended to interact with consumers** to disclose to each consumer that they are interacting with an AI system — unless a reasonable person would not believe they were interacting with a human.

This provision covers:
- AI-powered customer service chatbots
- Interactive marketing tools (AI shopping assistants, product recommendation chatbots)
- AI-generated influencers or virtual brand representatives
- Personalized email or messaging AI that appears to be from a human representative

For consumer brands relying on conversational AI for marketing, this is a direct compliance obligation even without any "consequential decision" element. The disclosure must be made before or at the outset of the interaction.

### NAD's Parallel Approach: Advertising Truthfulness as AI Compliance Foundation

The [National Advertising Division](https://bbbprograms.org/programs/all-programs/national-advertising-division) has developed an independent enforcement track for AI in advertising claims that parallels SB 24-205's consumer protection goals. In 2024, NAD reviewed advertising involving AI capabilities and required modifications in cases where advertising misrepresented AI product performance — for example, requiring Google to unlist a video demonstrating Gemini capabilities that did not accurately reflect the system's actual speed and ease of use.

[NAD's 2024 Annual Conference](https://www.dglaw.com/insights-from-day-2-of-the-2024-nad-conference-ftc-perspectives-data-based-claims-dark-patterns-and-artificial-intelligence/) reflected alignment between NAD and the FTC: AI-generated advertising claims require substantiation just as traditional claims do; AI-generated reviews face heightened scrutiny; and disclosure requirements apply equally to AI-generated and human-created content. The FTC's Operation AI Comply enforcement wave in late 2024 targeted companies using AI to generate fake reviews and misleading performance claims.

BBB National Programs has argued publicly that companies with robust NAD compliance records — particularly those that participated in self-regulatory review of AI-related claims before SB 24-205's effective date — are better positioned to demonstrate "reasonable care" to Colorado regulators because they have documented independent third-party review of their consumer-facing AI practices.

### CARU's May 2024 AI Compliance Warning: Child-Directed Advertising Specifics

CARU's May 2024 [compliance warning](https://assets.bbbprograms.org/docs/default-source/caru/2024_caru_ai-compliance-warning_final.pdf) — issued concurrently with SB 24-205's passage — specifically addressed risks of AI in child-directed media that overlap with SB 24-205's consumer protection goals:

- **Deepfakes and simulated content:** CARU requires that AI-generated content in child-directed advertising be clearly identifiable as advertising, and warns that AI deepfakes and simulated realistic figures (including celebrities) require special disclosure.
- **Voice cloning:** AI-powered voice cloning within ads directed at children raises deception concerns under CARU guidelines.
- **Data collection for machine learning:** Using children's personal data collected via interactive AI for machine learning without COPPA-compliant parental consent violates COPPA and CARU guidelines.

For consumer brands advertising to children using AI-powered interactive tools, CARU enforcement is the immediate compliance risk — predating and potentially more stringent than SB 24-205 requirements in this segment.

### Self-Regulatory Safe Harbors: The BBB National Programs Argument

BBB National Programs has advanced the policy argument that industry self-regulatory programs should be recognized as satisfying or contributing to state AI law compliance obligations. The logic follows from SB 24-205's own structure: deployers who follow NIST AI RMF or ISO 42001 receive a **rebuttable presumption** of reasonable care. BBB National Programs positions its programs as analogous independent accountability mechanisms — particularly for the consumer-interaction and advertising-specific dimensions of AI compliance that NIST RMF does not directly address.

Specifically, BBB National Programs has recommended that:

1. **State regulators include self-regulatory programs as credible third-party validators** in AI compliance frameworks, allowing deployers to point to NAD review or DAAP compliance as evidence of reasonable care.
2. **AI regulations reference industry-specific self-regulatory standards** (e.g., DAA principles for behavioral advertising, CARU guidelines for child-directed AI) as a floor for consumer protection in advertising contexts.
3. **Companies adopt independent algorithmic audits** of consumer-facing AI systems as part of their risk management programs — a practice that NAD's monitoring function partially fulfills for advertising claim accuracy.

The FTC has an established relationship with BBB National Programs under which [NAD referrals to the FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/legal-library/browse/cases-proceedings/resolution-referrals-bbb-national-programs) for non-compliant advertisers trigger FTC enforcement review. This referral mechanism demonstrates the kind of regulatory-private cooperation SB 24-205's framework envisions.

## Impact Assessment [MEDIUM confidence]

### Who in the Advertising Sector Is Directly Affected

**Consumer brands with AI chatbots or virtual assistants:** Any Colorado-connected brand using AI systems intended to interact with consumers faces the disclosure requirement under SB 24-205 Section 6-1-1704(1). This is not limited to high-risk systems and covers the broad universe of AI-powered marketing technology.

**Advertising technology companies:** Ad tech vendors whose systems process consumer data to make audience segmentation or targeting decisions face developer-level documentation obligations if any downstream deployer uses that data in a consequential decision. The developer-deployer chain in modern programmatic advertising is complex enough that certain ad tech companies may bear developer obligations they have not anticipated.

**Insurers and financial advertisers:** These sectors have specific exemptions from SB 24-205's high-risk requirements if they comply with their respective sectoral AI governance regimes. However, those exemptions require affirmative compliance with the sectoral standards, not merely the absence of SB 24-205 violations.

**Child-directed digital marketing:** For any advertiser using generative AI in child-directed contexts, the combination of SB 24-205's consumer-interaction disclosure requirement, CARU's May 2024 compliance warning, and COPPA creates overlapping obligations that may require immediate remediation independent of the June 30, 2026 compliance deadline.

### Compliance Considerations for Advertising Organizations

The consumer-interaction disclosure requirement is effective **June 30, 2026** (following the SB 25B-004 delay). Companies operating AI-powered consumer interfaces in Colorado should audit these systems for disclosure compliance before that date.

For high-risk AI systems used in advertising-adjacent sectors (financial services marketing, insurance ad targeting with underwriting linkage), the deployer obligations — impact assessments, risk management policies aligned with NIST AI RMF or equivalent, and consumer notice mechanisms — require significant compliance infrastructure that cannot be assembled quickly.

Governor Polis's stated preference for federal preemption and President Trump's December 2025 executive order targeting state AI laws have introduced regulatory uncertainty. As of April 2026, Colorado SB 24-205's June 30, 2026 effective date remains in place, and no federal preemption has occurred. Businesses should plan for compliance while monitoring federal developments.

### The Self-Regulation Opportunity

BBB National Programs frames Colorado's law not only as a compliance burden but as an opportunity for industry to demonstrate that self-regulatory accountability mechanisms can satisfy statutory requirements. For advertising businesses, this suggests a dual strategy: develop internal AI governance aligned with NIST AI RMF for any high-risk systems, while engaging with NAD and CARU self-regulatory processes for consumer-facing AI claims as evidence of independent third-party accountability. This approach is consistent with the law's safe harbor structure and positions companies favorably in any enforcement inquiry.

## Action Items

- Audit all AI systems deployed in Colorado-connected consumer interactions to determine whether they trigger the consumer-interaction disclosure requirement (applies broadly, not just to high-risk systems; effective June 30, 2026).
- Classify AI systems used in advertising-adjacent sectors (financial services, insurance, employment) against the "consequential decision" definition to determine whether high-risk deployer obligations apply.
- Review ad technology vendor contracts to understand which vendors bear developer obligations under SB 24-205 and whether their documentation packages for deployers are prepared.
- Engage with NAD self-regulatory review processes for AI-related advertising claims as a proactive step toward demonstrating reasonable care to Colorado regulators.
- For child-directed advertising using generative AI, treat CARU's May 2024 compliance warning as immediately operative — these obligations predate and supplement SB 24-205's June 2026 effective date.
- Monitor federal preemption developments: President Trump's December 2025 executive order targets state AI laws; the regulatory landscape may change before June 30, 2026.
- Track Colorado Attorney General rulemaking at [coag.gov/ai/](https://coag.gov/ai/) for guidance on advertising-sector compliance.
