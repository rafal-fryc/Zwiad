# Colorado Creates Artificial Intelligence Impact Task Force to Revise SB 24-205

**Jurisdiction:** Colorado | **Category:** AI Law | **Date:** August 16, 2024

## Executive Summary [HIGH confidence]

On June 6, 2024, Colorado Governor Jared Polis signed [House Bill 24-1468](https://leg.colorado.gov/bills/hb24-1468) into law, creating the Colorado Artificial Intelligence Impact Task Force — a 26-member body of policymakers, industry representatives, academics, and civil society experts tasked with studying and recommending refinements to the state's landmark [SB 24-205](https://leg.colorado.gov/bills/sb24-205), the first comprehensive state AI law in the United States. The task force was created in direct response to Governor Polis's reservations about SB 24-205, which he signed with a letter urging the legislature to "significantly improve" the law before it took effect. The task force was required to submit a report of recommendations to the Joint Technology Committee and the Governor's Office by February 1, 2025. That report was completed on schedule and identified numerous contested areas — including the definition of "consequential decisions," algorithmic discrimination standards, business exemption thresholds, and trade secret protections — that have since driven multiple rounds of legislative revision attempts, a special session, and an enforcement delay to June 30, 2026.

## Background [HIGH confidence]

### Colorado SB 24-205: The Nation's First Comprehensive State AI Law

Colorado's [SB 24-205](https://leg.colorado.gov/bills/sb24-205), formally titled the "Consumer Protections for Artificial Intelligence" Act, was signed by Governor Polis on May 17, 2024, after passing the State Senate on May 3 and the House on May 8. The law is modeled in part on the EU AI Act and represents the first broad, private-sector AI regulation enacted by any U.S. state.

The law targets "high-risk artificial intelligence systems" — defined as AI systems that make or are a substantial factor in making "consequential decisions" affecting consumers in employment, education, housing, financial services, healthcare, and legal services. Entities falling under the law are classified as either "developers" (those who create or modify the AI system) or "deployers" (those who deploy the system to make decisions about consumers). Both categories face distinct compliance obligations.

Governor Polis signed the bill with a notable letter of reservation, publicly calling on the legislature to "significantly improve" the law before its originally scheduled effective date of February 1, 2026. This signing-with-reservations posture was unusual and telegraphed that the legislature would be expected to revisit the law promptly — which is precisely what HB 24-1468 institutionalized.

### Prior AI Oversight Structure: Facial Recognition Task Force

Before HB 24-1468, Colorado had already established a task force focused on facial recognition technology. HB 24-1468 directly amended the existing facial recognition task force structure, renaming and broadening it to become the AI Impact Task Force. The bill's expansion of membership from the prior facial recognition body to 26 members reflects the broader scope of artificial intelligence and biometric technology now covered.

## Detailed Analysis [HIGH confidence]

### HB 24-1468: Task Force Structure and Mandate

[House Bill 24-1468](https://leg.colorado.gov/bills/hb24-1468), signed June 6, 2024, formally creates the Colorado Artificial Intelligence Impact Task Force by amending Colorado Revised Statutes Section 2-3-1707 (later codified as [C.R.S. § 2-3-1707](https://law.justia.com/codes/colorado/title-2/legislative-services/article-3/part-17/section-2-3-1707/)). The bill retransforms the prior "biometric technology and artificial intelligence policy task force" (itself a renamed facial recognition task force) into a broader body with explicit authority to review SB 24-205.

**Membership (26 members):** Appointed no later than August 1, 2024, by five appointing authorities — the Governor, the President of the Senate, the Senate Minority Leader, the Speaker of the House of Representatives, and the House Minority Leader. Members represent:
- Organizations, communities, and governmental entities affected by AI systems
- Academic institutions studying AI and technology policy
- Businesses in the AI and biometric technology industry
- Civil rights and consumer protection advocates
- Experts in generative AI, biometrics, and social media technology
- Advocates for youth safety and privacy

**Issues of Study:** The task force is charged with examining a broad range of AI policy questions, including:
- Definitions of key terms (including "consequential decisions" and "algorithmic discrimination")
- Notice and disclosure requirements for AI systems
- Codes of conduct and best practices for AI development and deployment
- Recommendations for protecting disproportionately impacted communities from algorithmic discrimination
- Governance frameworks for automated decision systems and biometric technology
- How Colorado state government can effectively regulate AI and automated decision systems

**Meeting Requirements:** The task force must convene at least five times and shall allow for virtual participation at any in-person meetings.

**Reporting Deadline:** On or before February 1, 2025, the task force must submit a report to the Joint Technology Committee and the Governor's Office summarizing its findings and policy recommendations.

**Sunset:** The task force is scheduled for repeal on September 1, 2027, subject to sunset review prior to that date.

### The February 2025 Task Force Report and Its Findings

The task force met its statutory deadline, submitting its [Report and Recommendations](https://content.leg.colorado.gov/sites/default/files/images/report_and_recommendations_0.pdf) to the Joint Technology Committee and Governor's Office on or before February 1, 2025. The report identified areas of both consensus and deep disagreement:

**Areas of Minor Consensus:** Most stakeholders agreed that technical clarifications were needed to notification and documentation requirements under SB 24-205.

**Highly Contested Areas:**

1. **Definition of "Consequential Decisions"**: The scope of this definition — which determines which AI-driven processes trigger the law's compliance requirements — was identified as a central area of disagreement. Industry argued for narrower definitions to reduce compliance burden; consumer advocates argued for breadth to match the law's anti-discrimination objectives.

2. **Algorithmic Discrimination Standards**: Critics found the existing definition potentially vague, making it difficult for businesses to assess whether their AI systems comply. The precise standard — disparate treatment vs. disparate impact, and across which protected classes — remained contested.

3. **Business Exemption Thresholds**: The law already exempts small businesses (under 50 employees) from certain requirements. Industry representatives argued exemption thresholds should be raised or expanded to mid-sized businesses; civil rights groups opposed broadening exemptions.

4. **Right to Cure Before Enforcement**: This became one of the most divisive questions. Industry groups argued that companies should receive notice and an opportunity to cure violations before enforcement action by the Attorney General. Consumer advocates maintained that a right-to-cure provision could weaken deterrence and delay accountability.

5. **Trade Secret Protections**: Businesses expressed concern that mandatory AI disclosures required under SB 24-205 could force revelation of proprietary training data, model architecture, or governance procedures. Transparency advocates countered that such disclosures are essential to detecting and proving algorithmic discrimination.

6. **Impact Assessment Timing and Scope**: The timing of required impact assessments (initial, annual, and post-modification) and the documentation required to satisfy them generated significant debate over compliance feasibility.

### Key Provisions of SB 24-205 the Task Force Was Convened to Review

For context, the substantive obligations the task force was charged with evaluating include:

**Developer Obligations (effective as of the law's effective date):**
- Use reasonable care to protect consumers from known or reasonably foreseeable risks of algorithmic discrimination
- Furnish deployers with documentation describing the system's intended uses, training data governance measures, evaluations for algorithmic discrimination, foreseeable limitations, and technical capabilities
- Notify the Attorney General and all known deployers within 90 days of discovering an unreasonable risk of algorithmic discrimination

**Deployer Obligations:**
- Establish and maintain a risk management policy and program, benchmarked to the [NIST AI Risk Management Framework](https://www.nist.gov/system/files/documents/2023/01/26/AI%20RMF%201.0.pdf) or an equivalent recognized framework
- Conduct initial impact assessments within 90 days of the law's effective date, then annually and after any substantial modification
- Notify consumers when a high-risk AI system makes or substantially contributes to an adverse consequential decision about them
- Allow consumers to correct personal data that the AI system used in making a consequential decision
- Provide consumers the opportunity to appeal adverse decisions via human review, where technically feasible

**Enforcement:** The [Colorado Attorney General](https://leg.colorado.gov/bills/sb24-205) has exclusive enforcement authority via the Colorado Consumer Protection Act. Penalties reach $20,000 per violation. The original law provided a 60-day cure window before enforcement.

**Safe Harbor:** Compliance with nationally or internationally recognized AI risk management frameworks (e.g., NIST AI RMF, ISO/IEC 42001) creates a rebuttable presumption of reasonable care.

## Impact Assessment [MEDIUM confidence]

### Immediate Compliance Uncertainty for Affected Businesses

The task force's creation and the ensuing report created a prolonged period of legal uncertainty that has substantially complicated compliance planning for AI developers and deployers operating in Colorado. As of the finding date (August 2024), businesses faced the prospect of a law with an original February 1, 2026, effective date but with explicit legislative intent to substantially revise it — creating a dilemma over whether to build compliance programs toward the law as written or wait for amendments.

The [Fisher Phillips analysis of the task force report](https://www.fisherphillips.com/en/insights/insights/colorados-ai-task-force-warns-of-compliance-challenges-ahead-of-groundbreaking-2026-ai-law) emphasized that the contested areas — particularly the definition of consequential decisions and the right to cure — were not resolved by the task force and would require legislative action. This left compliance officers without clear guidance on how to scope their obligations.

### Industries Most Affected

The law's reach is broad but particularly acute for:
- **Employers** using AI tools in hiring, performance evaluation, or termination decisions
- **Healthcare entities** deploying AI for clinical decision support (with limited HIPAA exemptions)
- **Financial services firms** using AI in lending, credit, or insurance underwriting (with limited prudential regulator exemptions)
- **Housing and tenant screening** platforms relying on automated risk models
- **EdTech** companies using AI to assess students or allocate educational resources

The task force's membership included representatives from each of these sectors, and the contested areas in the report directly track the divergent concerns of industry and consumer advocates across these domains.

### Legislative Trajectory Following the Task Force Report

The task force's February 2025 report informed — but did not resolve — subsequent legislative efforts. The regular 2025 legislative session produced no enacted amendments to SB 24-205. Governor Polis then called a special session in August 2025, which resulted only in [SB 25B-004](https://leg.colorado.gov/bills/sb25b-004) — a delay of the effective date from February 1, 2026, to June 30, 2026. No substantive provisions were altered.

In October 2025, Governor Polis convened a new [AI Policy Working Group](https://www.coloradopolitics.com/2025/10/15/gov-polis-convenes-new-working-group-to-address-colorados-lingering-ai-law-challenges/) — a successor body to the original task force — to negotiate a replacement framework. That working group achieved unanimous agreement on a new proposed framework in [March 2026](https://governorsoffice.colorado.gov/governor/news/colorado-artificial-intelligence-policy-workgroup-delivers-unanimous-support-revised-policy), which the legislature was expected to take up during the 2026 session before the June 30, 2026, deadline.

### Federal Preemption Overlay

The task force's work has also intersected with a significant federal development: President Trump's [December 11, 2025 executive order](https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2025/12/eliminating-state-law-obstruction-of-national-artificial-intelligence-policy/) directing the federal government to challenge "onerous" state AI laws for preemption. Colorado SB 24-205 is among the state laws most prominently referenced in that preemption context, adding further urgency to the legislature's 2026 revision efforts.

## Action Items

- **Monitor the 2026 Colorado legislative session closely:** The AI Policy Working Group's March 2026 framework proposal is expected to result in legislation before the June 30, 2026, effective date. Affected businesses should track introduced bills against the framework's provisions, particularly changes to exemption thresholds, impact assessment requirements, and the right-to-cure mechanism.
- **Begin SB 24-205 gap analysis now:** Even if the law is substantially amended, the core structure — developer/deployer duties, impact assessments, consumer notice, and anti-discrimination obligations — is likely to survive in modified form. Organizations should map their AI systems against the current law to identify high-risk systems and begin documentation programs.
- **Evaluate safe harbor eligibility:** Organizations that have not yet adopted the NIST AI Risk Management Framework or ISO/IEC 42001 should assess whether alignment with those standards is feasible before the June 30, 2026, deadline, given the rebuttable presumption of reasonable care attached to framework compliance.
- **Track federal preemption developments:** The Trump administration's executive order creates potential preemption risk for SB 24-205. If federal legislation or agency action displaces Colorado's law, compliance investments may need to be redirected. Monitor Commerce Department and DOJ AI Litigation Task Force activity.
- **Participate in legislative process:** The ongoing revision process remains active and contested. Organizations with compliance concerns should engage with the legislature, the AI Policy Working Group, and the Attorney General's Office during the 2026 session.
