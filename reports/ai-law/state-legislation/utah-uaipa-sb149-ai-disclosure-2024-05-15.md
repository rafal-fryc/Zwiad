# Utah Enacts First AI-Focused Consumer Protection Law: The Utah Artificial Intelligence Policy Act (SB 149)

**Jurisdiction:** Utah | **Category:** AI Law | **Date:** May 15, 2024

## Executive Summary [HIGH confidence]

On March 13, 2024, Utah Governor Spencer Cox signed [Senate Bill 149 (SB 149)](https://le.utah.gov/~2024/bills/static/SB0149.html), the Utah Artificial Intelligence Policy Act ("UAIPA"), making Utah the first state in the United States to enact legislation specifically targeting generative AI in the private sector. The law took effect May 1, 2024. The UAIPA imposes two categories of disclosure obligations on entities using generative AI to interact with consumers: mandatory proactive disclosures for regulated occupations, and on-request disclosures for all covered entities engaging in consumer protection-regulated activities. The law also forecloses the "AI-made-me-do-it" defense in consumer protection enforcement, expressly prohibiting businesses from attributing violations to generative AI. Enforcement rests with the Utah Division of Consumer Protection (UDCP) with fines up to $2,500 per violation; no private right of action is created. The law further establishes the Office of Artificial Intelligence Policy and an AI Learning Laboratory regulatory sandbox designed to encourage responsible AI innovation in Utah. In 2025, the Utah legislature significantly amended the UAIPA to narrow its disclosure requirements and extend its sunset date.

## Background [HIGH confidence]

Utah enacted the UAIPA during a period of rapidly expanding state legislative activity on AI, predating most other state AI disclosure frameworks. As of its enactment, no other US state had adopted a law specifically regulating the private-sector use of generative AI. Several states had enacted or were considering broader comprehensive AI governance measures, but Utah's approach was narrower and more targeted — focusing on the consumer-protection interface between AI systems and end users.

The bill, SB 149, was introduced in the 2024 Utah legislative session as "Artificial Intelligence Amendments." It amended Utah's existing consumer protection statutes — primarily the Utah Consumer Sales Practices Act — rather than creating a standalone AI code. The legislative approach reflected a design choice to graft AI-specific obligations onto proven enforcement infrastructure rather than creating new regulatory machinery from scratch.

Prior to UAIPA, Utah had enacted the Utah Consumer Privacy Act (effective December 31, 2023) as part of the wave of state comprehensive privacy legislation. The UAIPA built on that foundation without modifying the privacy act itself. Utah's regulatory framework for professionals — administered by the Division of Professional Licensing under the Department of Commerce — provided the existing "regulated occupation" infrastructure that the UAIPA leveraged.

The broader national context at enactment included active federal consideration of AI governance frameworks and the EU AI Act negotiations nearing completion. State legislators in Utah positioned UAIPA as both a consumer protection measure and a signal to the market and other states about one possible template for AI-specific regulation.

## Detailed Analysis [HIGH confidence]

### Scope: What Is "Generative AI" Under the UAIPA

The UAIPA defines "generative AI" as an artificial system that: (a) is trained on data; (b) interacts with a person using text, audio, or visual communication; and (c) generates non-scripted outputs similar to outputs created by a human, with limited or no human oversight. This definition intentionally excludes scripted chatbots and traditional decision-support tools that do not generate novel, human-like outputs. It focuses squarely on large-language-model-style systems and other foundation-model-based products deployed in consumer-facing contexts.

### Tier 1: Regulated Occupation Disclosure (Proactive)

Entities in "regulated occupations" face the more stringent, proactive disclosure tier. A regulated occupation is one regulated by the Utah Department of Commerce that requires a license or state certification to practice — examples include accountants, architects, engineers, nurses, physicians, pharmacists, social workers, and other licensed healthcare professionals. When a licensee uses generative AI to provide regulated services to a consumer, they must "prominently" disclose that the consumer is interacting with generative AI:

- **Oral or audible exchanges:** Verbal disclosure at the start of the exchange or conversation.
- **Electronic messaging:** Disclosure before the written exchange begins.

This proactive requirement places regulated professionals on notice that silence or ambiguity is not permissible — the consumer must know before the substantive interaction begins that they are engaging with AI rather than a human professional.

### Tier 2: Consumer Protection Activity Disclosure (On-Request)

For entities engaging in any activity regulated by the Utah Division of Consumer Protection — covering consumer sales, telemarketing, charitable solicitations, and numerous other consumer-facing commercial activities — the UAIPA imposes a narrower, on-request disclosure obligation. Under this tier, a person who "uses, prompts, or otherwise causes generative AI to interact with a person" must "clearly and conspicuously disclose" that the person is interacting with generative AI and not a human "if asked or prompted by the person." The statute requires the AI system itself to be capable of making this disclosure — meaning the AI must be configured to expressly state it is AI when a consumer asks.

### No "AI Defense" in Consumer Protection Actions

A legally significant provision of the UAIPA forecloses a potential defense in consumer protection enforcement. The act expressly provides that it is not a defense to a consumer protection violation that generative AI "made the violative statement; undertook the violative act; or was used in furtherance of the violation." This language directly addresses anticipatory arguments by businesses that the AI, as an autonomous system, was responsible for unlawful conduct rather than the human or entity that deployed it. Under the UAIPA, accountability runs to the deployer.

### Office of Artificial Intelligence Policy and the AI Learning Laboratory

Alongside the disclosure obligations, the UAIPA creates the [Office of Artificial Intelligence Policy](https://le.utah.gov/~2024/bills/static/SB0149.html) within Utah's government. The Office is tasked with:

- Administering an **AI Learning Laboratory Program** — a regulatory sandbox allowing companies to apply for up to two years of "regulatory mitigation" while developing AI systems.
- Consulting with businesses and stakeholders about proposed AI regulations.
- Drafting rules for participation in the Learning Lab.
- Defining criteria for organizations seeking mitigation of regulatory requirements.

Regulatory mitigations can include reduced fines for violations and cure periods before fines are imposed, giving innovative AI developers room to operate under an observed but protected status. This innovation-friendly component reflects Utah's dual goals: consumer protection paired with a desire to attract and support AI development within the state.

### Enforcement and Penalties

Enforcement authority rests with the [Utah Division of Consumer Protection (UDCP)](https://consumerprotection.utah.gov/). The UDCP may impose administrative fines of up to **$2,500 per violation** of the UAIPA. Each deceptive act or practice constitutes a separate violation, so fines can accumulate rapidly across multiple consumer interactions. Courts may, in UDCP-initiated proceedings, also:

- Enjoin violating activity.
- Order disgorgement of funds obtained in violation of the UAIPA.

The Utah Attorney General may independently seek civil penalties of **$5,000 per violation** against any person who breaches an existing administrative or judicial order. **No private right of action exists** under the UAIPA — enforcement is solely governmental.

### 2025 Amendments: Significant Narrowing

The original UAIPA, as enacted in May 2024, was materially amended in March 2025 when the Utah legislature enacted [SB 226](https://le.utah.gov/~2025/bills/static/SB0226.html) and SB 332. These amendments took effect May 7, 2025, and made the following significant changes:

1. **High-Risk Threshold for Proactive Disclosure:** The original law required regulated businesses to prominently disclose AI use at the outset of any consumer interaction. As amended, proactive disclosure at the outset is only required when the generative AI interaction constitutes a "high-risk" AI interaction — defined as one involving (i) collection of sensitive personal information (financial, health, or biometric data) and (ii) provision of personalized recommendations or advice "that could reasonably be relied upon to make significant personal decisions" (such as financial, legal, medical, or mental health advice).

2. **Clarified "Clear and Unambiguous Request" Standard:** The on-request disclosure trigger was clarified to require "a clear and unambiguous request" to determine whether the interaction involves a human or AI — a narrowing from the original broader "asked or prompted" language.

3. **Sunset Extension:** SB 332 extended the UAIPA's sunset date, which was set to expire May 7, 2025, to July 1, 2027, preserving the framework while the legislature continues to refine it.

These amendments reflect a pattern visible in other states' AI legislation where initial broad mandates are narrowed after industry engagement during the implementation period. [Davis Polk noted](https://www.davispolk.com/insights/client-update/utah-scales-back-reach-generative-ai-consumer-protection-law) that the changes "significantly scale back" the law's consumer-facing requirements and align Utah's approach more closely with a risk-tiered model.

## Impact Assessment [MEDIUM confidence]

### Affected Entities

The UAIPA as originally enacted applied to two broad groups:

1. **Licensed professionals** under the Utah Department of Commerce: healthcare providers, attorneys, accountants, architects, engineers, social workers, and others subject to professional licensing.
2. **Businesses engaged in UDCP-regulated activities**: consumer sales, telemarketing, charitable solicitations, and other commercial dealings with Utah consumers.

In practice, any company using a customer-facing AI chatbot, virtual assistant, or AI-driven customer service tool that communicates in text, audio, or visual form — and that generates non-scripted outputs — falls within scope when serving Utah consumers. This covers a broad swath of e-commerce, financial services, healthcare platforms, and professional services firms.

The 2025 amendments narrow the proactive disclosure obligation substantially. Post-amendment, businesses not dealing in "high-risk" AI interactions — those not involving sensitive personal data combined with significant personalized advice — face only the lighter on-request disclosure duty.

### Compliance Requirements

Under the original 2024 framework, covered entities needed to:

- Audit AI tools deployed in customer interactions to determine if they meet the UAIPA "generative AI" definition.
- Implement disclosure mechanisms: verbal disclosures for voice interactions, pre-conversation notices for text/chat interfaces.
- Ensure AI systems were configured to affirmatively identify themselves as AI when asked.
- Review consumer protection compliance programs to remove any arguments that shift liability to the AI itself.
- Consider applying for the AI Learning Laboratory sandbox if in active AI development.

Post-2025 amendments, the primary compliance focus for most businesses shifts to: (1) identifying whether any AI interactions qualify as "high-risk" under the amended definition, and (2) maintaining the on-request disclosure capability across all AI-consumer interfaces.

### Enforcement Outlook

As of the writing of this report, no significant public enforcement actions under the UAIPA have been identified. The UDCP's enforcement program for AI violations is still nascent. The lack of a private right of action limits litigation risk to governmental enforcement. Given the relatively low per-violation fine ceiling ($2,500 UDCP / $5,000 AG), the primary enforcement risk for large-scale consumer AI deployments is reputational and injunctive rather than financial — though disgorgement remains a meaningful remedy for companies that profited from non-disclosed AI interactions. The 2025 amendments, by narrowing scope, are likely to reduce the volume of technical violations while keeping enforcement focused on genuinely high-risk consumer AI interactions.

### National Significance

Utah's approach served as a reference point for other states considering AI disclosure laws. Its consumer protection integration model — amending existing consumer protection statutes rather than creating new AI-specific codes — has been studied by legislators in other states. The AI Learning Laboratory sandbox model has also been considered as a template for innovation-preserving AI governance.

## Action Items

- Audit all consumer-facing AI tools for "generative AI" coverage under the UAIPA definition; scripted chatbots likely fall outside scope but LLM-based systems do not.
- For regulated occupations (licensed healthcare, legal, accounting, engineering professions): implement proactive disclosure procedures for both oral and electronic AI-assisted consumer interactions involving "high-risk" criteria under the 2025 amendments.
- For all UDCP-regulated businesses: ensure AI systems can affirmatively identify themselves as AI in response to a clear consumer request.
- Remove any "AI-as-intervening-cause" reasoning from consumer protection defense playbooks for Utah-related matters.
- Monitor AI Learning Laboratory Program rule development through the Utah Office of Artificial Intelligence Policy for potential sandbox eligibility.
- Track UAIPA enforcement actions from the UDCP and Utah AG's office as enforcement program matures.
- Note the July 1, 2027 sunset date: anticipate potential additional legislative amendments or renewal proceedings before that date.
