# Missouri AG Promulgates Algorithmic Freedom Regulation for Social Media Platforms

**Jurisdiction:** Missouri | **Category:** AI Law / Algorithmic Accountability | **Date:** January 21, 2025

## Executive Summary [MEDIUM confidence]

In January 2025, Missouri Attorney General Andrew Bailey announced the promulgation of a novel regulation under the state's Merchandising Practices Act that would require large social media platforms to offer users a choice of third-party content moderation algorithms rather than exclusively relying on the platform's proprietary system. Framed as the first rule of its kind in the nation, the regulation was codified as [15 C.S.R. § 60-19](https://ago.mo.gov/wp-content/uploads/15-CSR-60-19.020-to-.040-Algorithmic-Choice-Proposed-Rulemaking-.pdf) and required platforms with 50 million or more U.S. monthly users to present a "choice screen" to users at account creation and every six months thereafter. The rule faced significant procedural, constitutional, and feasibility objections during the public comment period, and was quietly withdrawn by Bailey in September 2025 — shortly before he resigned to accept a federal appointment — with the AG's office citing concerns about implementation feasibility rather than lack of statutory authority.

## Background [HIGH confidence]

### The Broader Social Media Regulation Context

The Missouri regulation emerged from a national debate over whether large social media platforms' content moderation practices constitute deceptive or anticompetitive conduct. Beginning in 2021, Florida and Texas enacted laws directly restricting platforms' ability to remove or deprioritize user content. Those statutes were challenged on First Amendment grounds, and the U.S. Supreme Court addressed them in [*Moody v. NetChoice, LLC*, 603 U.S. ___ (July 1, 2024)](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/23pdf/22-277_d18f.pdf), vacating and remanding the lower court judgments 9-0. The majority opinion, authored by Justice Kagan, reaffirmed that platforms' editorial decisions — including how they compile and present third-party content — are protected expression under the First Amendment. The Court did not definitively resolve whether the statutes were unconstitutional, but the decision established a strong doctrinal framework that made direct content moderation mandates constitutionally suspect.

Rather than mandate a specific moderation outcome, AG Bailey's January 2025 regulation took a different structural approach: it sought to require platforms to offer an open market of competing content moderation services. The theory was that denying users access to competing algorithmic choices constituted an "unfair practice" under Missouri consumer protection law.

### The Missouri Merchandising Practices Act

The statutory authority underlying the regulation is [Mo. Rev. Stat. § 407.020](https://revisor.mo.gov/main/OneSection.aspx?section=407.020), the core prohibition of the Missouri Merchandising Practices Act (MMPA), which declares it unlawful for any person to use "any deception, fraud, false pretense, false promise, misrepresentation, unfair practice or the concealment, suppression, or omission of any material fact in connection with the sale or advertisement of any merchandise in trade or commerce." The AG also cited [§ 407.145 RSMo](https://revisor.mo.gov/main/OneSection.aspx?section=407.145) as additional rulemaking authority. The AG's office framed social media platforms' unilateral control over content algorithms — without disclosing that competing moderators exist or could be employed — as an unfair commercial practice against Missouri consumers.

### January 2025 Initial Announcement

AG Bailey first announced the regulation on January 17, 2025, in a [press release from the Missouri Attorney General's Office](https://ago.mo.gov/attorney-general-bailey-promulgates-regulation-securing-algorithmic-freedom-for-social-media-users/). The AG characterized the rule as dismantling what he called the "'Big Brother' speech-control machinery of corporate America" and emphasized that it would give Missouri residents the ability to select content moderation systems aligned with their preferences, including conservative-leaning or libertarian moderation philosophies. The rule's introductory language described it as the first regulation of its kind in the United States.

## Detailed Analysis [MEDIUM confidence]

### Scope and Coverage

The regulation targeted social media platforms meeting a dual size threshold: 50 million or more monthly U.S. active users and 1 billion or more global users. This framing was designed to capture the largest platforms (Facebook, Instagram, YouTube, TikTok, X/Twitter) while exempting smaller platforms from the compliance burden.

### Core Requirements of 15 C.S.R. § 60-19

The proposed rule, [filed as 15 C.S.R. § 60-19.020–.040](https://ago.mo.gov/wp-content/uploads/15-CSR-60-19.020-to-.040-Algorithmic-Choice-Proposed-Rulemaking-.pdf), imposed four principal obligations on covered platforms:

1. **Choice Screen Requirement.** Upon account activation and at least every six months thereafter, platforms must present users with a selection screen offering (a) the platform's own in-house content moderation algorithm or (b) one or more independent third-party content moderators. No default selection may be pre-chosen on the user's behalf.

2. **Non-Discrimination Requirement.** The choice screen must not favor the platform's own moderation service through design, placement, or other means that would inhibit user uptake of competing moderators.

3. **Interoperability and Data Access.** When a user selects a third-party moderator, the platform must provide that moderator with interoperable access to user data necessary to perform the moderation function. This is one of the most technically complex provisions: it effectively requires platforms to build open APIs supporting external algorithmic access to content streams, user signals, and behavioral data.

4. **Passthrough Obligation.** The platform must not moderate, censor, or suppress content that the user's chosen third-party moderator would otherwise permit the user to view.

The rule defined "content moderation" as "filtering, selecting, amplifying, curating, or suppressing content for a user to post or view," and a "content moderator" as a company or person performing that function. Platforms retained a carve-out allowing them to restrict third-party moderators' data access where strictly necessary to protect trade secrets, proprietary processes, user privacy, and information security.

### Legal Theory and AG's Rationale

According to law firm coverage by [Kelley Drye & Warren](https://www.kelleydrye.com/viewpoints/blogs/ad-law-access/missouri-attorney-general-introduces-new-rule-requiring-social-media-companies-to-offer-competitor-content-moderators), the AG's theory was that tech companies engaged in deceptive and unfair trade practices by implementing their own content moderation algorithm without offering competitor algorithms or allowing users to choose their own content moderators. Invoking both free speech interests and anti-competitive practice prohibitions under the MMPA, Bailey's office argued that the platforms' omission of competing algorithm options constituted a material omission under § 407.020.

A [WilmerHale client alert](https://www.wilmerhale.com/en/insights/client-alerts/20250117-missouri-attorney-general-promulgates-algorithmic-freedom-regulation) noted that the regulation required a notice-and-comment period of 30 days after publication in the Missouri Register before it could take effect, and observed that the rule was likely to face a serious First Amendment hurdle in light of *Moody v. NetChoice*.

### Constitutional Vulnerabilities

The regulation attracted substantial criticism from free speech advocates and the communications industry. The principal First Amendment objection derived directly from *Moody v. NetChoice*: Justice Kagan's majority opinion held that platforms have a First Amendment right to "compil[e] third-party speech it wants in the way it wants," and that compelled carriage of competitor moderation systems could constitute compelled editorial association. As [Techdirt noted in May 2025](https://www.techdirt.com/2025/05/15/missouri-ag-thinks-supreme-court-ruling-lets-him-control-social-media-moderation-it-doesnt/), AG Bailey's subsequent attempt to invoke *Moody* as endorsing his approach misconstrued the decision's reasoning — the ruling's skepticism of direct content mandates applied with equal force to structural mandates compelling platforms to integrate third-party systems. [Above the Law similarly analyzed](https://abovethelaw.com/2025/05/missouri-ag-thinks-supreme-court-ruling-lets-him-control-social-media-moderation-it-doesnt/) the argument as legally untenable.

### Procedural History: January Announcement to May Refiling

The January 2025 announcement was characterized as a "promulgation" of the regulation, but under Missouri's Administrative Procedures Act the rule still required formal notice-and-comment publication in the Missouri Register before taking effect. After the initial announcement, the AG's office held public forums to gather input. The rule was then formally filed — or refiled with revisions — in or around May 2025, at which point coverage from [Mondaq](https://www.mondaq.com/unitedstates/social-media/1626940/missouri-attorney-general-introduces-new-rule-requiring-social-media-companies-to-offer-competitor-content-moderators) and [Hunton Andrews Kurth](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-information-security-law/missouri-ag-files-rule-requiring-social-media-platforms-to-permit-alternative-content-moderators) documented the formal filing under the MMPA. A [Daily Caller fact sheet](https://cdn01.dailycaller.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/Fact-Sheet-AG-Bailey-Files-Nations-First-Rule-to-End-Big-Tech-Censorship.pdf) from the AG's office described the May filing as a "landmark rule to end Big Tech's censorship monopoly."

### Withdrawal in September 2025

Following the public comment period, industry groups and free speech organizations filed comments attacking both the feasibility and the legal authority for the rule. In September 2025, just before Bailey departed to become co-deputy director of the FBI under a federal appointment, his office issued a [withdrawal notice](https://missouriindependent.com/2025/09/10/former-missouri-ag-shelves-rule-on-social-media-moderation-as-he-leaves-office/) for the regulation without issuing a public press release. The withdrawal notice explicitly stated: "The attorney general's decision to withdraw this rulemaking is not due to comments suggesting he lacks legal authority to issue the rule." Instead, the office said it "agreed with the concerns from commenters about the feasibility of implementing the rule as drafted and that more work would be required." Bailey was replaced as AG by Catherine Hanaway. [Kelley Drye reported](https://www.kelleydrye.com/viewpoints/blogs/ad-law-access/former-missouri-attorney-general-quietly-withdraws-first-of-its-kind-content-moderation-rule) the withdrawal as the quiet end of the country's first algorithmic choice rulemaking.

## Impact Assessment [MEDIUM confidence]

### Affected Entities

The regulation, had it taken effect, would have primarily affected the largest global social media platforms with substantial Missouri user bases. Given Missouri's 6+ million internet users, platforms including Meta (Facebook, Instagram), Alphabet (YouTube), ByteDance (TikTok), and X Corp (X/Twitter) would have faced direct compliance obligations. The rule also implicitly affected prospective third-party content moderation service providers — a nascent industry that the rule sought to stimulate.

### Compliance Complexity

The interoperability and data access requirements represented the most technically demanding elements. Building open, secure APIs granting external moderators access to user content streams while simultaneously protecting trade secrets, user privacy, and platform security would have required substantial engineering investment. Commenters' feasibility concerns likely centered on this provision: no existing framework for third-party content moderation services exists at scale, meaning platforms would have been required to develop infrastructure for an ecosystem that did not yet exist.

### Current Status: Rule Withdrawn, No Binding Obligations

As of September 2025, 15 C.S.R. § 60-19 has been withdrawn and is not in effect. Missouri businesses and social media platforms face no current compliance obligations under this rule. However, the withdrawal expressly preserved the AG's asserted legal authority under the MMPA, meaning a future Missouri AG could revive similar rulemaking. Attorney General Hanaway has not announced any intention to pursue a successor rule.

### National Precedent Considerations

This episode is significant as the first state AG attempt to regulate algorithmic choice through consumer protection rulemaking rather than direct legislation. The legal theory — that denying access to competing moderation algorithms is an "unfair practice" — remains untested in court and could be revived by other state AGs. The interoperability requirements also prefigure potential federal or state legislation in the spirit of digital markets interoperability frameworks being developed in the EU and proposed at the federal level.

## Action Items

- Social media platforms with large Missouri user bases should continue monitoring for any revival of algorithmic choice rulemaking by AG Hanaway or through Missouri legislative channels.
- Compliance teams should track whether the feasibility concerns identified during the comment period — particularly around interoperability APIs and third-party moderator access — inform any revised future proposal.
- Legal counsel should maintain the *Moody v. NetChoice* First Amendment analysis for algorithmic mandate proposals; the constitutional framework will be applied to any successor rules in Missouri or similar actions in other states.
- Monitor other state AGs for potential adoption of consumer-protection-based algorithmic choice theories, particularly in states with broad MMPA-equivalent statutes.
- Track Missouri's new AI regulatory activity — the Missouri Independent has reported [Missouri AI regulations stalling in the 2026 legislative session](https://missouriindependent.com/2026/04/07/missouri-ai-regulations-stall-as-lawmakers-fear-loss-of-rural-broadband-funds/) due to concerns about rural broadband funding impacts.
