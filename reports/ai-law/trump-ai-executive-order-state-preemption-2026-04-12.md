# Trump Executive Order and National AI Policy Framework: Federal Push to Preempt State AI Laws

## Executive Summary [HIGH confidence]

On December 11, 2025, President Trump signed an executive order titled ["Ensuring a National Policy Framework for Artificial Intelligence"](https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2025/12/eliminating-state-law-obstruction-of-national-artificial-intelligence-policy/), carrying the operative heading "Eliminating State Law Obstruction of National Artificial Intelligence Policy." The order declares it U.S. policy to pursue "global AI dominance through a minimally burdensome national policy framework" and directs a sequenced set of federal agency actions to challenge, preempt, or financially disincentivize state AI laws inconsistent with that policy. On March 20, 2026, the White House followed up by releasing a [National Policy Framework for Artificial Intelligence](https://www.whitehouse.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/03.20.26-National-Policy-Framework-for-Artificial-Intelligence-Legislative-Recommendations.pdf) — legislative recommendations to Congress providing the clearest statement yet of the administration's preferred architecture for federal AI legislation. The Framework calls for broad federal preemption of state AI laws imposing "undue burdens" on developers and general-purpose AI systems, while preserving state authority over child safety, fraud prevention, and consumer protection. Neither the EO nor the Framework itself alters existing legal obligations; both must be translated into legislation or survive judicial review to have lasting effect.

## Key Directives from the December 2025 Executive Order [HIGH confidence]

### DOJ AI Litigation Task Force
The Attorney General was required to establish an AI Litigation Task Force whose sole mission is to challenge state AI laws inconsistent with federal policy on grounds such as unconstitutional regulation of interstate commerce, federal preemption, or other unlawfulness. Attorney General Pam Bondi formally established the [DOJ AI Litigation Task Force on January 9, 2026](https://www.sidley.com/en/insights/newsupdates/2025/12/unpacking-the-december-11-2025-executive-order).

### BEAD Broadband Funding Conditions
Within 90 days of the order, the Secretary of Commerce was required to issue a Policy Notice specifying conditions under which states are eligible for remaining Broadband Equity, Access, and Deployment (BEAD) Program funding, making states with "onerous" AI laws ineligible for non-deployment funds "to the maximum extent allowed by Federal law."

### FTC Policy Statement on Bias Mitigation
The FTC was directed to issue a policy statement by March 11, 2026 explaining when state laws requiring alterations to the "truthful outputs of AI models" are preempted by the FTC Act. The operative theory is that state requirements compelling developers to adjust AI outputs for bias mitigation could force "deceptive" outputs under federal law.

### FCC AI Reporting and Disclosure Standard
The order also launched an FCC proceeding to consider a federal reporting and disclosure standard for AI models.

## Key Facts from the March 2026 National Policy Framework [HIGH confidence] [VERIFIED]

- The Framework was issued pursuant to the December 11, 2025 Executive Order, which directed the development of legislative recommendations alongside establishing the DOJ AI Litigation Task Force ([White House Fact Sheet](https://www.whitehouse.gov/fact-sheets/2025/12/fact-sheet-president-donald-j-trump-ensures-a-national-policy-framework-for-artificial-intelligence/)).

- The Framework recommends Congress preempt state laws that regulate AI model development or impose liability on AI developers for unlawful conduct by third parties using their systems, targeting laws that create "undue burdens" on innovation.

- States would retain authority to enforce generally applicable laws protecting children (including prohibition of AI-generated CSAM), preventing fraud, and safeguarding consumers — the Framework explicitly does not preempt these traditional police powers.

- The Framework proposes targeted federal standards for child safety, unauthorized digital replicas, and workforce development, but does not recommend creating a new federal AI regulatory agency.

- On copyright and AI training data, the Framework defers to courts rather than urging Congress to legislate, declining to take a definitive position on fair use.

- Multiple state AI laws took effect on January 1, 2026 (including Texas's Responsible AI Governance Act), and Colorado's AI Act (SB 24-205) is set for June 30, 2026. Congress has so far declined to enact comprehensive federal preemption, including rejecting such provisions in the One Big Beautiful Bill Act.

## Context [HIGH confidence]

The EO and Framework arrive against the backdrop of more than 1,000 AI-related bills introduced across U.S. states and territories in 2025. High-profile state laws potentially in the crosshairs include Colorado's AI Act, California's suite of 2024–2025 AI statutes, and New York City's algorithmic bias audit rule. The Framework is non-binding and does not itself alter existing legal obligations, but it represents the clearest statement yet of the administration's preferred architecture for federal AI legislation.

## Legal Limits and Outlook [MEDIUM confidence]

Commentators note substantial legal limits on the EO's reach: executive orders cannot unilaterally preempt state law, and any DOJ suits or FTC policy statements must still survive judicial review. Preemption theories under the FTC Act — particularly that state bias-mitigation laws are per se deceptive — are novel and untested. The [Center for American Progress](https://www.americanprogress.org/article/president-trumps-ai-national-policy-executive-order-is-an-unambiguous-threat-to-states-beyond-just-ai/) has characterized the order as an "unambiguous threat to states beyond just AI," highlighting the political controversy. Until legislation is enacted, existing state AI laws remain in full effect and enforceable.

## Action Items

- **Monitor congressional action:** Track whether Congress introduces legislation based on the Framework's recommendations. Until legislation is enacted, existing state AI laws remain in full effect and enforceable.
- **Maintain state compliance programs:** Organizations subject to Colorado SB 24-205 (effective June 30, 2026), Texas RAIGA, and other state AI laws should continue compliance preparations. Federal preemption is not assured and may not materialize.
- **Track DOJ AI Litigation Task Force filings:** Monitor for enforcement actions or legal challenges targeting specific state laws — Colorado's AI Act is the most-cited candidate — which could affect state law validity before any legislation passes.
- **Assess BEAD funding exposure:** For clients receiving or expecting BEAD broadband funding, review state AI statutes against the forthcoming Commerce Department Policy Notice on funding conditions.
- **Assess preemption exposure:** Identify which internal AI compliance obligations derive from state laws that the Framework targets for preemption (e.g., laws regulating AI model development or imposing developer liability for third-party misuse) to prepare for potential federal legislative action.
- **Track FTC policy statement:** Monitor whether the FTC's March 11, 2026 policy statement on state bias-mitigation preemption was issued and assess its impact on any ongoing bias-audit or algorithmic-fairness compliance work. **Update (April 2026): The FTC did not issue this statement by the March 11 deadline — see update section below.**

## Update 2026-04-27: FTC and Commerce Miss March EO Deadlines [MEDIUM confidence]

The March 11, 2026 deadlines established by the December 2025 Executive Order have passed without compliance. Per [IAPP reporting](http://info.iapp.org/MTM4LUVaTS0wNDIAAAGhX9bsXHMqf5oTz1X17s6ZsY-VTJFG2cjyWo8ncEO1HWE-isIlM_2cASCJMmOFj3dJ8t2tVtw=):

- **FTC:** Did not issue its required policy statement explaining when state laws requiring alterations to AI model outputs are preempted by the FTC Act.
- **Department of Commerce:** Did not publish its required evaluation of state AI laws or identify "onerous" state regulations for referral to the DOJ AI Litigation Task Force.

The missed deadlines reinforce the pattern of implementation friction noted in the Morgan Lewis analysis (source 16): federal policy stalls even as states continue enacting and enforcing AI laws. **Practical implication:** Organizations that have calibrated state AI compliance timelines against the possibility of federal preemption should not reduce compliance investments. The FTC's failure to issue a preemption theory and Commerce's failure to flag state laws for DOJ enforcement both reduce the near-term credibility of the administration's preemption push. State AI laws — including Colorado's (effective June 30, 2026), Texas's RAIGA (effective January 1, 2026), and California's suite — remain fully operative with no federal displacement in sight.
