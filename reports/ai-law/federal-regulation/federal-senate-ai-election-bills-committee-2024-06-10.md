# Senate Rules Committee Advances Three AI Election Bills in 118th Congress

**Jurisdiction:** Federal | **Category:** ai-law | **Date:** 2024-06-10

## Executive Summary [MEDIUM confidence]

On May 15, 2024, the Senate Rules and Administration Committee, under Chair Amy Klobuchar (D-MN), advanced three bipartisan bills targeting the use of artificial intelligence in federal elections. The package — consisting of the [Protect Elections from Deceptive AI Act (S. 2770)](https://www.congress.gov/bill/118th-congress/senate-bill/2770), the [AI Transparency in Elections Act of 2024 (S. 3875)](https://www.congress.gov/bill/118th-congress/senate-bill/3875), and the [Preparing Election Administrators for AI Act (S. 3897)](https://www.congress.gov/bill/118th-congress/senate-bill/3897) — represented the most comprehensive federal legislative effort to address AI-generated disinformation, deepfakes, and administrative disruption in elections. Despite committee passage, all three bills failed to reach a Senate floor vote and expired at the end of the 118th Congress in January 2025. The package establishes a key reference point for any renewed federal AI-election legislation in the 119th Congress.

## Background [HIGH confidence]

The 2024 election cycle exposed the electoral system to novel AI-enabled threats that no existing federal statute directly addressed. The most prominent early incident was a January 2024 robocall in New Hampshire that used a deepfake of President Biden's voice to discourage primary voters — a scheme for which the [FCC subsequently proposed a $6 million fine](https://www.fcc.gov/document/fcc-makes-ai-generated-voices-robocalls-illegal) against political consultant Steve Kramer. That same month, the FCC unanimously adopted a declaratory ruling clarifying that AI-generated voices in robocalls are "artificial" under the Telephone Consumer Protection Act (TCPA), making such calls illegal without prior express consent.

In parallel, the Senate was conducting AI governance oversight through its bipartisan AI Working Group, which in May 2024 released a [policy roadmap](https://www.ai.senate.gov/news) calling for sectoral legislation rather than a single omnibus AI bill. Elections emerged as a priority sector. Senator Klobuchar, who chairs the Rules Committee — the committee with jurisdiction over federal election law — had introduced or co-introduced each of the three bills before the May markup. An earlier version of AI-disclosure legislation (the REAL Political Ads Act) had been introduced in 2023 but did not advance.

Congress was also reacting to rapid state-level activity. By mid-2024, more than a dozen states had enacted or were advancing their own AI-election laws covering deepfakes, disclosure of AI-generated political ads, and related matters. Klobuchar explicitly argued that a patchwork of state laws was insufficient, stating on the record: "We cannot rely on a patchwork of state laws and voluntary commitments."

## Detailed Analysis [HIGH confidence]

### Protect Elections from Deceptive AI Act (S. 2770)

Introduced September 12, 2023, and co-sponsored by Senators Josh Hawley (R-MO), Chris Coons (D-DE), and Susan Collins (R-ME), [S. 2770](https://www.congress.gov/bill/118th-congress/senate-bill/2770) prohibits the intentional distribution of materially deceptive AI-generated audio or visual media depicting a federal candidate in communications intended to influence a federal election or solicit campaign contributions. "Deceptive AI media" is defined as content where AI or machine learning merges, combines, replaces, or superimposes content onto authentic images, audio, or video in a way that would not be apparent to a reasonable viewer.

Key carve-outs protect bona fide newscasts, satire, and parody, provided the broadcaster clearly acknowledges questions about the content's authenticity. A covered individual whose likeness appears in violating media may seek injunctive or other equitable relief in federal court. The [Congressional Budget Office scored S. 2770](https://www.cbo.gov/publication/60628) and the bill was reported favorably by the Rules Committee on May 15, 2024, with a 9–2 vote.

### AI Transparency in Elections Act of 2024 (S. 3875)

Introduced in March 2024 and co-sponsored by Senator Lisa Murkowski (R-AK), [S. 3875](https://www.congress.gov/bill/118th-congress/senate-bill/3875) amends the Federal Election Campaign Act of 1971 to require disclaimers on federal campaign political communications that include images, audio, or video substantially generated or altered by AI. Minor automated editing — color correction, cropping, resizing — is expressly excluded. The Federal Election Commission (FEC), in consultation with the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST), would promulgate implementing regulations and establish criteria for determining when AI content is "substantial." Penalties are capped at $50,000 per covered communication. The [CBO scored this bill as well](https://www.cbo.gov/publication/60629). It passed committee 9–2.

### Preparing Election Administrators for AI Act (S. 3897)

The sole bill to pass with unanimous bipartisan support (11–0), [S. 3897](https://www.congress.gov/bill/118th-congress/senate-bill/3897) directs the Election Assistance Commission (EAC), in consultation with NIST, to develop voluntary guidelines for election offices addressing: AI use in election administration, AI-related cybersecurity risks, information-sharing protocols, and AI-enabled election disinformation. The bill was co-sponsored by Senator Collins (R-ME) and Senator Mark Kelly (D-AZ). Its voluntary, advisory nature explains the cross-aisle consensus.

### Committee Markup and Floor Outcome

The May 15, 2024 markup produced split results: S. 3897 passed unanimously, while S. 2770 and S. 3875 cleared on party-line 9–2 votes, reflecting Republican concerns about First Amendment implications of content restrictions on AI-generated political speech. Following committee passage, Senate Majority Leader Schumer attempted to move the bills by unanimous consent — a procedure requiring all 100 senators to agree — but Republicans objected. In [floor remarks](https://www.democrats.senate.gov/newsroom/press-releases/majority-leader-schumer-floor-remarks-as-senate-republicans-block-vital-ai-election-legislation), Schumer stated: "If we're not careful AI has the potential to jaundice or even totally discredit our election system." The bills did not receive a floor vote before the end of the 118th Congress on January 3, 2025, and all three expired.

## Impact Assessment [MEDIUM confidence]

### Federal Regulatory Gap

The failure of all three bills leaves a significant federal gap in AI-election regulation. The FCC's TCPA declaratory ruling covers AI robocalls but does not address AI-generated visual deepfakes in broadcast or digital political advertising. The FEC has existing authority to regulate political ad disclaimers under the Federal Election Campaign Act, but has not independently moved to require AI-specific disclosures in the absence of statutory direction.

### State Patchwork Continues

In the absence of federal legislation, state-level regulation has proliferated. [California enacted multiple AI-election laws in 2024](https://www.skadden.com/insights/publications/2024/09/california-enacts-new-laws), including AB 2355 (requiring AI-disclosure in political ads effective January 1, 2025) and AB 2655 (imposing removal obligations on large platforms for election-period deepfakes). By late 2024, approximately 20 states had enacted some form of AI-election restriction. The resulting compliance landscape for multi-state political campaigns and ad platforms is fragmented, with inconsistent definitions of "substantial" AI alteration, varying penalty structures, and differing platform obligations.

### Industry and Platform Exposure

Political campaigns, advertising agencies, and digital platforms face overlapping and potentially conflicting state obligations in 2026 and beyond. Major ad platforms (Google, Meta) have independently implemented AI-disclosure requirements for political advertising, but voluntary measures do not preempt state mandates or substitute for federal uniformity.

### 119th Congress Outlook

[R Street Institute analysis](https://www.rstreet.org/commentary/ai-and-the-2024-election-part-ii-push-for-regulation-falls-short-in-washington-d-c/) published after the 2024 election concludes that federal AI-election regulation faces continued headwinds given First Amendment concerns and partisan disagreement over whether to restrict AI content versus require only disclosure. The administration's January 2026 executive order establishing a national AI policy framework specifically addresses federal preemption of state AI laws, which could affect whether any future federal AI-election statute supersedes the growing state-law patchwork.

## Action Items

- Campaigns and political committees operating in multiple states should map current state AI-election disclosure requirements (at minimum: California, New York, Florida, Colorado, and Texas) and implement compliant review processes for AI-generated ad content before the 2026 midterm cycle.
- Digital advertising platforms running federal political ads should prepare for a potential renewed push by the FEC or Congress to enact AI-disclosure rules in the 119th Congress; maintaining audit trails of AI-generated or AI-altered content in political ads will reduce exposure.
- Election administrators and county election officials should consult the EAC for any guidance issued pursuant to S. 3897's mandate — even in the absence of enacted law, the EAC may publish voluntary AI guidance using existing authority.
- Monitor whether the FEC initiates an independent rulemaking to require AI disclosures in political communications, given that S. 3875's underlying objective (FEC disclosure) falls within existing FEC statutory authority.
- Track whether the 119th Congress reintroduces any of the three bills or a combined successor, particularly given that the Preparing Election Administrators bill (S. 3897) attracted unanimous committee support and is the most likely candidate for standalone advancement.
