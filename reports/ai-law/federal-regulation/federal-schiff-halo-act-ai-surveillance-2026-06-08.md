# Senator Schiff's HALO Act: Federal Legislation to Restrict Military AI and Domestic Surveillance

**Jurisdiction:** Federal | **Category:** AI Law | **Date:** June 8, 2026

## Executive Summary [MEDIUM confidence]

On June 8, 2026, Senator Adam Schiff (D-Calif.) introduced the Human Authority in Lethal Operations Act of 2026 (HALO Act), comprehensive legislation imposing new requirements on the Department of Defense's (DOD) use of artificial intelligence. The bill's two principal pillars are: (1) requiring meaningful human control over autonomous and semi-autonomous weapon systems that may deploy lethal force, and (2) prohibiting the DOD from using AI for domestic mass surveillance of US persons, including monitoring individuals engaged in constitutionally protected activities. The HALO Act builds upon a series of similar Democratic proposals — the Gillibrand and Slotkin bills — that have emerged in the spring of 2026, all aimed at attaching AI guardrails to the annual National Defense Authorization Act (NDAA). The legislation arrives in the aftermath of a highly public dispute between the Pentagon and Anthropic over contractual limitations on the use of Claude AI for autonomous weapons and domestic surveillance. The bill's near-term prospects are uncertain; as an opposition-party measure it faces a difficult path through a Republican-controlled Congress, though supporters are targeting NDAA amendment vehicles that historically attract bipartisan support.

## Background [HIGH confidence]

### The Anthropic-Pentagon Dispute as Legislative Catalyst

The proximate cause of this wave of congressional AI legislation is a dispute between the Department of Defense and Anthropic that became public in early March 2026. Anthropic had signed a $200 million contract with the Pentagon for deployment of its Claude AI model, but negotiations broke down when the DOD sought unfettered use of the technology for all "lawful purposes." Anthropic refused to remove contractual restrictions barring use of Claude for fully autonomous weapons systems and domestic mass surveillance, citing its own internal usage policies.

In response, Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth designated Anthropic a [supply chain risk](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/03/09/anthropic-was-the-pentagons-choice-for-ai-now-its-banned-and-experts-are-worried.html) in early March 2026, and President Trump directed all federal agencies to cease use of Anthropic's technology with a six-month phaseout window. Anthropic sued the DOD, obtaining a preliminary injunction from a San Francisco federal court citing First Amendment retaliation, though a federal appeals court in Washington subsequently declined to block the DOD blacklisting on an emergency basis. As of June 2026, the underlying litigation [remains pending](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/05/19/anthropic-dod-blacklist-court-opening-arguments.html).

This dispute crystallized for lawmakers the absence of any statutory floor preventing the military from deploying AI for autonomous lethal decision-making or mass surveillance of Americans — a gap that multiple senators moved quickly to fill.

### Prior Legislative Activity

Senator Schiff had been [publicly drafting AI guardrails](https://www.schiff.senate.gov/news/press-releases/icymi-axios-sen-schiff-drafting-ai-guardrails-for-autonomous-weapons-domestic-spying/) for autonomous weapons and domestic spying since at least March 2026. His office identified the NDAA as the preferred legislative vehicle, following established practice of attaching defense-related policy riders to the annual must-pass bill.

In parallel, Senator Elissa Slotkin (D-Mich.) introduced the [AI Guardrails Act](https://www.slotkin.senate.gov/2026/03/17/slotkin-legislation-puts-common-sense-guardrails-on-dod-ai-use-around-lethal-force-spying-on-americans-and-nuclear-weapons/) in March 2026, which targeted the same three domains — lethal autonomous weapons, domestic surveillance, and nuclear weapons. Senator Kirsten Gillibrand (D-N.Y.) introduced complementary legislation requiring high-ranking DOD official approval for "high-consequence actions" involving AI. [Senators Chris Coons and Jack Reed](https://thehill.com/policy/defense/5906615-ai-military-guardrails-senate/) also advanced proposals in this space. The convergence of multiple Democratic bills signals coordinated strategy rather than isolated action.

### Biden Administration Background: NSM-24 and DOD Policy

The Biden administration had previously addressed AI in national security through [National Security Memorandum 24 (NSM-24)](https://www.schiff.senate.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/HALO-Act_Text.pdf), which established guidelines for responsible AI use by intelligence and defense agencies. The HALO Act explicitly references NSM-24's prohibitions — including restrictions on profiling individuals for constitutionally protected activities and on purchasing personal data from third parties in violation of applicable statutes — and seeks to codify these provisions as binding law rather than executive policy that a subsequent administration can revoke. The Trump administration had already rolled back significant elements of the Biden-era AI policy framework, creating the statutory gap the HALO Act seeks to address.

## Detailed Analysis [MEDIUM confidence]

### Core Provisions: Human Control Over Lethal Force

The HALO Act's primary focus is DOD weapons systems. The bill would [mandate a clear chain of command](https://www.schiff.senate.gov/news/press-releases/news-schiff-introduces-comprehensive-legislation-to-enact-commonsense-guardrails-for-defense-departments-use-of-ai/) with a designated human commander formally responsible for exercising ultimate discretion in all decisions to employ lethal force involving autonomous or semi-autonomous systems. This commander-accountability model is designed to ensure that AI identifies, recommends, or prioritizes targets, but that a legally accountable human officer retains the authority to authorize or decline engagement.

The bill also institutes:
- A structured review process required before development and deployment of any covered weapon system
- Ongoing testing and evaluation requirements for autonomous systems already fielded
- Mandatory post-action decision logs documenting how targets were selected and how the human commander exercised discretion
- Whistleblower protections for military and defense-contractor personnel who report violations

The prohibition on removing humans from the nuclear weapons decision chain aligns the HALO Act with longstanding bipartisan consensus and existing DOD policy, making this provision the least controversial element of the bill.

### Domestic Surveillance Prohibitions

The HALO Act contains a separate but equally significant domestic surveillance pillar. [Multiple reporting sources](https://gizmodo.com/democrats-want-a-military-ai-restriction-law-following-anthropics-pentagon-fallout-2000768990) confirm the bill would prohibit the DOD from:

- Using AI to conduct mass surveillance of American individuals or groups
- Profiling or monitoring individuals solely based on their exercise of First, Fourth, or Fourteenth Amendment rights
- Purchasing personal data about US persons from third-party commercial data brokers where such acquisition would circumvent statutory protections (e.g., prohibitions that would otherwise require a warrant)

The domestic surveillance provisions are directly responsive to growing concern that AI's capacity to aggregate and analyze commercial datasets — location data, communications metadata, purchasing records — creates effective mass surveillance infrastructure without requiring traditional electronic interception that would require court approval. Senator Schiff has separately [joined Senator Markey in pressing ICE](https://www.schiff.senate.gov/news/press-releases/icymi-sen-schiff-joins-markey-colleagues-to-press-ice-again-on-use-of-invasive-facial-recognition-surveillance-technology/) on its use of invasive facial recognition surveillance technology, establishing his broader civil liberties posture in this area.

NBC News reported in May 2026 that [AI's ability to sift through data and track Americans' locations](https://www.nbcnews.com/tech/security/ai-making-easy-government-spy-lawmakers-are-worried-rcna341499) had prompted lawmakers to reconsider parts of the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act (FISA), with the HALO Act representing one concrete legislative response to this broader concern.

### Scope: DOD Only

It is important to note that the HALO Act's restrictions apply specifically to the Department of Defense, not to the intelligence community, domestic law enforcement, or civilian agencies. The bill does not impose restrictions on FBI or DHS use of AI surveillance tools, facial recognition, or commercial data purchases — areas where separate legislative efforts, including proposals from Senators Markey, Merkley, Wyden, and Representative Jayapal, are underway. The HALO Act's domestic surveillance provisions, while significant for military contexts, do not constitute a general federal prohibition on government AI surveillance of Americans.

### Official Bill Text Status

The bill was introduced on June 8, 2026. The bill text is available as a draft PDF from [Senator Schiff's official website](https://www.schiff.senate.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/HALO-Act_Text.pdf) (119th Congress, 2d Session, designated as "S. ll" — a placeholder pending official Senate bill number assignment). Note that "S.3179" in the 119th Congress refers to a different bill introduced by Senator Moody on an unrelated immigration enforcement matter; Senator Schiff's HALO Act carries a distinct designation. Official text could not be confirmed on Congress.gov at time of writing, and the assigned Senate bill number should be verified at Congress.gov once enrollment is complete.

### NDAA Strategy

Schiff and fellow Democratic sponsors are pursuing inclusion in the [FY2027 National Defense Authorization Act](https://thehill.com/policy/defense/5906615-ai-military-guardrails-senate/), the annual defense policy bill that Congress must pass each year. The NDAA vehicle increases viability because it attracts bipartisan support and carries significant legislative weight, but the Republican-controlled Senate Armed Services Committee controls the markup process. AI policy groups have [formally called for NDAA guardrails](https://thehill.com/policy/technology/5908512-congress-add-ai-guardrails-ndaa/) on lethal autonomous weapons, and some Republican senators have expressed openness to at minimum a human-in-the-loop requirement for nuclear launch decisions.

## Impact Assessment [MEDIUM confidence]

### Defense Contractors and AI Vendors

If enacted, the HALO Act would directly affect defense contractors and AI companies operating under DOD contracts. The requirement for human-command accountability structures would impose new design mandates on autonomous systems under development, potentially requiring significant modifications to existing programs such as autonomous drone systems and AI-assisted targeting platforms. The Anthropic dispute has demonstrated that AI companies are themselves asserting usage restrictions — the HALO Act would codify the position Anthropic took contractually as federal law.

Companies such as Palantir, Anduril, and Shield AI, which provide AI-integrated defense platforms, would need to ensure their systems include the command-accountability architecture and record-keeping requirements mandated by the bill. The testing and evaluation requirements add another layer of compliance obligations throughout the acquisition lifecycle.

### Intelligence and Surveillance Technology Industry

The domestic surveillance prohibition, applied to DOD, would foreclose certain commercial data-broker contracts with defense entities. Companies providing bulk commercial location data, behavioral analytics, or social media monitoring tools to DOD would face new legal constraints. The private commercial data purchase prohibition is notable because it targets a surveillance modality that has been used to circumvent Fourth Amendment warrant requirements by purchasing data that would otherwise require legal process to obtain.

### Compliance Timeline

No enactment timeline can be specified at this stage. The bill was introduced on June 8, 2026, and has not yet been referred to or scheduled for markup by the Senate Armed Services Committee. If attached to the NDAA, the relevant compliance deadlines would likely run from the date of presidential signature, which for defense authorization bills typically occurs in December of each year. Standalone passage outside the NDAA is considered unlikely given the current political composition of Congress.

### Likelihood Assessment

The bill faces a difficult path in a Republican-controlled Senate. However, [several factors](https://thehill.com/policy/defense/5781156-schiff-drafts-ai-bill-military-guardrails/) increase its prospects for at least partial inclusion in the NDAA: (1) bipartisan concern about AI in nuclear weapons already exists; (2) the Anthropic-Pentagon dispute made the absence of statutory guardrails politically visible; (3) multiple Democratic senators are coordinating the same amendments, presenting a unified package; and (4) AI policy coalitions are formally lobbying for NDAA inclusion. The human-control-over-lethal-force provisions are more likely to attract Republican support than the domestic surveillance prohibitions.

## Action Items

- Monitor Senate Armed Services Committee markup of the FY2027 NDAA for inclusion of HALO Act provisions or compromise language
- Defense contractors and AI vendors with DOD contracts should assess current autonomous system designs against the bill's human-commander accountability requirements
- AI companies with existing or prospective DOD agreements should review contract language regarding permissible use cases in light of the bill's prohibition framework
- Legal and compliance teams should track whether the Senate bill number is formally assigned and the bill enrolled in Congress.gov for authoritative text tracking
- Track parallel legislative activity: Slotkin AI Guardrails Act and Gillibrand bill — these may be consolidated or traded off during NDAA negotiations
- Monitor the ongoing Anthropic v. DOD litigation, which may produce court rulings defining the scope of permissible government AI use restrictions independent of legislation
