# Republican AGs Argue for Unbiased Regulation of AI in Financial Services Sector

**Jurisdiction:** Federal, Multi-state | **Category:** Privacy / AI Regulation | **Date:** August 2024

## Executive Summary [MEDIUM confidence]

A coalition of 15 Republican state attorneys general, led by Tennessee AG Jonathan Skrmetti, submitted a comment letter on August 12, 2024, to the U.S. Department of the Treasury in response to the Treasury's [Request for Information on Uses, Opportunities, and Risks of Artificial Intelligence in the Financial Services Sector](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2024/06/12/2024-12336/request-for-information-on-uses-opportunities-and-risks-of-artificial-intelligence-in-the-financial) (Docket TREAS-DO-2024-0011). The letter urges Treasury to regulate AI in financial services on narrow, technical grounds focused on financial reliability and consumer protection — not on environmental, social, or cultural agendas. The AGs warn against using AI rulemaking to facilitate so-called "debanking" of disfavored lawful industries, to preempt state-level financial protection laws, or to embed Biden administration equity priorities into foundational AI regulation. The comment reflects a broader Republican AG strategy of coordinated state-level pushback against federal financial regulators perceived as exceeding their statutory mandates.

## Background [HIGH confidence]

### Treasury's AI Request for Information

On June 12, 2024, the U.S. Department of the Treasury [published an RFI in the Federal Register](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2024/06/12/2024-12336/request-for-information-on-uses-opportunities-and-risks-of-artificial-intelligence-in-the-financial) (Document No. 2024-12336, Docket TREAS-DO-2024-0011), seeking public input on the uses, opportunities, and risks of AI in the financial services sector. The comment deadline was August 12, 2024 — the same date the AGs' letter was submitted.

The RFI was organized into three substantive parts:

- **Part A:** Uses of AI in financial institutions, including types of models employed and variability in AI access across institution sizes.
- **Part B:** Opportunities and risks associated with AI use, including issues related to bias, discrimination, consumer protection, and data privacy.
- **Part C:** Potential further actions to advance responsible AI innovation and competition in the financial sector.

Treasury received [103 comment letters](https://home.treasury.gov/news/press-releases/jy2393) from a broad range of stakeholders, including financial institutions, fintech firms, consumer advocacy organizations, trade associations, and academic researchers. The RFI was part of Treasury's broader multi-year engagement with AI policy, which also included earlier reports specifically addressing AI governance and cybersecurity risks in financial services.

### Context: Republican AGs and Financial Regulation

The multi-state AG letter emerged within a pattern of Republican AGs using coordinated comment campaigns to challenge Biden-era financial regulators. Tennessee AG Jonathan Skrmetti had been particularly active on related issues: in June 2024, just weeks before the Treasury RFI comment deadline, [Skrmetti warned major credit card networks](https://www.tn.gov/attorneygeneral/news/2024/6/27/pr24-53.html) (American Express, Mastercard, and Visa) to comply with Tennessee's [Second Amendment Financial Privacy Act](https://blog.tenthamendmentcenter.com/2024/04/tennessee-governor-signs-second-amendment-financial-privacy-act-into-law/), which prohibits financial institutions from using merchant category codes (MCCs) to track firearm and ammunition purchases. That legislation, signed by Governor Bill Lee in April 2024 and effective July 1, 2024, exemplifies the genre of state laws the AGs were seeking to insulate from federal preemption via AI rulemaking.

## Detailed Analysis [MEDIUM confidence]

### Signatories

The comment letter was submitted on behalf of the attorneys general of [15 states](https://www.tn.gov/attorneygeneral/news/2024/8/13/pr24-62.html):

Tennessee (lead), Alabama, Arkansas, Florida, Idaho, Iowa, Louisiana, Mississippi, Montana, Nebraska, South Carolina, South Dakota, Utah, Virginia, and West Virginia.

### Core Arguments in the Letter

The AGs' letter advanced several interconnected arguments:

**1. Treasury should focus narrowly on financial risks.** The AGs urged Treasury to restrict any resulting AI regulation to genuine financial sector concerns — specifically, risks to financial system reliability, safety and soundness, and consumer protection. They opposed any use of the rulemaking process to pursue climate, equity, or other non-financial policy objectives.

**2. AI regulation must not be "politicized."** AG Skrmetti stated: "We need to ensure that foundational AI regulation in the financial sector does not fall prey to an Administration that has repeatedly abused its financial regulatory power to illegally impose activist mandates that have nothing to do with financial services." The letter argued Treasury should not act as an environmental regulator or as an arbiter of American culture through AI rulemaking.

**3. AI should not be used as a tool for debanking.** The AGs expressed concern that AI-based financial risk models could be deployed — whether intentionally or as a proximate effect of equity mandates — to disadvantage lawful businesses and consumers in disfavored sectors. The letter specifically cited church missionaries and cryptocurrency entrepreneurs as examples of groups that could be subject to AI-enabled debanking.

**4. Federal AI rules must not preempt state laws.** The coalition urged Treasury to avoid drafting AI regulations that would preempt or undermine state laws protecting financial access for lawful activities. This concern directly tracks the Tennessee Second Amendment Financial Privacy Act and similar statutes enacted in other Republican-led states targeting discriminatory use of payment and banking infrastructure.

**5. AI innovation should be encouraged.** The AGs acknowledged the potential benefits of AI in financial services — including consumer-facing chatbots, expedited credit underwriting, and enhanced fraud and financial crime detection — and urged Treasury not to create regulatory barriers to beneficial innovation.

### Regulatory Status of the RFI and Subsequent Developments

An RFI does not constitute a proposed rule and does not impose binding obligations. Treasury used the comment record to inform future policy analysis. On [December 19, 2024, the Treasury released a follow-up report](https://home.treasury.gov/news/press-releases/jy2760) synthesizing the 103 RFI responses and recommending next steps. The December 2024 report highlighted four broad opportunity areas (credit underwriting, fraud detection, customer service, and regulatory compliance) alongside risks related to data quality, model explainability ("black box" systems), and concentration risk from reliance on a small number of major AI infrastructure providers.

The December 2024 report did not specifically address the Republican AGs' debanking concerns or discuss preemption of state financial privacy laws. Under the Trump administration (inaugurated January 2025), the policy landscape shifted significantly: an [August 2025 Executive Order explicitly targeted "politicized or unlawful debanking"](https://www.mcguirewoods.com/client-resources/alerts/2025/8/executive-order-addresses-politicized-or-unlawful-debanking-by-financial-institutions/) by financial institutions, validating the concerns the AGs had raised in their 2024 Treasury comment.

### Relationship to Broader AG AI Activism

In a separate but contemporaneous development, Tennessee AG Skrmetti later [co-led a bipartisan letter opposing a federal amendment that would have limited state AI regulation authority](https://www.wkrn.com/news/tennessee-news/tn-attorney-general-co-leads-letter-opposing-amendment-that-would-limit-state-ai-regulation/), demonstrating consistent opposition to federal preemption of state-level AI and financial law regardless of which branch of government was acting. This dual posture — resisting federal AI overreach while protecting state regulatory space — characterizes the Republican AG coalition's approach to AI governance in financial services.

## Impact Assessment [MEDIUM confidence]

### Affected Entities

The AGs' letter has direct relevance to:

- **Financial institutions** subject to federal AI-related regulations or guidance, particularly those operating across multiple states with divergent state-level financial privacy laws.
- **Fintech companies and cryptocurrency businesses**, which were specifically cited as targets of potential AI-enabled debanking.
- **Faith-based organizations and other "disfavored" sectors** (including firearms dealers and related businesses) that have faced banking access challenges.
- **State legislatures** considering financial privacy legislation analogous to Tennessee's Second Amendment Financial Privacy Act.

### Compliance Considerations

Because this was an RFI comment rather than a final rule, no new compliance obligations arose directly from the letter. However, compliance officers at financial institutions should note:

- The AGs' letter signals strong multi-state resistance to any federal AI framework that incorporates ESG or equity scoring into financial risk models, which could generate legal challenges.
- States with Republican AGs are likely to challenge any Treasury AI rulemaking that is perceived as de facto financial regulation by agenda-driven proxy.
- Institutions operating in Tennessee and similar states must comply with existing state financial privacy laws (e.g., the Second Amendment Financial Privacy Act) regardless of federal guidance.

### Enforcement Outlook

The transition to the Trump administration in January 2025 substantially reduced the likelihood of Treasury pursuing an AI rulemaking that the Republican AGs opposed. The subsequent debanking Executive Order (August 2025) suggests that the policy concerns raised in the AGs' letter have been largely validated at the federal level. However, financial institutions remain subject to a patchwork of state-level financial privacy and fair-access laws that carry independent enforcement risk.

## Action Items

- Monitor Treasury for any AI-specific rulemaking or guidance following the December 2024 RFI report, noting that debanking and state preemption concerns remain politically salient.
- Financial institutions with AI-driven credit, fraud, or customer service models should review model governance frameworks for compliance with state fair-access and anti-debanking statutes.
- Organizations operating in Tennessee should confirm compliance with the Second Amendment Financial Privacy Act (effective July 1, 2024) and analogous state laws in other signatory states.
- Track Republican AG coalition letters on financial regulation — the 15-state coalition represents a coordinated enforcement threat for financial institutions perceived as politically discriminatory.
- Consult counsel before deploying AI models that use alternative data (rent, utilities, social indicators) in credit underwriting, particularly in states with active AG oversight of AI bias.
