# CFTC Technology Advisory Committee Calls for AI Rulemaking in Financial Markets

**Jurisdiction:** Federal | **Category:** AI Law | **Date:** 2024-05-02

## Executive Summary [HIGH confidence]

On May 2, 2024, the Commodity Futures Trading Commission's (CFTC) Technology Advisory Committee (TAC) approved and advanced a report titled "Responsible Artificial Intelligence in Financial Markets: Opportunities, Risks & Recommendations" to the full Commission. The report, drafted by the TAC's Subcommittee on Emerging and Evolving Technologies, makes five formal recommendations to the CFTC, including calls for agency rulemaking, adoption of a sector-specific AI Risk Management Framework (RMF) modeled on NIST standards, and enhanced interagency coordination. The development signals that CFTC-regulated entities — including swap dealers, futures commission merchants, designated contract markets, and swap execution facilities — should expect binding AI governance requirements within the next several years. For compliance officers and legal counsel, the report provides a detailed preview of the risk categories and governance structures likely to form the backbone of any forthcoming CFTC rulemaking on AI.

## Background [HIGH confidence]

The CFTC's Technology Advisory Committee was established in 1999 to advise the Commission on complex issues at the intersection of technology, law, policy, and finance. It conducts public meetings and submits reports and recommendations to the Commission. The TAC is sponsored by a designated Commissioner; the AI work stream was sponsored by Commissioner Christy Goldsmith Romero.

The AI report emerged from a multi-month study by the TAC's Subcommittee on Emerging and Evolving Technologies. The Subcommittee was co-chaired by Dr. Nichol Turner Lee, Senior Fellow and Director of the Brookings Center for Technology Innovation, and Todd Smith, Director of Information Systems at the National Futures Association (NFA). The Subcommittee's work culminated in a dedicated "AI Day" public meeting held at CFTC headquarters on [May 2, 2024](https://www.cftc.gov/PressRoom/Events/opaeventtac050224), during which the TAC formally approved the report and voted to advance it to the Commission.

Prior to the TAC report, the CFTC had already begun exploring AI's implications for derivatives markets. In January 2024, the CFTC issued a [Request for Comment on AI in CFTC-Regulated Markets](https://www.cftc.gov/PressRoom/PressReleases/8853-24), seeking industry input on how AI is being deployed and what regulatory gaps exist. That RFC established the foundation on which the TAC's recommendations were built. The "AI Day" meeting also featured presentations from the Federal Reserve, U.S. Department of the Treasury, and NIST on their respective AI policy initiatives, reflecting the broader interagency context in which the CFTC was operating.

Simultaneously, on May 2, 2024, the CFTC [announced the appointment of Dr. Ted Kaouk as its first Chief Artificial Intelligence Officer (CAIO)](https://www.cftc.gov/PressRoom/PressReleases/8897-24), signaling an institutional commitment to developing internal AI expertise — one of the TAC's core recommendations. The CAIO appointment and the TAC report together represent the most significant formal action the CFTC had taken on AI governance as of mid-2024.

## Detailed Analysis [HIGH confidence]

### Report Title and Scope

The full official report title is "Responsible Artificial Intelligence in Financial Markets: Opportunities, Risks & Recommendations." The [report is publicly available from CFTC.gov](https://www.cftc.gov/media/10626/TAC_AIReport050224/download). It covers AI use across the full lifecycle of derivatives trading and the entities that participate in CFTC-regulated markets, including registered entities such as Designated Contract Markets (DCMs), Swap Execution Facilities (SEFs), Swap Data Repositories (SDRs), and registrants such as swap dealers, futures commission merchants, introducing brokers, and commodity pool operators.

### Definition of Responsible AI

The report adopts a five-property definition of "responsible AI" that will likely anchor any future CFTC rulemaking: (1) **Fairness** — AI systems must not produce discriminatory or biased outcomes; (2) **Robustness** — AI must perform reliably and resist adversarial attacks; (3) **Transparency** — the purposes and mechanisms of AI systems must be disclosed; (4) **Explainability** — the reasoning behind AI outputs must be interpretable; and (5) **Privacy** — AI systems must handle personal and sensitive data lawfully and securely. This framework closely tracks the OSTP "Blueprint for an AI Bill of Rights" and the NIST AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF), both of which the report explicitly cites as reference standards.

### AI Use Cases in Derivatives Markets

The Subcommittee identified five major categories of AI use in CFTC-regulated markets:

1. **Trading and Investment**: AI tools to analyze market data, identify trade execution strategies, predict asset prices, and conduct high-frequency trading (HFT).
2. **Regulatory Compliance and Risk Management**: Market surveillance, recordkeeping, KYC/AML-CFT transaction monitoring, employee communications monitoring, and account transfer monitoring.
3. **Customer Service and Operations**: Chatbots, robo-advisors, fraud detection, and customer-facing AI interfaces.
4. **RegTech Applications**: Back-testing of trading strategies, predictive analytics, and credit risk modeling.
5. **Internal Risk Functions**: Operational risk management, counterparty credit risk assessment, and stress-testing models.

### Risk Categories Identified

The report provides a granular taxonomy of AI-associated risks in each use case category. Key risks include:

- **Data Overfitting**: AI models that are too tightly calibrated to training data and fail to generalize, producing unreliable predictions in live markets.
- **Data Poisoning**: Cyberattacks in which training data is intentionally corrupted to manipulate AI outputs — a significant concern for trading AI.
- **AI Hallucinations**: AI systems generating plausible but factually incorrect outputs, with potential for misinformation in customer-facing or compliance-facing applications.
- **Bias and Discrimination**: AI models that embed or amplify historical biases, producing disparate outcomes for protected classes in areas such as credit risk or customer service.
- **Explainability Deficits ("Black Box" Risk)**: AI systems whose decision-making is opaque, creating legal risk under existing CFTC disclosure and supervisory obligations.
- **Data Privacy Risk**: AI systems that process personally identifiable information (PII) in ways that may violate applicable privacy obligations.
- **Concentration Risk**: The derivatives market's growing reliance on a small number of foundation model providers and cloud infrastructure vendors, which could create systemic risk if a major provider fails or is compromised.
- **Critical Infrastructure Dependence**: Broader market stability risks arising from widespread adoption of similar AI architectures across multiple regulated entities.

The [CFTC press release](https://www.cftc.gov/PressRoom/PressReleases/8905-24) confirms these risk categories were formally presented and discussed at the TAC meeting before the report was advanced.

### The Five Formal Recommendations

The TAC advanced five formal recommendations to the Commission:

**Recommendation 1 — Public Roundtable and Industry Outreach:** The CFTC should host a public roundtable and engage in structured outreach to gather industry input on AI adoption in regulated markets. This recommendation acknowledges that the Commission needs more granular data on how AI is actually being deployed before it can design effective rules.

**Recommendation 2 — Sector-Specific AI Risk Management Framework (RMF):** The CFTC should define and adopt a sector-specific AI RMF for derivatives markets, modeled on the NIST AI RMF, to assess AI model efficiency and potential consumer harms as they apply to CFTC-registered entities. This is the most consequential recommendation — it explicitly calls for rulemaking to codify governance requirements that would be mandatory for registrants.

**Recommendation 3 — Regulatory Gap Analysis:** The CFTC should inventory existing AI regulations across the financial sector and identify gaps where current authority is insufficient. Where gaps exist, the report calls for the CFTC to develop further guidance or initiate rulemaking.

**Recommendation 4 — Interagency Alignment:** The CFTC should coordinate AI policy with the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC), the U.S. Department of the Treasury, the Federal Reserve, and other financial regulators to avoid fragmented or conflicting regulatory regimes.

**Recommendation 5 — Internal Capacity and Staff Expertise:** The CFTC should increase staff engagement in domestic and international AI standard-setting dialogues and supplement its budget to build internal AI expertise. The contemporaneous appointment of the agency's first Chief AI Officer directly responds to this recommendation.

### Legal Authority

The CFTC's rulemaking authority for AI governance would derive primarily from the Commodity Exchange Act (CEA), 7 U.S.C. § 1 et seq. Key existing regulatory hooks include: (a) risk management program requirements for swap dealers under CFTC Regulation 23.600; (b) system safeguards requirements for DCMs and SEFs under Regulations 38.1051 and 37.1401; and (c) recordkeeping requirements under Regulation 1.31 and Part 45. The TAC report acknowledges that "existing regulations likely already reach many AI-associated risks," while also noting that other areas may require clarification via guidance or formal amendment through rulemaking.

## Impact Assessment [MEDIUM confidence]

### Entities Affected

All CFTC-registered entities and registrants face potential new compliance obligations if the agency proceeds to rulemaking based on the TAC's recommendations. The most directly affected categories include:

- **Swap Dealers and Major Swap Participants**: Subject to robust risk management program requirements under Part 23 of CFTC regulations. Any AI RMF rulemaking would likely add AI-specific governance layers (model validation, third-party vendor oversight, documentation) to existing obligations.
- **Futures Commission Merchants (FCMs)**: Subject to risk management requirements under Regulation 1.11. FCMs using AI for customer onboarding, fraud detection, or trading system management would need documented AI governance procedures.
- **Designated Contract Markets (DCMs) and Swap Execution Facilities (SEFs)**: Subject to system safeguards and market surveillance obligations. AI-driven surveillance tools and order processing systems would be subject to new governance requirements.
- **Commodity Trading Advisors (CTAs) and Commodity Pool Operators (CPOs)**: Entities using AI-driven trading algorithms face potential new disclosure and documentation obligations.

### Compliance Implications

The report's call for a sector-specific RMF modeled on NIST AI RMF suggests that any forthcoming CFTC rules would likely require: (a) formal AI inventory and use-case documentation; (b) pre-deployment risk assessments for AI systems used in regulated functions; (c) ongoing model performance monitoring and validation; (d) third-party AI vendor due diligence and contractual protections; and (e) board and senior management accountability for AI governance programs.

The December 2024 [CFTC Staff Advisory on AI](https://www.cftc.gov/PressRoom/PressReleases/9013-24), issued seven months after the TAC report, confirms this trajectory. That advisory — issued jointly by the Divisions of Clearing and Risk, Data, Market Oversight, and Market Participants — explicitly reminded regulated entities of their existing CEA and CFTC regulation obligations as applied to AI, characterized itself as a "first step," and noted that staff would continue to evaluate whether new regulations are needed.

### Concentration and Systemic Risk

The TAC's identification of concentration risk as a key concern is notable. The report flags that the derivatives industry's growing reliance on a small number of AI foundation model providers (e.g., major cloud-based large language model providers) could create systemic vulnerabilities. This signals that CFTC rulemaking may include vendor diversity requirements or third-party concentration risk assessments, analogous to existing guidance on critical vendor concentration in banking regulation.

### Enforcement Outlook

As of mid-2024, the report carried advisory weight only — it was not binding on market participants. However, the December 2024 Staff Advisory made clear that existing CFTC regulations already reach many AI-associated risks, meaning enforcement exposure exists today for entities that fail to apply existing risk management, recordkeeping, and system safeguards obligations to their AI deployments. The staff's stated intent to continue evaluating the advisory and potentially recommend new regulations increases the probability of formal rulemaking within a 2-4 year horizon.

## Action Items

- Review all AI deployments across regulated functions (trading, surveillance, compliance, customer service) against the five responsible AI properties: fairness, robustness, transparency, explainability, and privacy.
- Assess whether existing risk management programs under Regulation 23.600 (swap dealers) or Regulation 1.11 (FCMs) adequately address AI-specific risks identified in the TAC report, including data poisoning, model overfitting, and concentration risk from AI vendors.
- Conduct a third-party AI vendor inventory; document the AI tools procured from external providers and assess whether vendor agreements include adequate representations on model governance, auditability, and data handling.
- Monitor the CFTC's response to the TAC recommendations: watch for follow-on roundtable announcements, proposed rulemakings in the Federal Register, or additional staff advisories that build on the December 2024 advisory.
- Engage in the CFTC's regulatory outreach processes (roundtables, comment periods) to provide industry perspective on what a workable sector-specific AI RMF would require from regulated entities.
- Consult the NIST AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF 1.0, January 2023) and OSTP "Blueprint for an AI Bill of Rights" as the baseline reference standards explicitly cited by the TAC report; begin mapping internal AI governance policies to those frameworks proactively.
- Track interagency developments: the TAC explicitly called for coordination with the SEC and Treasury; parallel SEC AI rulemaking or guidance could impose overlapping obligations for dually-registered entities.
