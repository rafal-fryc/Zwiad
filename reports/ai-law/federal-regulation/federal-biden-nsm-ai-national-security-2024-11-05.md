# Biden Administration Issues First-Ever National Security Memorandum on Artificial Intelligence

**Jurisdiction:** Federal | **Category:** ai-law | **Date:** 2024-11-05

## Executive Summary [HIGH confidence]

On October 24, 2024, President Biden signed the first-ever [National Security Memorandum (NSM) on Artificial Intelligence](https://bidenwhitehouse.archives.gov/briefing-room/presidential-actions/2024/10/24/memorandum-on-advancing-the-united-states-leadership-in-artificial-intelligence-harnessing-artificial-intelligence-to-fulfill-national-security-objectives-and-fostering-the-safety-security/), formally titled "Memorandum on Advancing the United States' Leadership in Artificial Intelligence; Harnessing Artificial Intelligence to Fulfill National Security Objectives; and Fostering the Safety, Security, and Trustworthiness of Artificial Intelligence." At nearly 40 pages, it is the most comprehensive articulation of U.S. national security strategy with respect to AI to date. The NSM was issued pursuant to subsection 4.8 of [Executive Order 14110](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CFR-2024-title3-vol1/pdf/CFR-2024-title3-vol1-eo14110.pdf) of October 30, 2023, which directed the National Security Council to oversee the development of a classified NSM governing AI in national security systems. Three overarching objectives drive the document: (1) preserving U.S. leadership in safe, secure, and trustworthy AI development; (2) harnessing frontier AI for national security missions; and (3) advancing international AI governance aligned with democratic values. The NSM's founding parent directive — EO 14110 — was revoked on January 20, 2025, by President Trump's [Executive Order 14148](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/01/28/2025-01901/initial-rescissions-of-harmful-executive-orders-and-actions), creating significant uncertainty about which NSM directives remain operative policy.

## Background [HIGH confidence]

### From Executive Order 14110 to the NSM

The October 2024 NSM grew directly from [Executive Order 14110, "Safe, Secure, and Trustworthy Development and Use of Artificial Intelligence,"](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CFR-2024-title3-vol1/pdf/CFR-2024-title3-vol1-eo14110.pdf) signed October 30, 2023. Section 4.8 of EO 14110 directed the Assistant to the President for National Security Affairs (APNSA) to lead an interagency process to produce a National Security Memorandum governing AI used in national security systems, for military purposes, and for intelligence collection and analysis. The NSM was intended to address the governance gap EO 14110 itself acknowledged: the executive order's primary consumer-safety and civil society provisions did not directly address classified intelligence applications of AI.

EO 14110 had itself emerged from a broader policy context. The [National Security Commission on Artificial Intelligence (NSCAI)](https://www.nscai.gov), created by the FY2019 National Defense Authorization Act, had issued a landmark [final report in March 2021](https://www.nscai.gov/2021-final-report/) warning that the United States risked losing its AI edge to China. The NSCAI recommended $32 billion annually in non-defense AI R&D, a rapid build-out of AI talent across government, and the integration of AI into intelligence and defense systems under clear ethical and governance frameworks. The October 2024 NSM operationalized many of these recommendations at the level of classified national security systems.

### The Frontier AI Context

The NSM is explicitly focused on "frontier AI models" — highly capable, general-purpose AI systems such as large language models that became publicly prominent after OpenAI's release of ChatGPT in November 2022. Unlike narrow AI applications, frontier models present novel dual-use concerns: their same general capabilities that enable scientific discovery can assist in generating biological threats, conducting offensive cyber operations, or producing information warfare at scale. The NSM addressed this dual-use character by establishing classified testing requirements and cross-agency safety evaluations specifically targeting frontier AI risks.

## Detailed Analysis [HIGH confidence]

### Three-Pillar Structure

The NSM is organized around three primary objectives, each carrying distinct agency responsibilities and implementation timelines.

**Pillar 1 — U.S. Leadership in Safe and Trustworthy AI.** The [Department of Commerce](https://bidenwhitehouse.archives.gov/briefing-room/statements-releases/2024/10/24/fact-sheet-biden-harris-administration-outlines-coordinated-approach-to-harness-power-of-ai-for-u-s-national-security/), acting through the [AI Safety Institute (AISI)](https://www.nist.gov/artificial-intelligence) and NIST, is designated the primary federal government point of contact with private-sector AI developers for voluntary testing of dual-use foundation models. Within 180 days of the NSM (approximately April 22, 2025), AISI was directed to issue guidance for AI developers on how to test, evaluate, and manage risks to safety, security, and trustworthiness arising from dual-use foundation models. AISI was also positioned to coordinate classified evaluations with national security agencies, bridging the commercial and classified domains.

**Pillar 2 — Harnessing AI for National Security Missions.** The NSM directs the [Department of Defense (DOD)](https://www.mayerbrown.com/en/insights/publications/2024/10/biden-administration-issues-first-ever-national-security-memorandum-on-artificial-intelligence) and the Intelligence Community (IC) to examine how existing policies and procedures can be revised to enable effective and responsible AI use. DOD and the Office of the Director of National Intelligence (ODNI) are directed to establish a joint working group to address AI procurement by DOD and IC elements, with recommendations to the Federal Acquisition Regulatory Council (FAR Council) for regulatory changes to accelerate and simplify AI acquisition.

The [National Security Agency (NSA)](https://www.csis.org/analysis/biden-administrations-national-security-memorandum-ai-explained) is directed to develop the capability to perform rapid, systematic classified testing of AI models' capacity to detect, generate, and exacerbate offensive cyber threats. The Department of Energy (DOE) is similarly directed to conduct classified testing related to nuclear and radiological risk scenarios arising from AI.

Within 210 days of the NSM, DOE, DHS, and AISI — in consultation with DOD and other agencies — are directed to develop a roadmap for classified evaluations of advanced AI models' capacity to generate or exacerbate deliberate chemical and biological threats. DOE is directed to establish a pilot program for classified testing in this area.

**Pillar 3 — International AI Governance.** The [Department of State](https://www.cov.com/en/news-and-insights/insights/2024/11/white-house-issues-national-security-memorandum-on-artificial-intelligence-ai) is directed to produce a strategy for the advancement of international AI governance norms aligned with safe, secure, and trustworthy AI, and with democratic values including human rights, civil liberties, and privacy. This pillar reflects the Biden administration's multilateral approach to AI governance, consistent with the [Political Declaration on Responsible Military Use of AI](https://www.state.gov/political-declaration-on-responsible-military-use-of-artificial-intelligence-and-autonomy/) and engagements through the G7 Hiroshima AI Process.

### AI Governance Framework for National Security

Alongside the NSM itself, the administration released the [Framework to Advance AI Governance and Risk Management in National Security](https://bidenwhitehouse.archives.gov/briefing-room/statements-releases/2024/10/24/fact-sheet-biden-harris-administration-outlines-coordinated-approach-to-harness-power-of-ai-for-u-s-national-security/). This Framework operationalizes governance requirements for AI systems used in national security missions, covering:

- **Risk management:** Agencies must monitor, assess, and mitigate AI risks related to privacy, bias and discrimination, safety, and human rights abuses.
- **Prohibited uses:** Agencies are directed to identify and prohibit "high-impact AI use cases" based on risks they pose to national security, international norms, and civil rights. Specifically, agencies are prohibited from using AI to assign emotions, evaluate trustworthiness, or infer race in ways inconsistent with law.
- **Accountability structures:** Each covered agency must name a [Chief AI Officer](https://www.cov.com/en/news-and-insights/insights/2024/11/white-house-issues-national-security-memorandum-on-artificial-intelligence-ai) and establish an AI Governance Board that includes privacy, civil liberties, and safety officials.
- **Data management:** Agencies must develop data management policies governing the use of AI for automated, mission-critical determinations, with standards for evaluation and auditing.

### Classified Annex

The NSM includes a classified annex addressing particularly sensitive national security applications. The annex is not publicly available; however, the [White House fact sheet](https://bidenwhitehouse.archives.gov/briefing-room/statements-releases/2024/10/24/fact-sheet-biden-harris-administration-outlines-coordinated-approach-to-harness-power-of-ai-for-u-s-national-security/) confirms it contains additional directives for the intelligence community on AI adoption, testing, and governance in classified contexts.

### Civil Liberties Protections

A notable feature of the NSM is its explicit integration of civil liberties protections into national security AI use. This is significant because national security activities are often exempt from the consumer-protection frameworks that govern commercial AI use. The NSM and accompanying Framework [require agencies](https://www.cov.com/en/news-and-insights/insights/2024/11/white-house-issues-national-security-memorandum-on-artificial-intelligence-ai) to ensure privacy protections are implemented throughout the AI procurement process, mandate that AI governance boards include privacy and civil liberties officers, and establish accountability mechanisms for AI systems that affect individuals' rights.

### Relationship to the Mintz/Lexology Source Reporting

The source finding for this report — [a Mintz client alert published via Lexology](https://www.mintz.com/insights-center/viewpoints/54731/2024-10-31-biden-administration-issues-national-security) — provides accurate primary coverage of the NSM's issuance and connects it to the broader suite of Biden AI Executive Order implementation actions taking place in October–November 2024. The Mintz analysis situates the NSM within the final phase of EO 14110 implementation, noting the simultaneous release of OMB AI procurement guidance and the DOJ Civil Rights Division's interagency AI convening (see Related Reports).

## Policy Status Under the Trump Administration [MEDIUM confidence]

The NSM's policy status is materially affected by the January 2025 change in administration. On January 20, 2025, President Trump signed [Executive Order 14148, "Initial Rescissions of Harmful Executive Orders and Actions,"](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/01/28/2025-01901/initial-rescissions-of-harmful-executive-orders-and-actions) which revoked EO 14110 — the parent directive that mandated the NSM's creation. On January 23, 2025, Trump also signed [Executive Order 14179, "Removing Barriers to American Leadership in Artificial Intelligence,"](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/01/31/2025-02172/removing-barriers-to-american-leadership-in-artificial-intelligence) which directed agencies to review and suspend actions taken pursuant to EO 14110 that conflict with the new administration's AI policy priorities.

Legal analysts at [Hunton Andrews Kurth](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-information-security-law/the-impact-of-ai-executive-orders-revocation-remains-uncertain-but-new-trump-eo-points-to-path-forward) and elsewhere have noted that while the NSM's parent EO is revoked, the NSM itself — as a distinct presidential instrument — has not been explicitly rescinded by name. The practical effect is uncertain. Agency implementing regulations, working groups, and Chief AI Officer structures created pursuant to the NSM may have been suspended or informally discontinued by the Trump administration's AI action plan and the April 2025 OMB AI memoranda (M-25-21 and M-25-22), which rescind and replace Biden-era OMB AI guidance without explicitly addressing the NSM's classified annexes or national security testing directives.

The Institute for Law & AI has noted that [some national security AI testing infrastructure](https://law-ai.org/the-national-security-memo-on-ai-what-to-expect-in-trump-2-0/) — particularly AISI's role in classified model evaluations — may persist in modified form under the new administration, given bipartisan consensus on the need to assess AI risks to critical infrastructure, biological weapons programs, and offensive cyber capabilities.

## Impact Assessment [HIGH confidence]

### Affected Entities

The NSM directly governs federal agencies with national security missions, including DOD, the intelligence community, NSA, DOE, DHS, and the Department of State. Private-sector AI developers holding contracts with these agencies or participating in voluntary testing under AISI's program are indirectly affected.

### Compliance Requirements and Timelines

Key implementation timelines established by the NSM (measured from October 24, 2024):

| Deadline | Requirement | Lead Agency |
|---|---|---|
| 180 days (~Apr 22, 2025) | Issue guidance for AI developers on testing dual-use foundation models | DOC/AISI |
| 210 days (~May 22, 2025) | Develop roadmap for classified evaluations of chemical/biological AI risks | DOE, DHS, AISI |
| Ongoing | Establish Chief AI Officers and AI Governance Boards at covered agencies | All covered agencies |
| Ongoing | DOD/ODNI working group on AI procurement to submit FAR Council recommendations | DOD, ODNI |

Given EO 14110's revocation on January 20, 2025, the enforcement status of these deadlines is uncertain under the Trump administration.

### Industry and Research Sector Implications

For private-sector AI developers — particularly those building frontier models — the NSM's designation of AISI as the primary interlocutor for voluntary safety testing had concrete compliance implications during the Biden era. Developers working with the intelligence community or DOD supply chain faced new governance expectations around procurement, civil liberties review, and risk classification of high-impact AI use cases. The Trump administration's April 2025 OMB memos (M-25-21 and M-25-22) have substantially altered the procurement framework, and NIST's role — which was central to the NSM's safety evaluation infrastructure — has been [notably de-emphasized](https://www.globalpolicywatch.com/2025/04/omb-issues-first-trump-2-0-era-requirements-for-ai-use-and-procurement-by-federal-agencies/) in the successor guidance.

## Action Items

- Government contractors and AI developers working with DOD or IC agencies should audit their AI product governance documentation against both the Biden-era NSM framework and the successor Trump OMB memos (M-25-21, M-25-22) to understand the current governing requirements.
- Chief AI Officers and privacy/civil liberties officers designated under the NSM framework should confirm whether their agency has formally rescinded or maintained NSM-derived governance board structures under the Trump administration's review directives.
- Organizations performing AISI-sponsored voluntary testing or holding agreements with AISI should confirm the current status of those arrangements given NIST/AISI's reduced prominence in the successor OMB AI framework.
- Track the Trump administration's forthcoming AI Action Plan (directed by EO 14179) for any explicit treatment of the national security AI testing and governance infrastructure created by the Biden NSM.
- Legal counsel advising AI developers should note that the classified annex to the NSM, and any agency-internal procedures derived from it, are not publicly accessible and may require engagement with agency contracting officers to determine current applicability.
