# Bipartisan Senate AI Working Group Releases Policy Roadmap Calling for $32 Billion in R&D Funding

**Jurisdiction:** Federal | **Category:** ai-law | **Date:** 2024-05-15

## Executive Summary [HIGH confidence]

On May 15, 2024, the Bipartisan Senate AI Working Group — comprising Senate Majority Leader Chuck Schumer (D-NY), Senator Mike Rounds (R-SD), Senator Todd Young (R-IN), and Senator Martin Heinrich (D-NM) — released a 31-page policy document titled ["Driving U.S. Innovation in Artificial Intelligence: A Roadmap for Artificial Intelligence Policy in the United States Senate"](https://www.schumer.senate.gov/imo/media/doc/Roadmap_Electronic1.32pm.pdf). The roadmap followed nine first-of-their-kind AI Insight Forums held between September and December 2023, during which more than 150 experts from industry, academia, and civil society participated. The document identifies eight bipartisan priority areas for AI legislation — ranging from R&D funding and workforce training to election integrity, national security, and intellectual property — and directs individual Senate committees of jurisdiction to develop legislation in each area rather than pursuing an omnibus bill. The Working Group recommends emergency appropriations to reach $32 billion per year in non-defense AI R&D spending, a target first proposed by the National Security Commission on Artificial Intelligence (NSCAI) in 2021. Reaction to the roadmap was mixed: law firms and industry generally welcomed its innovation emphasis and bipartisan framing, while civil rights groups criticized it for prioritizing AI development over safety and protections against algorithmic harm.

## Background [HIGH confidence]

### The NSCAI Baseline

The $32 billion annual non-defense AI R&D target adopted by the roadmap originated with the [National Security Commission on Artificial Intelligence](https://www.nscai.gov), a body created by the John S. McCain National Defense Authorization Act for Fiscal Year 2019. The NSCAI's [final report, issued in March 2021](https://www.nscai.gov/2021-final-report/), was chaired by former Google CEO Eric Schmidt and called for doubling government AI spending annually to reach $32 billion by fiscal year 2026. The commission envisioned these funds flowing to multiple federal agencies, tripling the number of federally funded AI research institutes, and supporting individual researchers. As of the roadmap's release, Congress had not appropriated funds at that scale.

### Senate AI Insight Forums (2023)

In September 2023, [Leader Schumer convened the Senate's inaugural AI Insight Forum](https://www.mintz.com/insights-center/viewpoints/2191/2023-09-21-leader-schumer-holds-senates-inaugural-ai-insight-forum), launching what became nine total forums over the remainder of 2023. Topics covered at various forums included R&D funding and immigration challenges, workforce and high-impact AI applications, election integrity, and — in the final two forums in December 2023 — ["doomsday scenarios"](https://www.axios.com/pro/tech-policy/2023/12/05/senate-ai-forums-wrap-for-2023-this-week) (near- and long-term AI risks) and national security. In total, the forums drew over 150 participants, including representatives of industry, think tanks from across the political spectrum (including the Center for American Progress and the Heritage Foundation), labor unions, and civic engagement organizations. The forums were held off the record and included at least one classified all-senators briefing focused solely on AI — the first of its kind.

### Legislative Context

Prior to the roadmap's release, Congress had not passed comprehensive federal AI legislation. The [EU AI Act](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu) — a comprehensive, risk-tiered regulatory framework — was advancing toward enactment in Europe at the same time. In the US, the Biden administration had issued a sweeping [Executive Order on Safe, Secure, and Trustworthy AI](https://www.whitehouse.gov/briefing-room/presidential-actions/2023/10/30/executive-order-on-the-safe-secure-and-trustworthy-development-and-use-of-artificial-intelligence/) in October 2023, directing federal agencies to take numerous AI-related actions. Congress had considered but not passed significant AI bills; the roadmap was the Senate's most comprehensive legislative blueprint to that point.

## Detailed Analysis [HIGH confidence]

### Structure and Approach

The roadmap explicitly rejects the approach of a single omnibus AI bill. Instead, as [Majority Leader Schumer stated on the Senate floor](https://www.democrats.senate.gov/newsroom/press-releases/majority-leader-schumer-floor-remarks-on-the-release-of-the-roadmap-for-ai-policy-by-the-senate-bipartisan-senate-ai-working-group), the Senate will "review and consider legislation on a rolling basis as smaller bills arise," with individual committees of jurisdiction leading legislative development in their respective areas. The roadmap serves as a framework to guide — but not bind — those committees. According to multiple law firm analyses, this "committee-by-committee" approach was seen as the most politically viable path given the breadth of AI's impact across existing regulatory domains, including consumer protection, civil rights, national security, and intellectual property.

### The Eight Priority Areas

The roadmap organizes its recommendations into eight priority areas:

**1. Supporting U.S. Innovation in AI**

The Working Group recommends emergency appropriations to reach at least [$32 billion per year in non-defense AI R&D spending](https://www.nextgov.com/artificial-intelligence/2024/05/senate-ai-roadmap-calls-32-billion-year-ai-programs/396589/), consistent with the NSCAI 2021 recommendation. The roadmap specifies that these funds should go toward: AI and semiconductor R&D across federal agencies; outstanding programs under the [CHIPS and Science Act](https://www.congress.gov/bill/117th-congress/house-bill/4346); [NIST's U.S. AI Safety Institute](https://www.nist.gov/artificial-intelligence/executive-order-safe-secure-and-trustworthy-artificial-intelligence); the [National AI Research Resource (NAIRR)](https://new.nsf.gov/focus-areas/artificial-intelligence/nairr); and modernization of federal government IT infrastructure. The roadmap also calls for ensuring institutions of higher education and companies of varying sizes can compete in AI innovation.

**2. AI and the Workforce**

The roadmap calls for training programs to upskill workers for an AI-driven economy, and for policymaking processes that actively engage stakeholders on job displacement. The [Axios reporting](https://www.axios.com/pro/tech-policy/2023/10/31/inside-schumers-workforce-high-impact-ai-forums) from the forums noted that workforce concerns — including the pace and distributional effects of AI-driven displacement — were among the most contentious topics discussed.

**3. High-Impact Uses of AI**

The roadmap recognizes that certain AI applications in high-stakes domains — including healthcare, criminal justice, housing, and education — carry heightened risk of harm and warrant closer scrutiny. It endorses a risk-based framework with "heightened scrutiny for high-risk uses," encouraging committees to consider when a product release decision warrants regulatory review. The roadmap emphasizes the need for testing and evaluation standards calibrated to the stakes involved.

**4. Elections and Democracy**

The Working Group acknowledges the threat posed by deepfakes and other AI-generated synthetic content to electoral integrity. The roadmap's release coincided with the [Senate Rules Committee advancing three election-integrity bills](https://www.bhfs.com/insights/alerts-articles/2024/senate-ai-working-group-releases-landmark-policy-roadmap) introduced by Chair Amy Klobuchar (D-MN). The roadmap calls for addressing nonconsensual AI-generated content in political advertising.

**5. Privacy and Liability**

The roadmap calls for integration of AI regulation into existing privacy and consumer protection laws, and for committees to consider liability frameworks that appropriately assign responsibility across the AI development and deployment chain. It supports enforcement of existing civil rights laws that apply to AI-driven decisions, including in employment, lending, and housing contexts.

**6. Transparency, Explainability, Intellectual Property, and Copyright**

This priority area received significant attention from law firms. Key recommendations include: (a) legislation protecting against unauthorized commercial use of an individual's name, image, likeness, and voice; (b) legislation directing committees to review results from the [U.S. Copyright Office](https://www.copyright.gov/ai/) and [U.S. Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO)](https://www.uspto.gov/initiatives/artificial-intelligence) on AI's impact on IP law; (c) consideration of federal policy governing training data, including data containing sensitive personal information or copyrighted works; (d) evaluation of transparency requirements for training data; and (e) adoption of watermarking and other provenance tools to differentiate synthetic content from authentic content, which was "discussed at length" during the forums, according to the [Akin Gump analysis](https://www.akingump.com/en/insights/alerts/senate-gang-of-four-releases-long-anticipated-ai-whitepaper).

**7. Safeguarding Against AI Risks**

The Working Group endorses a "capabilities-focused, risk-based" governance regime, and calls for development of standard practices for risk testing and evaluation of AI models. The roadmap encourages committees to create structures for evaluating when a product release decision warrants regulatory scrutiny, consistent with the risk-tiered approach advocated in the EU AI Act and by NIST's AI Risk Management Framework. It also emphasizes the importance of preserving U.S. AI competitiveness while managing catastrophic risk — topics addressed in the final two forums in December 2023.

**8. National Security**

The roadmap devotes a full section to national security, acknowledging the unique considerations that arise when AI intersects with military capability, deterrence, intelligence, and defense procurement. It calls for advancing R&D at the intersection of AI and national security and industrial efficiency while maintaining appropriate safeguards. This section reflects the December 2023 classified all-senators briefing, the first such briefing focused exclusively on AI.

### Implementation Mechanism

The roadmap contains no binding legislative text, treaty obligations, or rulemaking directives. Its force is wholly political: it signals areas of bipartisan Senate consensus and instructs committees to proceed with legislative development. As [Mayer Brown's analysis](https://www.mayerbrown.com/en/insights/publications/2024/05/senate-ai-working-group-releases-roadmap-for-artificial-intelligence-policy) notes, the document's "clear statement that the respective committees of jurisdiction should develop legislation within their areas of expertise" effectively means the next phase involves individual committee deliberations rather than floor consideration of an omnibus bill.

## Impact Assessment [MEDIUM confidence]

### Industry and Innovation Stakeholders

For technology companies and AI developers, the roadmap was broadly favorable in framing. The emphasis on the $32 billion R&D investment and the committee-by-committee approach signals that Congress is unlikely to move quickly toward restrictive AI-specific regulation at the federal level. The [Davis Wright Tremaine analysis](https://www.dwt.com/blogs/artificial-intelligence-law-advisor/2024/05/bipartisan-senate-ai-policy-roadmap-released) characterized the roadmap as identifying "areas of consensus" rather than mandating new burdens on industry. The rejection of an omnibus approach was seen by many observers as favorable to the technology sector, which had lobbied against comprehensive prescriptive legislation.

### Civil Rights and Public Interest Organizations

Reaction from civil rights advocates was sharply critical. The [NAACP Legal Defense Fund](https://www.naacpldf.org/press-release/ldf-expresses-disappointment-in-senate-roadmap-on-ai-policy/) expressed disappointment, stating the roadmap "fails to offer substantive recommendations on or funding to address the heightened risk of algorithmic bias and other discriminatory practices that contribute to racial inequality." LDF and other groups noted that the $32 billion figure is overwhelmingly oriented toward AI development and competitive positioning, with minimal enforceable commitments to address civil rights harms. Critics also noted that the roadmap "fails to acknowledge the harms caused by government uses of AI, including the use of facial recognition and other technologies by law enforcement," per [TechPolicy.Press reporting](https://www.techpolicy.press/us-senate-ai-report-meets-mostly-disappointment-condemnation/).

### Legislative Outlook Post-Release

The committee-by-committee approach launched by the roadmap produced some early momentum. In the months following the release, both the Commerce and Homeland Security Committees marked up and approved bipartisan AI bills. Senator Klobuchar brought two bipartisan election-integrity bills to the Senate floor. However, per [Schumer's own assessment](https://www.democrats.senate.gov/newsroom/press-releases/majority-leader-schumer-floor-remarks-commending-efforts-to-advance-vital-bipartisan-artificial-intelligence-legislation-in-the-senate), much of the legislative work was expected to extend into the next Congress (i.e., the 119th Congress, beginning January 2025). The November 2024 elections and the change in administration under President Trump subsequently shifted the federal AI policy landscape significantly — with the Trump administration's December 2025 executive order on AI preemption of state laws representing a sharp departure from the regulatory posture contemplated by the roadmap.

### Affected Sectors

The roadmap's eight priority areas touch virtually every major industry. The most immediate practical implications at the time of release were for:
- **Technology companies:** Training data transparency, IP and copyright obligations for generative AI, and watermarking requirements are all areas of active federal legislative interest.
- **Healthcare, financial services, and employment:** High-impact AI applications in these sectors face heightened regulatory scrutiny under the roadmap's framework.
- **Election technology and political advertising vendors:** Deepfake and synthetic media disclosure requirements for political content are a near-term legislative priority.
- **Federal contractors:** NIST AI Safety Institute standards and federal IT modernization touch companies with government contracts.

## Action Items

- Monitor Senate committee legislative activity in the eight priority areas identified by the roadmap: the relevant committees include Commerce, Judiciary, Homeland Security, Rules, Armed Services, and HELP.
- Track the NAIRR and NIST AI Safety Institute funding appropriations process; the roadmap's $32 billion target remains aspirational and subject to annual appropriations cycles.
- Assess exposure to training data transparency requirements: companies using third-party or web-scraped datasets should review data provenance practices given likely legislative interest in this area.
- Review name, image, likeness, and voice (NILV) protections: legislative action in this area is an identified priority and will affect any application that uses AI to generate synthetic depictions of real individuals.
- Track the U.S. Copyright Office and USPTO AI-and-IP reports referenced in the roadmap; Congress is directed to take action "if necessary" following those reports, which have since been partially published.
- Review the Trump administration's December 2025 national AI policy framework to assess how subsequent executive-branch AI policy diverges from the Senate roadmap's approach, particularly on state preemption.
