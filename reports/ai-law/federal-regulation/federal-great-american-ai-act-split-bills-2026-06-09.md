# Sponsor of Great American AI Act Eyes Splitting Framework Into Multiple Bills

**Jurisdiction:** Federal | **Category:** AI Law | **Date:** June 9, 2026

## Executive Summary [MEDIUM confidence]

Representative Jay Obernolte (R-CA), a lead co-sponsor of the [Great American Artificial Intelligence Act of 2026 (GAAIA)](https://obernolte.house.gov/media/press-releases/obernolte-trahan-release-discussion-draft-great-american-ai-act), has indicated openness to disaggregating the 269-page comprehensive AI governance framework into multiple narrower, more targeted bills to improve the odds of passage. The remark reflects a shift in legislative strategy in the face of broad opposition from labor groups, AI safety organizations, and House Democratic leadership — opposition that emerged almost immediately after the June 4, 2026 discussion draft was released. Individual titles within the GAAIA, particularly those addressing frontier AI safety (Title I) and cybersecurity (Title III), were mentioned as potential standalone legislative vehicles. The development is significant because it signals that the comprehensive approach may give way to a piecemeal strategy — a pattern that has historically been more successful for complex technology regulation in Congress.

**Note on central claim sourcing:** The specific statement attributed to Obernolte regarding splitting the bill rests on a single subscription-gated source (IAPP Daily Dashboard, June 9, 2026) whose redirect URL is not independently retrievable. No secondary news source corroborating this specific statement was identified during research or reviewer verification. See "The Splitting Signal and Its Legislative Context" section for full sourcing discussion.

## Background [HIGH confidence]

### Legislative Timeline

Federal AI governance efforts in the 119th Congress have proceeded along two parallel tracks. In April 2026, Representatives Ted Lieu (D-CA) and Jay Obernolte (R-CA) introduced the [American Leadership in AI Act](https://lieu.house.gov/media-center/press-releases/reps-lieu-and-obernolte-introduce-bipartisan-bill-advance-american), a consolidation of over 20 bipartisan legislative proposals drawn from the Bipartisan Artificial Intelligence Task Force's recommendations. That bill covered six broad areas — AI standards and testing, research infrastructure, federal AI governance and procurement, workforce protection, deepfake deterrence, and AI education — but notably did not include a state preemption provision or binding obligations on frontier AI developers.

Separately, on June 4, 2026, Obernolte and Representative Lori Trahan (D-MA) released a [discussion draft of the GAAIA](https://obernolte.house.gov/media/press-releases/obernolte-trahan-release-discussion-draft-great-american-ai-act) — a more ambitious 269-page framework intended to be the first comprehensive federal AI governance statute. The GAAIA discussion draft was explicitly labeled a pre-introduction consultation document; sponsors invited public comments to GAAIA@mail.house.gov before formal introduction.

### GAAIA Structure and Key Provisions

The GAAIA is organized into four titles per [DLA Piper's analysis](https://www.dlapiper.com/en-us/insights/publications/2026/06/unpacking-the-great-american-ai-act):

- **Title I — Frontier AI Governance:** Imposes binding compliance obligations on "large frontier developers" (companies with over $500 million in annual revenue that have trained a frontier AI model). Requirements include: publishing a detailed "frontier AI framework" safety plan; semi-annual third-party audits conducted by licensed Independent Verification Organizations (IVOs) overseen by the Center for AI Standards and Innovation (CAISI); transparency reporting on model capabilities, intended uses, and risk assessments; reporting "critical safety incidents" to the CAISI director; and whistleblower protections for employees who report AI violations.

- **Title II — Workforce:** Directs NIST and the National Science Foundation to establish AI education grants and prizes; requires the Census Bureau and Bureau of Labor Statistics to update federal surveys to capture AI adoption data.

- **Title III — Cybersecurity:** Would reauthorize the Cybersecurity Act of 2015 through 2035 (incorporating existing information-sharing liability protections) and authorize CISA to award grants to maintainers of critical open-source software. The title incorporates text from prior bipartisan cybersecurity legislation.

- **Title IV — Research, Development, and International Cooperation:** Authorizes $100 million per fiscal year for CAISI for voluntary AI standards and guidelines; addresses federal AI research infrastructure and international competitiveness.

The bill's most controversial provision — a three-year moratorium on state laws specifically regulating the *development* of AI models — runs across Titles I through III. Per [Roll Call](https://rollcall.com/2026/06/04/bipartisan-ai-draft-proposes-three-year-preemption-of-state-laws/), states would retain authority to regulate AI *use* and *deployment* within their borders, but lose the ability to legislate how models are built during the preemption window.

This statement was made approximately five days after the discussion draft's release — during the period when public and institutional opposition was intensifying.

### Why Comprehensive AI Legislation Is Difficult to Pass [MEDIUM confidence]

The GAAIA faces structural legislative obstacles that help explain Obernolte's openness to disaggregation. Opposition came from multiple, ideologically distinct constituencies within days of the draft's release:

- **AI safety organizations:** Americans for Responsible Innovation and the Alliance for Secure AI both opposed the state preemption provision. Brad Carson, president of Americans for Responsible Innovation, characterized the preemption as "a generational mistake" that turns "the current floor on state AI legislation" into "a federal ceiling," per [TechTimes](https://www.techtimes.com/articles/317903/20260606/federal-ai-regulation-bill-freezes-state-consumer-protections-three-years-sparks-revolt.htm). The Alliance for Secure AI stated the bill "falls short of the framework necessary to keep Americans safe from the dangers of advanced AI."

- **Democratic leadership:** The House Democratic Commission on AI and the Innovation Economy released a statement that the GAAIA "does not meet the enormity of the moment," per [Transformer News](https://www.transformernews.ai/p/the-best-ai-bill-yet-may-not-get-obernolte-trahan-gaaia). Trahan's bipartisan collaboration with Obernolte also reportedly created friction with Democratic leadership pursuing a separate partisan approach.

- **Consumer and labor advocates:** [Public Citizen](https://www.citizen.org/news/obernolte-trahan-bill-strips-states-authority-to-protect-consumers-workers-and-children/) argued the bill strips states of authority to protect consumers, workers, and children. Labor unions urged Congress to reject the bill outright.

- **State officials:** A coalition of 36 state attorneys general had already written to Congress in November 2025 opposing any federal preemption of state AI laws. The GAAIA's three-year moratorium directly conflicts with that position.

- **Industry concerns:** The [Washington Examiner](https://www.washingtonexaminer.com/policy/technology/4604001/pitfalls-bipartisan-great-american-ai-act-regulations/) identified opposition from industry groups as well, noting that groups representing both the industry and civil society raised objections.

[Transformer News](https://www.transformernews.ai/p/the-best-ai-bill-yet-may-not-get-obernolte-trahan-gaaia) characterized the GAAIA as "the best, most serious AI safety bill yet" while noting its passage prospects are uncertain precisely because of the breadth of opposition.

### Historical Pattern: Piecemeal vs. Comprehensive Technology Legislation [MEDIUM confidence]

The strategic logic of breaking a comprehensive framework into individual bills is well established in US technology policy. Sponsors of the GAAIA themselves adopted this approach in April 2026 with the American Leadership in AI Act, which bundled over 20 existing bipartisan proposals. The GAAIA represents a more ambitious second attempt at comprehensiveness.

If Obernolte proceeds with disaggregation, the cybersecurity title (Title III) may be the most passable standalone vehicle: it largely extends existing statutes (the Cybersecurity Act of 2015 and existing CISA authorities) with bipartisan support on national security grounds. The frontier AI safety provisions in Title I are more politically contested because they impose binding obligations on major technology companies and include the preemption clause.

The [Cato Institute's analysis](https://www.cato.org/blog/there-finally-federal-ai-framework-what-obernolte-trahan-bill-means-ai-policy-debate) frames the GAAIA as the first serious attempt to create a US federal AI framework, noting it reflects a market-oriented philosophy — presume that general-purpose technology is free to use, then respond narrowly to specific targeted concerns — rather than a precautionary or risk-classification-based approach (as seen in the EU AI Act).

### Relationship to Executive Branch Activity [MEDIUM confidence]

Congress's comprehensive AI push is occurring alongside significant executive branch activity. On June 2, 2026, the Trump White House issued an AI innovation and security executive order, per existing knowledge base reporting. The White House National Policy Framework for AI (released in March 2026) also took a position on federal preemption of state AI laws. Congressional and executive activity may be moving in parallel or at cross-purposes; the executive branch has not publicly endorsed the GAAIA.

## Impact Assessment [MEDIUM confidence]

### If the GAAIA Passes as a Single Bill

Organizations that qualify as "large frontier developers" (annual revenue over $500 million; have trained a frontier AI model) — including OpenAI, Anthropic, Google DeepMind, Meta AI, and xAI — would face the most immediate compliance obligations: frontier AI framework publication, IVO auditing relationships, transparency reporting, and critical safety incident reporting protocols. States would lose the authority to regulate AI model development for three years. Smaller AI developers and deployers would be largely unaffected by Title I but may benefit from or be affected by workforce, cybersecurity, and research provisions.

### If the GAAIA Is Split into Standalone Bills

A frontier AI safety bill (derived from Title I) would impose the binding IVO audit regime and frontier AI framework requirements on large developers without requiring Congress to resolve the preemption fight simultaneously — potentially allowing the audit and transparency provisions to advance independently. A standalone cybersecurity bill (derived from Title III) would be largely uncontroversial and might advance quickly given bipartisan national security consensus. The state preemption provision is most tightly associated with Title I; removing it from the preemption fight could enable more targeted industry compliance obligations to pass.

### Monitoring Considerations

The GAAIA remains in discussion draft stage as of June 15, 2026. No formal introduction has occurred. The public comment period is ongoing (submissions to GAAIA@mail.house.gov). Obernolte's openness to splitting does not constitute a formal decision; the legislative trajectory remains fluid. Organizations with significant frontier AI development activity should track whether a standalone Title I bill is introduced and whether a preemption provision is retained in any successor vehicle.

**Summary:** A July 6, 2026 analysis by Covington & Burling confirms the Great American AI Act (GAAIA) discussion draft continues to face bipartisan opposition on the preemption question, but identifies a new fault line beyond the Democratic/state-authority objections already documented: many House Republicans and industry stakeholders now criticize the draft for not preempting enough state law, arguing its three-year moratorium on state AI development regulation leaves most state deployment- and application-level AI rules untouched, undermining the goal of a uniform national framework.
