# Trump Administration AI Policy at an Impasse: Three Factions Fight for Primacy After Canceled Executive Order

**Jurisdiction:** Federal | **Category:** AI Law | **Date:** 2026-06-01

## Summary [HIGH confidence]

Following President Trump's abrupt May 21, 2026 cancellation of a landmark AI cybersecurity executive order, [Politico reported](https://www.morninghoney.com/p/trump-pulled-back-from-signing-an-ai-executive-order-now-his-top-advisers-are-fighting-over-what-comes-next) that senior cabinet officials are now openly divided into three distinct camps on how the United States should govern AI risks. Negotiations are described as "back to square one," with each faction competing to shape the president's next move. The paralysis leaves the US without any binding federal framework governing advanced AI model deployment, even as frontier AI capabilities — including autonomous cyberattack tools — continue advancing.

## Key Facts [HIGH confidence]

- On May 21, 2026, President Trump canceled the signing of a planned executive order that would have created a voluntary 90-day pre-release review framework for frontier AI models, after last-minute lobbying by former AI czar David Sacks, Elon Musk, and Mark Zuckerberg. Trump told reporters he "didn't like certain aspects" and did not want to impede the US lead over China. ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/05/21/trump-ai-executive-order-postponed.html))
- Three administration factions have emerged with irreconcilable positions, per [Politico reporting relayed by Morning Honey](https://www.morninghoney.com/p/trump-pulled-back-from-signing-an-ai-executive-order-now-his-top-advisers-are-fighting-over-what-comes-next):
  - **Innovation camp** (David Sacks): Opposes any formal oversight mechanism; prioritizes outpacing China above all else. Sacks departed his formal White House role earlier in 2026 but retains direct presidential access. His view: even voluntary review could become a de facto permit regime abused by future administrations.
  - **Frontier safety camp** (Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth, Under Secretary of Defense for Research and Engineering Emil Michael): Supports tighter restrictions on frontier models, particularly those with offensive cybersecurity capabilities. Fears that regulatory sloppiness invites adversarial exploitation. Backdrop: Hegseth previously declared the Pentagon "will not employ AI models that won't allow you to fight wars," and the DoD gave Anthropic an ultimatum over use restrictions. ([CBS News](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/anthropic-pentagon-pete-hegseth-feud/))
  - **Voluntary transparency camp** (Chief of Staff Susie Wiles, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent): Seeks a middle path under which AI companies voluntarily provide the government a "first glance" at new models before release — not a licensing regime, but structured early access. Wiles stated the goal is to "ensure the best and safest tech is deployed rapidly to defeat any and all threats." ([The Hill](https://thehill.com/policy/technology/5868111-trump-administration-ai-winners-losers-susie-wiles/))
- The canceled order would also have established a Treasury-led cybersecurity clearinghouse to identify AI model vulnerabilities — a structural choice criticized by experts given Treasury's limited AI security expertise, with CISA sidelined due to a loss of roughly a third of its workforce since early 2025. ([Axios](https://www.axios.com/2026/05/26/cisa-white-house-cybersecurity-ai))
- NIST's Center for AI Standards and Innovation (CAISI) has completed voluntary pre-deployment evaluations for all five major US AI labs, including agreements with Google DeepMind, Microsoft, and xAI announced in May 2026. The CAISI program represents the most structured existing federal AI oversight mechanism, but is entirely voluntary and carries no legal force. ([Axios](https://www.axios.com/2026/05/13/ai-executive-action-white-house-infighting))
- The White House has stated it is "studying" revised approaches to AI security following the cancellation, with a revised draft expected by Q3 2026, but no firm timeline has been confirmed. ([The Decoder](https://the-decoder.com/trump-pulls-ai-safety-order-after-last-minute-calls-from-musk-zuckerberg-and-sacks/))
- The broader White House legislative vehicle — the [National Policy Framework for Artificial Intelligence](https://www.whitehouse.gov/releases/2026/03/president-donald-j-trump-unveils-national-ai-legislative-framework/) released March 20, 2026 — is non-binding and faces Democratic opposition and a razor-thin House majority, limiting near-term Congressional prospects for comprehensive AI legislation.

## Why It Matters [HIGH confidence]

**No federal AI governance framework exists.** The cancellation of the executive order leaves no statutory or executive instrument governing pre-release review of frontier AI models at the federal level. The CAISI voluntary program is the sole federal touchpoint, and it carries no binding obligations for AI developers. Organizations cannot calibrate compliance postures against non-existent requirements.

**The three-faction split signals sustained policy paralysis.** Each faction controls durable institutional resources: Sacks and industry allies have demonstrated the ability to block executive action through direct presidential access; Hegseth controls the largest federal AI budget and procurement relationships; Wiles and Bessent control White House staff processes and interagency coordination. The stalemate is structural, not merely a matter of drafting or negotiation.

**Voluntary mechanisms will likely fill the vacuum — temporarily.** The CAISI voluntary pre-deployment evaluation program, combined with informal ONCD engagement, represents the de facto federal AI governance infrastructure. AI developers that participate proactively position themselves to shape any formal successor framework. Those that do not risk being left out of policy design.

**Industry lobbying precedent is now entrenched.** The successful May 21 intervention by Sacks, Musk, and Zuckerberg establishes a clear precedent: a coalition of prominent AI developers can block federal AI safety action through direct presidential access. This lowers the probability that any future federal AI governance measure will advance without explicit industry consent — and concentrates that consent in a small number of large companies.

**State AI regulation will expand in the absence of federal action.** The March 2026 White House AI legislative framework sought federal preemption of state AI laws, but Congress has not acted. With federal authority in abeyance, states continue advancing their own AI legislation — Colorado's AI Act, Connecticut's SB5, and others — creating the compliance patchwork the administration sought to forestall.

## What To Watch [MEDIUM confidence]

- **Q3 2026 revised executive order draft:** The White House has indicated a revised order is in preparation. Watch for whether the Wiles/Bessent voluntary transparency model prevails or whether Sacks' deregulatory position remains dominant. The specific treatment of cybersecurity-capable frontier models (analogous to Anthropic's Claude Mythos) will be the key signal.
- **Congressional AI legislation:** The TRUMP AMERICA AI Act discussion draft (Sen. Blackburn, March 2026) and the bipartisan House AI preemption bill remain the most active legislative vehicles. Neither has advanced to committee markup as of June 2026.
- **CAISI voluntary program expansion:** Monitor whether CAISI agreements are extended to additional labs or whether participation thresholds are formalized. Any shift from voluntary to structured-voluntary (with defined timelines and scope) would signal movement toward a Wiles/Bessent-type framework.
- **DoD-industry AI tensions:** The Hegseth camp's pressure on AI developers for unrestricted military use rights — visible in the Pentagon-Anthropic dispute — may escalate as DoD AI procurement grows. Watch for contract award conditions or use-restriction policy changes from the Defense Department.
- **CISA reconstitution:** CISA's depleted capacity has been identified as a structural gap in any AI cybersecurity architecture. Congressional appropriations for CISA in FY2027 will indicate whether the agency is positioned to play the cybersecurity clearinghouse role the canceled order envisioned.

## Action Items

- **AI developers and deployers:** Engage CAISI voluntarily now. With no binding framework imminent, early participation in CAISI evaluations provides a track record that will carry weight when a formal framework eventually arrives. Document your engagement.
- **Compliance and legal teams:** Update AI governance risk assessments to reflect the absence of a federal frontier model review framework. Note that the CAISI program remains the operative federal touchpoint and that the regulatory environment is "back to square one" per administration officials.
- **Government contractors (AI):** Monitor DoD procurement conditions. The Hegseth camp's push for unrestricted AI use in military contexts may generate contract requirements that conflict with commercial AI providers' acceptable use policies — a conflict that arose sharply in the Anthropic-Pentagon dispute.
- **State compliance:** In the absence of federal preemption, maintain state-by-state AI compliance tracking. Colorado, Connecticut, and New York remain active legislative environments with distinct compliance requirements.
- **Policy engagement:** Trade associations and individual companies should engage both the ONCD and Congressional staff on the competing proposals. The faction fight means the final outcome is genuinely undetermined and susceptible to industry input — but the window for shaping the Q3 2026 revised draft is closing.
