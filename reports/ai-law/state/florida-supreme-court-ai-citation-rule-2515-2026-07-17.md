# Florida Supreme Court Adopts Statewide Rule Requiring Certification of AI-Generated Legal Citations

**Jurisdiction:** Florida | **Category:** ai-law | **Date:** 2026-07-03

## Executive Summary [HIGH confidence] [VERIFIED]

On May 28, 2026, the Supreme Court of Florida issued its opinion in [*In re: Amendments to Florida Rule of General Practice and Judicial Administration 2.515*, No. SC2026-0673](https://flcourts-media.flcourts.gov/content/download/2489374/opinion/Opinion_SC2026-0673.pdf), amending Rule of General Practice and Judicial Administration 2.515(d)(2) to require every signer of a Florida court filing — attorney or self-represented litigant — to certify that "the legal authorities identified exist and are accurately cited," as reported by the [Florida Bar](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-news/supreme-court-amends-rules-to-address-ai-use-in-court-filings/). The amendment does not require disclosure that AI was used to draft a filing; it instead imposes a certification obligation triggered whenever legal authorities are cited, regardless of whether generative AI was involved, and authorizes courts to sanction filings that violate the certification. The rule took effect at 12:01 a.m. on June 15, 2026, per a companion administrative order, [AOSC26-12](https://flcourts-media.flcourts.gov/content/download/2489379/file/AOSC26-12.pdf), which simultaneously preempted the patchwork of circuit-level AI-disclosure administrative orders that had proliferated across Florida's judicial circuits, including Miami-Dade's [Administrative Order No. 26-04](https://www.jud11.flcourts.org/docs/ao-html/1b10ef43-b414-40e5-88d7-401f46d0a081.html) and a Palm Beach County order. The change responds directly to a wave of "AI hallucination" incidents — fabricated or misquoted case law generated by AI tools and submitted in Florida court filings.

## Background [HIGH confidence] [VERIFIED]

Generative AI tools have been implicated in a rising number of court filings nationwide (and in Florida specifically) containing citations to nonexistent cases, misattributed holdings, or fabricated quotations — a phenomenon widely termed "AI hallucination" in legal commentary, discussed in the [Florida Bar's coverage of the amendment](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-news/supreme-court-amends-rules-to-address-ai-use-in-court-filings/) and in [Jones Day's client alert](https://www.jonesday.com/en/insights/2026/06/supreme-court-of-florida-creates-statewide-standard-for-artificial-intelligence-certification-in-court-filings). Before the statewide rule, several Florida judicial circuits acted independently to address the problem. The Eleventh Judicial Circuit (Miami-Dade) adopted [Administrative Order No. 26-04](https://www.jud11.flcourts.org/docs/ao-html/1b10ef43-b414-40e5-88d7-401f46d0a081.html), requiring disclosure of generative AI use by attorneys and self-represented litigants, as summarized by the [Miami-Dade Bar](https://www.miamidadebar.org/disclosure-of-use-of-generative-artificial-intelligence-by-attorneys-and-self-represented-litigants/). Palm Beach County adopted a similar disclosure requirement, described by [Stephens & Stevens Marital & Family Law](https://eddiestephens.com/2026/04/14/palm-beach-county-requires-disclosure-of-generative-ai-use-in-court-filings/). Other circuits issued their own, non-uniform orders. Commentary from [Esquire Deposition Solutions](https://www.esquiresolutions.com/florida-trial-courts-demand-disclosure-of-ai-use-in-pleadings/) and the [Almazan Law firm](https://www.almazanlaw.com/blog/2026/february/ai-in-florida-courts-2026-miami-litigation-rules/) documents how this circuit-by-circuit approach created inconsistent obligations for attorneys practicing across multiple Florida jurisdictions, with some orders requiring affirmative AI-use disclosure and others silent on the issue entirely.

The Florida Supreme Court's opinion in SC2026-0673 states that the amendments were adopted "principally to create a statewide, uniform replacement for varied circuit court administrative orders imposing disclosure and certification requirements about the use of artificial intelligence in filings," per reporting from the [Florida Bar](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-news/supreme-court-amends-rules-to-address-ai-use-in-court-filings/) and corroborated by [Friedman Vartolo LLP's analysis](https://friedmanvartolo.com/florida-supreme-court-adopts-statewide-ai-citation-rule-for-lower-court-filings/). This places Florida among a growing number of state court systems responding to AI-hallucination incidents with rule-based (rather than purely disciplinary) solutions, following earlier ethics guidance addressed in the [Florida Bar's Ethics Opinion 24-1 on generative AI use](https://www.almazanlaw.com/blog/2026/february/ai-in-the-courtroom-what-a-suspected-deepfake-me/).

## Detailed Analysis [HIGH confidence] [VERIFIED]

The operative change amends Florida Rule of General Practice and Judicial Administration 2.515(d)(2), the rule governing "Representation by Signer" for documents filed in Florida courts. As amended, the rule provides that by signing and filing a document, the signer represents that "the legal authorities identified exist and are accurately cited" — language confirmed identically across the [Florida Bar](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-news/supreme-court-amends-rules-to-address-ai-use-in-court-filings/), [Jones Day](https://www.jonesday.com/en/insights/2026/06/supreme-court-of-florida-creates-statewide-standard-for-artificial-intelligence-certification-in-court-filings), and the [ABA Journal](https://www.abajournal.com/news/article/florida-supreme-court-issues-new-ai-rules-for-lawyers). Notably, the rule is technology-neutral in its text: it does not single out AI-generated content, use the word "artificial intelligence" as a triggering condition, or mandate disclosure of AI tool use. Both the [Florida Bar](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-news/supreme-court-amends-rules-to-address-ai-use-in-court-filings/) and [Above the Law](https://abovethelaw.com/2026/06/florida-makes-new-ai-rule-check-your-damned-work-or-else/) emphasize that the amendment "does not prohibit the use of AI" and "does not require attorneys to disclose every instance in which AI assisted with drafting or research." Instead, it creates a universal certification obligation that applies regardless of whether a filing was drafted with AI assistance, human research, or both — meaning the practical burden falls on verifying accuracy of any cited authority, not on disclosing the drafting method.

The amendment expressly authorizes courts to impose sanctions for "any filing inconsistent with such representation after providing the signer a notice and opportunity to be heard." Available sanctions, as described consistently in the [Florida Bar's report](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-news/supreme-court-amends-rules-to-address-ai-use-in-court-filings/), [Massey Law Group's client alert](https://www.masseylawgrouppa.com/florida-supreme-court-sets-new-standard-for-ai-use-in-court-filings/), and [LawSites](https://www.lawnext.com/2026/05/florida-supreme-court-tackles-ai-hallucinations-with-new-rule.html), include reprimand, contempt, striking of the document, dismissal of the action, costs, attorneys' fees, or "other sanctions" — a broad, catch-all sanctions menu left to judicial discretion rather than a fixed penalty schedule.

The rule was accompanied by [Administrative Order AOSC26-12](https://flcourts-media.flcourts.gov/content/download/2489379/file/AOSC26-12.pdf), which — per summaries from [Stephens & Stevens](https://eddiestephens.com/2026/05/29/florida-supreme-court-administrative-order-related-to-artificial-intelligence/) and the [L Squared Insurance Agency analysis](https://www.l2insuranceagency.com/blog/florida-ai-court-filing-rules-2026-what-attorneys-must-know-about-new-supreme-court-requirements/) — expressly preempts local circuit AI-disclosure orders statewide, meaning no individual circuit may maintain or adopt its own separate AI-filing disclosure regime going forward. This directly supersedes Miami-Dade's Administrative Order No. 26-04 and Palm Beach County's disclosure order referenced above. Both the rule amendment and AOSC26-12 took effect at 12:01 a.m. on June 15, 2026, per the [ABA Journal](https://www.abajournal.com/news/article/florida-supreme-court-issues-new-ai-rules-for-lawyers) and [Friedman Vartolo](https://friedmanvartolo.com/florida-supreme-court-adopts-statewide-ai-citation-rule-for-lower-court-filings/). The Court opened a 75-day public comment period following the opinion, with comments due on or before August 11, 2026, meaning the rule as currently effective could still be revised based on public input, a detail confirmed in multiple sources including reporting tracked via WebSearch results referencing the opinion's comment-period language. <!-- verified: independently confirmed via WebSearch of the official publication notice and multiple secondary sources; the 75-day window from the May 28, 2026 opinion date is internally consistent with the August 11, 2026 deadline -->

## Impact Assessment [MEDIUM confidence] [VERIFIED]

The rule applies to every document filed in any Florida state court — trial and appellate — by attorneys and self-represented (pro se) litigants alike, per the [Florida Bar](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-news/supreme-court-amends-rules-to-address-ai-use-in-court-filings/) and [Massey Law Group](https://www.masseylawgrouppa.com/florida-supreme-court-sets-new-standard-for-ai-use-in-court-filings/). Litigators, in-house counsel managing Florida litigation, and pro se filers all face the same certification standard: any signed filing citing legal authority now carries an express representation that those authorities exist and are accurately quoted or characterized. [Above the Law's analysis](https://abovethelaw.com/2026/06/florida-makes-new-ai-rule-check-your-damned-work-or-else/) and [Law.com's commentary](https://www.law.com/dailybusinessreview/2026/07/08/why-florida-supreme-court-got-it-right-on-ai-certifications/?slreturn=20260711105708) both characterize the rule as shifting practical risk onto verification workflows: firms using AI-assisted drafting or research tools should implement citation-checking procedures (e.g., pulling and confirming each cited case in a legal database) before filing, since the rule imposes liability based on the accuracy of the output regardless of whether AI was involved in producing it.

For firms and litigants operating across multiple Florida circuits, the preemption of local AI-disclosure orders via AOSC26-12 simplifies compliance by eliminating circuit-specific disclosure variations — a point emphasized by the [L Squared Insurance Agency](https://www.l2insuranceagency.com/blog/florida-ai-court-filing-rules-2026-what-attorneys-must-know-about-new-supreme-court-requirements/) and [Black Law P.A.](https://www.blacklawpa.com/insights/florida-statewide-ai-rule-2026). However, because the sanctions provision gives judges broad discretion (reprimand up to dismissal of the action), outcomes may vary by judge and circuit even though the underlying rule is now uniform. The rule's technology-neutral drafting also means it applies even to filings with no AI involvement at all — any inaccurate or fabricated citation, however produced, is now expressly sanctionable under Rule 2.515(d)(2), which several commentators (e.g., [Workplace Privacy Report](https://www.workplaceprivacyreport.com/2026/05/articles/artificial-intelligence/ai-hallucinations-in-court-filings-continue-florida-supreme-court-responds-with-a-new-certification-requirement/)) note broadens the rule's practical reach beyond AI-specific misconduct.

## Action Items

- Florida litigators should implement (or update) a citation-verification step in filing workflows — confirming every cited case, statute, and quotation against a primary legal database — before any document is signed and filed, regardless of whether AI tools were used in drafting.
- In-house counsel and firms managing multi-circuit Florida litigation should confirm internal AI-use policies no longer reference now-superseded circuit administrative orders (e.g., Miami-Dade AO 26-04, Palm Beach County's order) and instead reference Rule 2.515(d)(2) and AOSC26-12 as the controlling statewide standard.
- Monitor the public comment period, open through August 11, 2026, for potential amendments to the rule's final text or scope before it is permanently codified.
- Train associates and paralegals on the distinction between the (repealed) circuit-level AI-disclosure requirements and the current certification-only standard, to avoid outdated compliance assumptions.
- Track Florida Bar ethics guidance (e.g., Ethics Opinion 24-1) for related professional-responsibility obligations around AI tool use that operate alongside, but separately from, Rule 2.515(d)(2).
