# Massachusetts AG Issues Advisory Applying Consumer Protection, Civil Rights, and Data Privacy Laws to Artificial Intelligence

**Jurisdiction:** Massachusetts | **Category:** AI Law / Enforcement | **Date:** April 16, 2024

## Executive Summary [HIGH confidence]

On April 16, 2024, Massachusetts Attorney General Andrea Joy Campbell issued an [Attorney General Advisory on the Application of the Commonwealth's Consumer Protection, Civil Rights, and Data Privacy Laws to Artificial Intelligence](https://www.mass.gov/doc/ago-ai-advisory-41624/download), making Massachusetts the first state AG's office in the country to issue targeted AI guidance of this kind. The advisory puts developers, suppliers, and users of AI systems on notice that Massachusetts's existing legal framework — including the Consumer Protection Act (Chapter 93A), anti-discrimination statutes (G.L. c. 151B), and data security standards (Chapter 93H) — applies to AI systems just as it does to any other technology. The office stated expressly that it intends to enforce these laws accordingly. The advisory is significant not because it creates new law but because it signals that the Massachusetts AG views AI as an enforcement priority under a broad set of existing statutes, covering AI use in consumer-facing products, employment, housing, credit, healthcare, and public benefits decisions. Less than fifteen months after the advisory's release, the office followed through with a [$2.5 million settlement with student loan lender Earnest Operations LLC](https://www.mass.gov/news/ag-campbell-announces-25-million-settlement-with-student-loan-lender-for-unlawful-practices-through-ai-use-other-consumer-protection-violations) over AI underwriting models that produced discriminatory results based on race and immigration status — confirming that the advisory was not merely symbolic.

## Background [HIGH confidence]

### Massachusetts Legal Framework Prior to the Advisory

Massachusetts has not enacted a standalone comprehensive AI statute. Instead, the Commonwealth's consumer protection, civil rights, and data security laws create an overlapping framework that the AG's office has now mapped onto AI development and deployment.

[Chapter 93A of the Massachusetts Consumer Protection Act](https://malegislature.gov/Laws/GeneralLaws/PartI/TitleXV/Chapter93A) is the Commonwealth's primary unfair and deceptive acts and practices (UDAP) statute. It applies broadly to "persons" conducting trade or commerce in Massachusetts and authorizes the AG to seek injunctive relief, civil penalties, and restitution. Chapter 93A's "unfair" prong is especially powerful: it reaches conduct that is not technically deceptive but that offends established public policy or causes substantial consumer harm. This breadth makes it a natural vehicle for AI enforcement.

[G.L. c. 151B, § 4](https://malegislature.gov/Laws/GeneralLaws/PartI/TitleXXI/Chapter151B/Section4) prohibits discrimination in employment, housing, public accommodations, and credit on the basis of protected characteristics including race, color, national origin, sex, age, disability, and others. The statute reaches not just intentional discrimination but practices with an unlawful disparate impact on protected groups.

[Chapter 93H](https://malegislature.gov/Laws/GeneralLaws/PartI/TitleXV/Chapter93H) and its implementing [201 CMR 17.00](https://www.mass.gov/regulations/201-CMR-17-standards-for-the-protection-of-personal-information-of-residents-of-the-commonwealth) (Standards for the Protection of Personal Information) impose substantive data security requirements on any entity that owns or licenses personal information about Massachusetts residents. Covered entities must maintain a written information security program (WISP) and comply with breach notification obligations.

### The State AG AI Enforcement Trend

The Massachusetts advisory did not emerge in a vacuum. In early 2024, a broader trend of state AGs using existing consumer protection authority to regulate AI was already underway. As [Foley Hoag's State AG Insights blog](https://foleyhoag.com/news-and-insights/blogs/state-ag-insights/2024/march/state-attorneys-general-take-action-on-artificial-intelligence/) documented in March 2024, state AGs were monitoring AI-related conduct and signaling enforcement intent under UDAP statutes, privacy laws, and civil rights frameworks — even in the absence of AI-specific legislation. The Massachusetts advisory built on this national momentum and took the additional step of issuing a formal written advisory, a more deliberate enforcement signal than informal statements or press releases.

The advisory also came against the backdrop of growing federal attention to AI risks. The Biden administration had issued its [Executive Order on the Safe, Secure, and Trustworthy Development and Use of Artificial Intelligence](https://www.whitehouse.gov/briefing-room/presidential-actions/2023/10/30/executive-order-on-the-safe-secure-and-trustworthy-development-and-use-of-artificial-intelligence/) in October 2023, and the FTC had signaled its own interest in AI-related deception and bias. State AGs, including AG Campbell, positioned their advisories as complementary to — and in some cases faster than — federal action.

## Detailed Analysis [HIGH confidence]

### Scope and Addressees

The advisory explicitly addresses three categories of actors in the AI ecosystem: **developers** (entities that build or train AI systems), **suppliers** (entities that license or distribute AI systems), and **users** (entities that deploy AI systems in their operations or products). This three-part scope is significant: it means that downstream deployers of off-the-shelf AI tools cannot avoid liability by pointing to the developer. Each link in the AI supply chain bears independent compliance obligations under Massachusetts law.

The advisory covers a wide range of AI system types, including:
- Algorithmic decision-making tools used in employment, housing, credit, and public benefits
- Generative AI systems (chatbots, text generators, image/video generators)
- AI-powered marketing and advertising systems
- Automated underwriting and scoring models
- AI diagnostic and clinical decision-support tools in healthcare

### Consumer Protection: Chapter 93A

The advisory identifies several categories of AI conduct that constitute unfair or deceptive practices under Chapter 93A:

**Deceptive marketing and advertising of AI systems.** It is unlawful to falsely advertise the quality, value, or usability of an AI system; supply an AI system that is defective, unusable, or impractical for the advertised purpose; or misrepresent the reliability, manner of performance, safety, or condition of an AI system — including making false statements that a system is "free from bias."

**Deepfakes, voice cloning, and chatbot-facilitated fraud.** The advisory specifically flags AI-generated audio and video content used to deceive consumers into engaging in business transactions or surrendering personal information as if to a trusted business partner. Chatbots used to perpetrate scams or to surreptitiously collect sensitive personal data are expressly identified as potential Chapter 93A violations.

**Generative AI content misrepresentation.** Misrepresenting AI-generated content as human-generated in commercial contexts, or deploying AI in ways that mislead consumers about the nature of the interaction (e.g., an AI chatbot posing as a human customer service representative without disclosure), are flagged as potentially deceptive.

**Credit and financial services.** The advisory notes that creditors using AI systems to make credit decisions remain bound by the federal Equal Credit Opportunity Act's requirement to provide specific and accurate reasons for adverse actions — a requirement the advisory suggests AI opacity can make difficult to satisfy.

### Anti-Discrimination: G.L. c. 151B and Related Statutes

The advisory adopts a broad interpretation of AI-related discrimination liability. Under G.L. c. 151B, it is unlawful for developers, suppliers, or users of AI systems to deploy technology that discriminates against Massachusetts residents on the basis of a legally protected characteristic, including where:

- The AI system **uses discriminatory inputs** (e.g., training data that encodes historical bias, proxy variables that correlate with protected characteristics);
- The AI system **produces discriminatory results**, even without discriminatory intent — that is, even facially neutral AI systems can violate Massachusetts law if their outputs have a disparate impact on protected groups.

The advisory calls out high-stakes decision domains as particular areas of concern: housing (AI recommendation models for rentals and sales), credit and lending (automated underwriting), employment (algorithmic hiring, screening, and performance evaluation), and access to public benefits. These are areas where AI decision-making "stands between consumers and their ability to secure housing, credit and public benefits, among other life essentials."

Healthcare is a further area of concern. The [Verrill Law analysis](https://www.verrill-law.com/news/massachusetts-attorney-general-artificial-intelligence-guidance-what-healthcare-providers-and-health-it-developers-need-to-know/) of the advisory noted that healthcare providers and health IT developers using AI diagnostic tools, clinical decision-support systems, or patient triage algorithms face anti-discrimination exposure if those systems produce disparate results based on race, sex, disability, or other protected characteristics.

### Data Security: Chapter 93H and 201 CMR 17.00

The advisory makes clear that the Commonwealth's data security framework — including 201 CMR 17.00's written information security program (WISP) requirements and Chapter 93H's breach notification obligations — applies to personal information processed by AI systems. Specific obligations include:

- Developers, suppliers, and users of AI systems that process personal information about Massachusetts residents must implement reasonable security measures proportionate to the sensitivity and volume of the data.
- Breach notification requirements under Chapter 93H apply when an AI system is compromised in a way that results in unauthorized access to personal information.
- Entities must assess whether their AI systems introduce new attack vectors or data exposure risks that require updates to existing WISPs.

The advisory does not impose new AI-specific data security standards, but it signals that standard data security practices — security risk assessments, access controls, encryption, vendor oversight — must account for the unique risks that AI systems (including large language models and machine learning pipelines) introduce.

## Impact Assessment [HIGH confidence]

### Who Is Affected

The advisory's breadth — covering developers, suppliers, and users across the AI supply chain — means that virtually any Massachusetts business (or out-of-state business with Massachusetts customers or employees) that develops, sells, or deploys AI systems faces potential enforcement exposure. Key affected sectors include:

- **Financial services and fintech:** Lenders, insurers, and credit reporting entities using AI for underwriting, scoring, or customer service face the highest scrutiny, as confirmed by the 2025 Earnest settlement.
- **Employers:** Businesses using AI for hiring, performance evaluation, or termination decisions face anti-discrimination exposure. The advisory's disparate impact framework means that even facially neutral AI hiring tools require fairness testing.
- **Real estate and housing:** AI-powered rental listing platforms, property recommendation engines, and tenant screening tools must be evaluated for discriminatory outputs.
- **Healthcare:** Providers and health IT developers using AI diagnostics or clinical decision support must assess bias risks under both state law and federal anti-discrimination frameworks (Section 1557 of the ACA).
- **Retail and consumer technology:** Businesses using AI for customer segmentation, personalized pricing, or targeted advertising face Chapter 93A scrutiny for deceptive or manipulative AI practices.

### Enforcement Signals

The advisory explicitly states that the AG "intends to enforce" Massachusetts law against AI-related conduct. Several enforcement signals are worth noting:

1. **The 2025 Earnest settlement confirms the advisory was not bluster.** The [AG's $2.5 million settlement with Earnest Operations LLC](https://www.mass.gov/news/ag-campbell-announces-25-million-settlement-with-student-loan-lender-for-unlawful-practices-through-ai-use-other-consumer-protection-violations) — announced July 10, 2025 — is a direct enforcement action under the framework the advisory described. Earnest's AI underwriting model used a Cohort Default Rate variable that produced disparate impact against Black and Hispanic applicants, and the company failed to test its models for bias. The settlement required $2.5 million in payments, elimination of the discriminatory variables, annual fair lending testing, and a corporate AI governance structure.

2. **Parallel litigation by AG Campbell.** The Massachusetts SJC's April 2026 ruling in [*Commonwealth v. Meta Platforms, Inc.*](https://www.mass.gov/news/ag-campbell-issues-advisory-providing-guidance-on-how-state-consumer-protection-and-other-laws-apply-to-artificial-intelligence) allowing AG Campbell's Chapter 93A suit against Meta over addictive design to proceed demonstrates Campbell's willingness to use consumer protection law aggressively against technology companies — and her office's institutional appetite for tech-sector enforcement.

3. **The national AG coalition.** A bipartisan coalition of 36 state AGs, coordinated through [NAAG](https://www.naag.org/press-releases/bipartisan-coalition-of-36-state-attorneys-general-opposes-federal-ban-on-state-ai-laws/), has formally opposed federal preemption of state AI laws, signaling that state enforcement — including by Massachusetts — is expected to intensify, not recede, as federal AI policy develops.

### Comparison with Other State AG AI Actions [MEDIUM confidence]

Massachusetts was the first state AG to issue a formal written advisory mapping existing law to AI, but several other states have since followed similar approaches:

- **Colorado:** The [Colorado AI Act (SB 24-205)](https://leg.colorado.gov/bills/sb24-205) — enacted May 2024 — takes a more structured statutory approach, requiring deployers of high-risk AI systems to conduct impact assessments and provide consumer disclosures. Colorado's law is more prescriptive than Massachusetts's advisory but narrower in scope (limited to "high-risk" AI). Enforcement has been [delayed until June 30, 2026](reports/ai-law/state-legislation/colorado-ai-act-enforcement-delayed-2026-04-13.md).
- **California:** California's approach is fragmented across multiple agencies and statutes — the CPPA's automated decision-making rules, AB 2013 (AI training data transparency), the AI Transparency Act — but does not include a comparable AG-issued enforcement advisory. California's AG has broad CCPA and UCL enforcement authority that could be mobilized similarly.
- **Texas:** The Texas AG has brought enforcement actions under the Texas Data Privacy and Security Act (TDPSA), but Texas has not issued a comparable AI-specific advisory. The Texas House Select Committee on AI and Emerging Technology has been studying the issue.
- **Multi-state coordination:** The [NAAG letter](https://www.naag.org/press-releases/bipartisan-coalition-of-36-state-attorneys-general-opposes-federal-ban-on-state-ai-laws/) opposing federal preemption of state AI laws signals that state AGs view their existing enforcement authority as essential and non-negotiable — a posture that makes multi-state AI enforcement investigations increasingly likely.

The Massachusetts advisory's use of general-purpose UDAP and anti-discrimination statutes, rather than waiting for new AI-specific legislation, represents a strategic choice that other AGs are increasingly emulating. As [Morgan Lewis noted in April 2026](https://www.morganlewis.com/pubs/2026/04/ai-enforcement-accelerates-as-federal-policy-stalls-and-states-step-in), "AI enforcement accelerates as federal policy stalls and states step in" — a dynamic that directly extends the Massachusetts model nationally.

## Action Items

- **AI vendors and deployers:** Conduct a legal mapping of AI systems against Massachusetts Chapter 93A, G.L. c. 151B, and Chapter 93H obligations. Document this mapping as evidence of compliance diligence.
- **Bias and fairness testing:** Implement statistical disparate impact testing for any AI system used in high-stakes decisions (employment, lending, housing, insurance, healthcare). Retain testing records. The Earnest settlement requires annual fair lending testing — treat this as a compliance floor.
- **Marketing and advertising:** Audit all marketing claims about AI systems for accuracy. Remove unsubstantiated claims of bias-free performance. Ensure AI-generated content in consumer-facing contexts is disclosed as such.
- **Chatbot and generative AI disclosures:** Review customer-facing chatbot deployments. Implement clear disclosures where AI may be mistaken for a human. Assess whether AI systems could be used to deceive consumers in ways that implicate Chapter 93A.
- **Data security updates:** Review and update written information security programs (WISPs) to address AI-specific risks, including training data handling, model access controls, and breach scenarios involving AI system compromise.
- **Vendor contracts:** Ensure AI vendor agreements include representations about bias testing, compliance with anti-discrimination law, and data security standards. The advisory's coverage of all three layers (developer/supplier/user) means deployers cannot outsource compliance to vendors.
- **Ongoing monitoring:** Track Massachusetts AG enforcement actions in the AI space — particularly in financial services, employment, and healthcare — as leading indicators of future enforcement priorities. Monitor NAAG coordination on multistate AI investigations.
