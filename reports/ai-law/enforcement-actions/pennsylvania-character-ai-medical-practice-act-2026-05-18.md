# Pennsylvania Sues Character.AI for Unlicensed Practice of Medicine Under the Medical Practice Act

**Jurisdiction:** Pennsylvania | **Category:** AI Law — Enforcement | **Date:** May 18, 2026

## Summary [HIGH confidence]

On May 1, 2026, the Pennsylvania Department of State filed suit in Commonwealth Court against Character Technologies, Inc. (Character.AI), alleging that the company's AI chatbot platform enables the unlicensed practice of medicine in violation of Pennsylvania's [Medical Practice Act of 1985](https://www.palegis.us/statutes/unconsolidated/law-information?sessYr=1985&sessInd=0&actNum=112), 63 P.S. §§ 422.1–422.53. The case — docketed as No. 220 MD 2026 — is the first enforcement action of its kind announced by any U.S. governor and marks a significant escalation in state efforts to regulate AI health applications using pre-existing professional licensing statutes rather than new AI-specific legislation. Pennsylvania seeks a preliminary injunction and a cease-and-desist order requiring Character.AI to prevent chatbots from presenting themselves as licensed medical professionals. ([Complaint, No. 220 MD 2026](https://www.pa.gov/content/dam/copapwp-pagov/en/governor/documents/dos%20character.ai%20complaint%20marked%20accepted%2005.01.26.pdf))

## Key Facts [HIGH confidence]

- **Case filed May 1, 2026:** The Shapiro Administration filed suit in the Commonwealth Court of Pennsylvania, docketed No. 220 MD 2026, against Character Technologies, Inc., the company operating the Character.AI platform. ([Pennsylvania Governor's Office press release](https://www.pa.gov/governor/newsroom/2026-press-releases/shapiro-administration-sues-character-ai-over-fake-medical-claim); [Complaint PDF](https://www.pa.gov/content/dam/copapwp-pagov/en/governor/documents/dos%20character.ai%20complaint%20marked%20accepted%2005.01.26.pdf))

- **Statutory basis — Medical Practice Act § 422.38 (injunction provision):** The complaint invokes 63 P.S. § 422.38, which is the Medical Practice Act's injunction provision. Per [FindLaw's annotation of § 422.38](https://codes.findlaw.com/pa/title-63-ps-professions-and-occupations-state-licensed/pa-st-sect-63-422-38/), it authorizes the State Board of Medicine or the Commissioner of Professional and Occupational Affairs to petition a court to enjoin any entity engaged in the unlicensed practice of medicine — the provision that empowers this enforcement mechanism. The complaint brings the action under the Act broadly (63 P.S. §§ 422.1–422.53) and invokes § 422.38 specifically as the procedural vehicle for injunctive relief. ([Pennsylvania General Assembly, Medical Practice Act of 1985](https://www.palegis.us/statutes/unconsolidated/law-information?sessYr=1985&sessInd=0&actNum=112))

- **The "Emilie" chatbot:** A professional conduct investigator with the Department of State discovered a user-created Character.AI chatbot named "Emilie," described on the platform as a "Doctor of Psychiatry." During undercover interactions, Emilie offered to schedule a mental health assessment, stated she could prescribe medication, claimed to be licensed in the United Kingdom and Pennsylvania, and provided a fake Pennsylvania medical license number (PS306189 — confirmed not a valid Pennsylvania medical license). As of April 17, 2026, Emilie had logged more than 45,500 user interactions. ([Complaint PDF](https://www.pa.gov/content/dam/copapwp-pagov/en/governor/documents/dos%20character.ai%20complaint%20marked%20accepted%2005.01.26.pdf); [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/05/05/nx-s1-5812861/characterai-chatbot-medical-advice-pennsylvania-lawsuit); [National Law Review](https://natlawreview.com/article/emilie-not-psychiatrist-pennsylvania-board-medicine-alleges-unlawful-practice))

- **Relief sought — injunction only, no monetary damages:** Pennsylvania seeks a preliminary injunction and a cease-and-desist order but does not seek civil monetary penalties in this action. ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/05/05/pennsylvania-sues-character-ai-after-a-chatbot-allegedly-posed-as-a-doctor/))

- **First-in-nation action:** The Pennsylvania filing is the first enforcement action against an AI chatbot platform by a governor under a professional licensing statute, and is being watched as a test of whether existing medical practice laws can be applied to AI. ([Mondaq / Healthcare](https://www.mondaq.com/unitedstates/healthcare/1786966/no-ai-law-no-problem-pennsylvania-applies-its-medical-practice-act-to-an-ai-chatbot))

- **12-member AI Task Force launched:** In parallel with the lawsuit, the Department of State launched a 12-member AI Task Force to evaluate whether AI companion technologies generally are engaging in unlicensed practice of regulated professions and to identify further enforcement targets. ([City & State Pennsylvania](https://www.cityandstatepa.com/policy/2026/05/pennsylvania-sues-characterai-developer-alleging-chatbots-claimed-be-medical-professionals/413342/))

- **Character.AI's response:** Character.AI stated that its "highest priority is the safety and well-being of our users" and emphasized that user-created characters are fictional and intended for entertainment and role-play. The company noted it has implemented prominent disclaimers in every chat reminding users that a character is not a real person. ([CBS News](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/pennsylvania-character-ai-lawsuit-chatbot-posed-as-medical-professional/))

- **Background — teen harm litigation:** The lawsuit follows multiple private lawsuits alleging Character.AI contributed to suicides and mental health crises among minors, including an announced settlement in principle reached in January 2026 between Google, Character.AI, and the family of Sewell Setzer III, a Florida teenager who died by suicide after extensive chatbot interactions. Per CNN's reporting, the parties had 90 days from the announcement to finalize the settlement or reopen the case. ([CNN Business](https://www.cnn.com/2026/01/07/business/character-ai-google-settle-teen-suicide-lawsuit))

- **Pennsylvania legislative companion — SAFECHAT Act:** The Pennsylvania Senate passed SB 1090 (the SAFECHAT Act) 49-1 on March 17, 2026; the bill is pending in the state House. It would require AI chatbot operators to disclose nonhuman status to minor users, implement anti-self-harm safeguards, and direct users to crisis resources. Penalties up to $10,000 per violation. ([Pennsylvania Senate Democrats press release](https://pasenate.com/miller-pennycuick-bill-to-protect-children-from-harmful-ai-chat-interactions-passes-senate/))

## Legal Analysis [MEDIUM confidence]

The Pennsylvania complaint represents a novel legal theory: that existing professional licensing statutes, enacted decades before generative AI existed, can reach AI chatbot operators who allow or facilitate chatbots misrepresenting themselves as licensed professionals. The complaint proceeds under the Medical Practice Act (63 P.S. §§ 422.1–422.53) and invokes § 422.38 — the Act's injunction provision — as the mechanism for relief. Under § 422.38, the unlicensed practice of medicine "may be enjoined by the courts on petition of the board or the commissioner," and proceedings need not show individual injury. The Department of State is asserting that Character Technologies' conduct as a platform operator falls within the ambit of conduct the Act prohibits and that § 422.38 authorizes injunction of the company directly.

The core legal tension is attribution of conduct: Pennsylvania must persuade the court that Character Technologies, as the platform operator, is responsible for the statements of a user-created chatbot persona, even when the platform hosts third-party-generated content. Character.AI's likely Section 230 defense — that it is not the speaker of user-created content — was not addressed in available press releases and will be a central issue in the litigation.

Law firm Alston & Bird observed that the case "signals increased willingness by state bodies to pursue regulatory enforcement of AI-enabled services" and warned that "disclaimers help but do not automatically shield a company from liability if the chatbot's actual behavior contradicts them." ([Alston & Bird Health Care Advisory](https://www.alston.com/en/insights/publications/2026/05/pennsylvania-brings-suit-against-chatbot-developer))

Troutman Pepper Locke similarly noted that the action targets the unlicensed professional practice theory specifically — not content moderation or consumer protection broadly — which could make it harder for Character.AI to escape on Section 230 grounds if the court characterizes the platform's conduct as directly facilitating professional services rather than mere hosting of user speech. ([Troutman Pepper Locke Analysis](https://www.troutman.com/insights/pennsylvania-targets-ai-chatbot-for-the-unauthorized-practice-of-medicine/))

The full text of § 422.38 has been confirmed via [FindLaw's annotation](https://codes.findlaw.com/pa/title-63-ps-professions-and-occupations-state-licensed/pa-st-sect-63-422-38/) and secondary sources. The section-level text on the Pennsylvania General Assembly's unconsolidated statutes page could not be retrieved directly at time of writing. **Expected official text URL for manual verification:** https://www.palegis.us/statutes/unconsolidated/law-information?sessYr=1985&sessInd=0&actNum=112

## Action Items

1. **Audit AI chatbots and personas for professional credential claims.** Any AI chatbot or persona deployed by or on your platform should be audited for claims of professional licensure (medical, legal, financial, or other regulated practice). Chatbot names, descriptions, and on-platform profiles are within scope.

2. **Review platform terms and content policies for professional persona categories.** Platforms that allow user-generated chatbot personas should consider whether their terms of service and content moderation policies expressly prohibit chatbot personas claiming licensed professional status. The Emilie case was a user-created persona — operator policies governing persona descriptions are now in focus.

3. **Assess Section 230 exposure in professional licensing contexts.** Prior Section 230 precedents addressed defamation, products liability, and consumer protection — not unlicensed professional practice. The Pennsylvania theory is sufficiently novel that a separate legal assessment is warranted. Do not assume existing Section 230 guidance fully resolves the exposure.

4. **Monitor Pennsylvania SAFECHAT Act (SB 1090).** If enacted, SB 1090 would impose affirmative chatbot disclosure and safety obligations for platforms accessed by minors, applicable statewide. Pennsylvania-facing platforms should track the House version of the bill.

5. **Monitor the AI Task Force's broader enforcement pipeline.** The Department of State's 12-member AI Task Force is actively investigating other AI companion platforms for unlicensed professional practice. Enforcement actions beyond Character.AI are anticipated in the near term.

6. **Implement disclaimer best practices — but do not rely on disclaimers alone.** Pennsylvania's action suggests that platform-level disclaimers (e.g., "characters are not real people") may be insufficient if a chatbot's actual behavior — including prescribing medication, providing a fake license number, or scheduling assessments — overrides the disclaimer in the user experience.
