# Massachusetts AG AI Advisory: Compliance Obligations for Businesses Under Consumer Protection, Civil Rights, and Data Privacy Laws

**Jurisdiction:** Massachusetts | **Category:** AI Law / Guidance | **Date:** May 15, 2024

## Executive Summary [HIGH confidence]

On April 16, 2024, Massachusetts Attorney General Andrea Joy Campbell issued an [Attorney General Advisory on the Application of the Commonwealth's Consumer Protection, Civil Rights, and Data Privacy Laws to Artificial Intelligence](https://www.mass.gov/doc/ago-ai-advisory-41624/download), making Massachusetts the first state to issue targeted AG-level AI compliance guidance of this kind. The advisory — which drew analysis from law firms across the advertising, financial services, healthcare, and private equity sectors — applies to a wide range of entities: developers, suppliers, and users of AI systems. It does not create new law but clarifies that three established Massachusetts legal frameworks already govern AI: Chapter 93A (unfair and deceptive trade practices), state anti-discrimination statutes, and Chapter 93H (data security). Regulated businesses operating in Massachusetts or handling Massachusetts residents' personal data should treat this advisory as an enforcement signal; the AG's office subsequently settled a [$2.5 million action against a student loan lender](https://www.mass.gov/news/ag-campbell-announces-25-million-settlement-with-student-loan-lender-for-unlawful-practices-through-ai-use-other-consumer-protection-violations) whose AI underwriting model produced discriminatory results, confirming that the advisory was not merely hortatory.

## Background [HIGH confidence]

### The Massachusetts Legal Framework

Massachusetts has not enacted a standalone comprehensive AI statute. Instead, the Commonwealth operates through three overlapping legal regimes that, combined, create broad coverage of AI development and deployment:

1. **[Chapter 93A — Massachusetts Consumer Protection Act](https://malegislature.gov/Laws/GeneralLaws/PartI/TitleXV/Chapter93A):** The Commonwealth's primary unfair and deceptive acts and practices (UDAP) statute, applying to persons conducting "trade or commerce" in Massachusetts. The AG may seek injunctive relief, civil penalties, and restitution. Chapter 93A's "unfair" prong — which does not require proof of deception — is particularly powerful and can reach AI conduct that causes substantial consumer harm even without an affirmative misrepresentation.

2. **[G.L. c. 151B — Anti-Discrimination Law](https://malegislature.gov/Laws/GeneralLaws/PartI/TitleXXI/Chapter151B):** Prohibits discrimination on the basis of legally protected characteristics in employment, housing, places of public accommodation, and credit. The advisory clarifies that AI systems used in algorithmic decision-making that rely on discriminatory inputs or produce discriminatory outputs are subject to enforcement under this statute.

3. **[Chapter 93H — Standards for the Protection of Personal Information](https://malegislature.gov/Laws/GeneralLaws/PartI/TitleXV/Chapter93H) and the [201 CMR 17.00 Regulations](https://www.mass.gov/regulations/201-CMR-1700-standards-for-the-protection-of-personal-information-of-residents-of-the):** Massachusetts data security requirements applicable to entities that own or license personal information of state residents. Violations of Chapter 93H are expressly enforceable under Chapter 93A, meaning data security failures by AI systems can be aggregated into consumer protection claims.

### Context: State AG Activity on AI Prior to April 2024

By April 2024, Massachusetts was entering an already-active field of state AG enforcement interest in AI. [Foley Hoag's State AG Insights blog](https://foleyhoag.com/news-and-insights/blogs/state-ag-insights/2024/march/state-attorneys-general-take-action-on-artificial-intelligence/) noted that state AGs had been escalating attention to AI-related consumer protection issues throughout 2023 and early 2024. The Massachusetts advisory was, however, the most systematic and sector-spanning guidance issued by any state AG's office to that point, and it served as a model for guidance subsequently issued by [New Jersey](https://www.nj.gov/oag/newsreleases24/2024-AI-Guidance_Final.pdf) and [Oregon](https://www.doj.state.or.us/oregon-department-of-justice/news-releases/attorney-general-rosenblum-issues-ai-guidance-for-businesses/).

## Detailed Analysis [HIGH confidence]

### Scope of the Advisory: Who Is Covered

The advisory is expressly addressed to three categories of actors in the AI supply chain:

- **Developers:** Entities that design, build, or train AI systems.
- **Suppliers:** Entities that distribute, license, or sell AI systems to others.
- **Users:** Entities that deploy AI systems in consumer-facing products, operational decisions, or business processes.

The AG's framing is functionally comprehensive: any organization that touches an AI system in a commercial context in Massachusetts falls within at least one of these categories. The advisory does not establish minimum thresholds for coverage based on revenue, number of employees, or size of AI deployment.

### Consumer Protection: Chapter 93A Applications to AI

The advisory presents a non-exhaustive list of conduct that the AG's office views as potentially unfair or deceptive under Chapter 93A:

- **False advertising** of the quality, value, or usability of an AI system.
- **Supply of defective AI:** Providing an AI system that is defective, unusable, or impractical for the advertised purpose.
- **Misrepresentation of reliability or safety:** Overstating accuracy rates, error rates, or safety characteristics of an AI model.
- **Warranty violations:** Offering an AI system for sale in breach of express or implied warranties, including implied merchantability.
- **Deepfakes and synthetic media:** Using multimedia content — including deepfakes, voice cloning, and AI chatbots — to impersonate or deceive.
- **Chatbot-enabled fraud:** Operating AI chatbots as instruments of consumer fraud or deception.

The advertising sector implications are notable: [Orrick's analysis](https://www.orrick.com/en/Insights/2024/04/Massachusetts-Attorney-General-Shares-Artificial-Intelligence-Guidance-What-Businesses-Need-to-Know) observes that companies using generative AI for advertising content — product claims, customer service chatbots, and AI-generated marketing materials — must ensure those uses do not constitute unfair or deceptive trade practices, which includes ensuring AI-generated content is accurate and that AI tools are not deployed in ways that mislead consumers.

### Anti-Discrimination: Algorithmic Accountability Under G.L. c. 151B

The advisory addresses AI-powered decision-making in high-stakes domains — employment, housing, credit, healthcare, and public benefits — as areas of particular enforcement concern. The advisory states that Massachusetts' anti-discrimination law prohibits AI developers, suppliers, and users from deploying technology that discriminates on the basis of any legally protected characteristic, "including algorithmic decision-making that relies on or uses discriminatory inputs and that produces discriminatory results."

The advisory explicitly integrates federal law: AI models used in credit and lending are subject to the [Equal Credit Opportunity Act (ECOA)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/15/1691), which requires covered creditors to provide specific and accurate adverse action notices even when the decision was made by an AI model. This is a compliance point that has proven consequential: the AG's $2.5 million settlement with [Earnest Operations LLC](https://www.mass.gov/news/ag-campbell-announces-25-million-settlement-with-student-loan-lender-for-unlawful-practices-through-ai-use-other-consumer-protection-violations) in July 2025 arose from AI underwriting that produced discriminatory credit outcomes based on race and immigration status.

[Koley Jessen's multistate survey](https://www.koleyjessen.com/insights/publications/state-attorneys-general-provide-guidance-on-artificial-intelligence-under-existing-data-privacy-laws) contextualizes Massachusetts' approach within a broader state AG trend of applying disparate impact theory to AI decision-making without waiting for new AI-specific legislation.

### Data Security: Chapter 93H and the Massachusetts Data Security Standards

Chapter 93H and its implementing regulations at [201 CMR 17.00](https://www.mass.gov/regulations/201-CMR-1700-standards-for-the-protection-of-personal-information-of-residents-of-the) require any entity that "owns or licenses" personal information of Massachusetts residents to maintain a written comprehensive information security program (WISP) and to implement reasonable safeguards. The advisory clarifies that:

- Personal data used to train, operate, or query an AI system is covered by these requirements.
- AI developers, suppliers, and users must take "necessary and appropriate steps" to safeguard personal information processed by AI systems.
- Breach notification requirements under Chapter 93H apply to AI-related data incidents.
- Violations of Chapter 93H are enforceable under Chapter 93A, enabling civil penalty exposure.

The [Verrill analysis](https://www.verrill-law.com/news/massachusetts-attorney-general-artificial-intelligence-guidance-what-healthcare-providers-and-health-it-developers-need-to-know/) emphasizes that healthcare providers and health IT developers face compounded obligations: Massachusetts data security rules apply alongside HIPAA, and AI systems processing protected health information trigger both frameworks simultaneously.

### Private Equity and Investor Implications

A notable dimension of the advisory, analyzed by [Troutman Pepper](https://www.troutman.com/insights/massachusetts-ag-advisory-opinion-has-implications-for-private-equity-firms-use-of-ai/), is its implicit reach to investors in AI companies. The advisory's broad "user" and "supplier" framing, combined with the AG's history of asserting claims against investors who exercise control over portfolio companies, creates exposure for private equity firms that:

- Fund consumer-facing businesses that deploy AI in violation of Chapter 93A or G.L. c. 151B.
- Fail to conduct AI-specific due diligence before investment.
- Do not contractually require portfolio companies to maintain AI governance and compliance programs.

The [Regulatory Oversight analysis](https://www.regulatoryoversight.com/2024/05/ags-focus-on-ai-has-implications-for-private-equity-investors/) confirms that the AG's focus on AI has direct implications for PE investors, recommending that due diligence processes now incorporate an analysis of a target company's AI use across internal operations and third-party vendors.

## Impact Assessment [MEDIUM confidence]

### Affected Entities and Industries

The advisory applies broadly across sectors. Industries with elevated risk profiles given the advisory's emphasis include:

- **Consumer technology:** Developers and deployers of AI chatbots, recommendation engines, and automated customer service tools.
- **Financial services and fintech:** AI-powered underwriting, credit scoring, and loan decisioning platforms subject to ECOA and Chapter 93A.
- **Healthcare and health IT:** AI diagnostic tools, clinical decision support, and health information platforms governed by both Massachusetts data security law and HIPAA.
- **Advertising and marketing:** Companies using AI-generated content, deepfakes, or AI-powered targeting subject to consumer protection scrutiny.
- **Employment:** Employers using AI for hiring, promotion, or performance evaluation that could produce disparate impact.
- **Real estate and housing:** AI-based rental screening or valuation tools at risk under G.L. c. 151B.
- **Private equity and venture capital:** Investors in companies that deploy AI face potential secondary exposure if portfolio companies violate the frameworks outlined in the advisory.

### Compliance Requirements and Timelines

The advisory did not establish new law or new compliance deadlines; it interpreted existing obligations. Entities already subject to Chapter 93A, G.L. c. 151B, and Chapter 93H have been legally obligated to comply with those statutes. The advisory creates urgency not through new requirements but through the AG's public declaration of enforcement intent and the signal that AI-related violations are actively monitored.

No grandfathering or transition period was announced for existing AI deployments.

### Enforcement Outlook

The advisory was followed by the [Earnest Operations LLC settlement in July 2025](https://www.mass.gov/news/ag-campbell-announces-25-million-settlement-with-student-loan-lender-for-unlawful-practices-through-ai-use-other-consumer-protection-violations) ($2.5 million), confirming active enforcement more than a year after the advisory was issued. The [Sidley Austin analysis of rising AI enforcement](https://datamatters.sidley.com/2024/12/10/rising-ai-enforcement-insights-from-state-attorney-general-settlement-and-u-s-ftc-sweep-for-risk-management-and-governance/) notes that Massachusetts was among the first state AGs to move from guidance to enforcement action in the AI space. The National Association of Attorneys General (NAAG) multistate coalition letters on AI reinforce that state-level enforcement is not diminishing in the face of federal inaction.

## Action Items

- **Audit AI use inventory:** Identify all AI systems used in consumer-facing, employment, housing, credit, and healthcare contexts and assess compliance with Chapter 93A, G.L. c. 151B, and Chapter 93H.
- **Update WISP:** Confirm that written comprehensive information security programs (required under 201 CMR 17.00) cover AI systems that process Massachusetts residents' personal data.
- **Review advertising and marketing AI:** Ensure AI-generated content, chatbot deployments, and AI-assisted advertising claims meet the truthfulness standards required under Chapter 93A.
- **Conduct bias testing:** For AI used in credit, employment, housing, or public benefits decisions, implement ongoing testing for discriminatory inputs or outputs that could violate G.L. c. 151B.
- **Adverse action notices:** Confirm that credit decisioning AI systems produce specific, accurate adverse action notices as required under ECOA, even when the decision was generated algorithmically.
- **Vendor contract review:** Update third-party AI vendor contracts to include AI compliance representations, disclosure requirements, and liability allocation provisions.
- **Private equity due diligence:** Incorporate AI governance review into M&A and investment due diligence processes for companies that develop, supply, or use AI systems.
- **Monitor enforcement:** Track AG enforcement actions for emerging theories; the Earnest settlement (July 2025) suggests that AI lending discrimination is a priority area.
