# Massachusetts AG AI Advisory: Implications for Private Equity Firms and Portfolio Company Liability

**Jurisdiction:** Massachusetts | **Category:** AI Law / Enforcement | **Date:** May 23, 2024

## Executive Summary [HIGH confidence]

The [Massachusetts AG's April 16, 2024 advisory on AI](https://www.mass.gov/doc/ago-ai-advisory-41624/download) — which applies existing state consumer protection, anti-discrimination, and data security laws to artificial intelligence — carries significant and underappreciated implications for private equity firms. While the advisory is addressed primarily to AI developers, suppliers, and users, PE firms with controlling interests in consumer-facing portfolio companies face derivative liability under legal theories the Massachusetts AG has already invoked against investors. A 2021 Massachusetts settlement in which private equity firm H.I.G. Capital paid $19.95 million (part of a $25 million total settlement) for a portfolio company's Medicaid billing fraud demonstrates that the AG's office is willing to pursue parent entities when they exercise sufficient control. As portfolio companies increasingly rely on AI in consumer-facing decisions — credit underwriting, employment, housing, healthcare — the advisory's framework extends these liability theories to AI-related violations. PE firms should treat the advisory as a signal to update investment due diligence and portfolio monitoring practices to address AI compliance risk. In July 2025, the AG confirmed this trajectory by settling a [$2.5 million action against a student loan lender](https://www.mass.gov/news/ag-campbell-announces-25-million-settlement-with-student-loan-lender-for-unlawful-practices-through-ai-use-other-consumer-protection-violations) whose AI underwriting model produced discriminatory results — the first such enforcement under the advisory.

## Background [HIGH confidence]

### The April 2024 AG Advisory

On April 16, 2024, Massachusetts Attorney General Andrea Joy Campbell issued an [Attorney General Advisory on the Application of the Commonwealth's Consumer Protection, Civil Rights, and Data Privacy Laws to Artificial Intelligence](https://www.mass.gov/doc/ago-ai-advisory-41624/download). The [AG's press release](https://www.mass.gov/news/ag-campbell-issues-advisory-providing-guidance-on-how-state-consumer-protection-and-other-laws-apply-to-artificial-intelligence) characterized it as the first such advisory issued by any state AG's office in the country. The advisory does not create new law but clarifies the AG's enforcement position: three existing Massachusetts legal frameworks apply to AI systems just as to any other technology.

- **[Chapter 93A (Massachusetts Consumer Protection Act)](https://malegislature.gov/Laws/GeneralLaws/PartI/TitleXV/Chapter93A):** Prohibits unfair or deceptive trade practices in Massachusetts. The "unfair" prong does not require deception and reaches conduct that causes substantial consumer harm inconsistent with public policy — a broad standard well suited to AI-related harms. The AG may seek injunctions, restitution, and civil penalties.
- **[G.L. c. 151B (Anti-Discrimination Law)](https://malegislature.gov/Laws/GeneralLaws/PartI/TitleXXI/Chapter151B):** Prohibits discrimination on protected characteristics in employment, housing, public accommodations, and credit. The advisory makes explicit that AI systems producing discriminatory outputs — even without intent — violate this statute.
- **[Chapter 93H and 201 CMR 17.00](https://www.mass.gov/regulations/201-CMR-17-standards-for-the-protection-of-personal-information-of-residents-of-the-commonwealth):** Massachusetts data security standards. Violations are enforceable as Chapter 93A violations, meaning data security failures by AI systems can be bundled into consumer protection claims.

### The H.I.G. Capital Precedent (2021)

The advisory's PE implications gain force from a settlement the Massachusetts AG's office secured three years before the advisory was issued. In October 2021, [private equity firm H.I.G. Capital agreed to pay $19.95 million](https://www.mass.gov/news/private-equity-firm-and-former-mental-health-center-executives-pay-25-million-over-alleged-false-claims-submitted-for-unlicensed-and-unsupervised-patient-care) (with former executives contributing the remaining $5.05 million of a $25 million total) to resolve allegations that H.I.G. caused South Bay Mental Health Center to submit fraudulent Medicaid billing claims. The AG's theory was that H.I.G., which held a majority of South Bay's board seats, had sufficient control over the portfolio company to be held liable for its unlawful conduct.

This was the [largest publicly disclosed government healthcare fraud settlement involving private equity oversight of a healthcare provider](https://www.hamiltonwingo.com/news-blog/2021/october/massachusetts-attorney-general-and-hamilton-wing/) in the nation at the time. A prior ruling from Judge Patti Saris in the U.S. District Court for the District of Massachusetts — the first summary judgment ruling of its kind against a private equity firm in a False Claims Act case — held that a PE firm could be liable for causing false claims submission if it exercised sufficient control.

While that case involved Medicaid fraud and the False Claims Act, the underlying liability theory — that an investor with board control can be held responsible for a portfolio company's legal violations — is readily transferable to consumer protection, civil rights, and data security claims under Chapter 93A and c. 151B. The Troutman Pepper analysis explicitly notes this connection: [regulators are increasingly focused on legal theories that allow them to bring claims not only against consumer-facing businesses but also the companies that exercise control of those businesses](https://www.troutman.com/insights/massachusetts-ag-advisory-opinion-has-implications-for-private-equity-firms-use-of-ai/).

## Detailed Analysis [MEDIUM confidence]

### How the Advisory Reaches PE Firms

The advisory is addressed to "developers, suppliers, and users" of AI. A PE firm that owns a controlling interest in a portfolio company may be considered a "user" of AI in relevant respects — particularly if it sets strategy, approves technology investments, or exercises operational oversight of AI-dependent business lines. Even firms that do not themselves use AI directly may face exposure as controlling entities for their portfolio companies' AI violations under two theories:

1. **Chapter 93A "control person" theory:** Massachusetts courts have held that parent entities or controlling shareholders can be liable under Chapter 93A if they directed, authorized, or ratified unfair or deceptive acts. A PE firm that approved or oversaw the adoption of an AI underwriting, hiring, or screening system could be swept into a 93A enforcement action alongside the portfolio company.

2. **Civil rights liability via disparate impact:** G.L. c. 151B, § 4 reaches entities that cause discriminatory outcomes, not merely those with discriminatory intent. A PE firm that pushed a portfolio company toward AI adoption without adequate bias testing faces potential civil rights liability if the resulting system produces discriminatory outputs in credit, employment, or housing decisions.

### Sector-Specific Exposure for PE

PE firms' portfolio companies are disproportionately concentrated in sectors where AI use in consumer-facing decisions is high and AG enforcement attention is intense:

- **Financial services / lending:** AI underwriting models used by portfolio-company lenders or servicers must comply with the [Equal Credit Opportunity Act (ECOA)](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1002/) and state anti-discrimination law. The advisory specifically warns that creditors using AI must still provide specific, accurate adverse action notices — a federal obligation under Regulation B that does not yield to AI opacity.
- **Healthcare:** AI-based clinical decision support, revenue cycle management, and prior authorization tools used by healthcare portfolio companies are subject to Chapter 93H data security standards and anti-discrimination requirements, particularly when clinical AI produces racially disparate treatment recommendations.
- **Employment/staffing:** AI-driven applicant screening, resume parsing, or performance management tools used by portfolio companies trigger c. 151B's employment discrimination prohibitions. The AG's advisory specifically calls out AI use in employment decisions.
- **Housing:** Portfolio companies in the real estate and property management sectors using AI for tenant screening, pricing, or maintenance prioritization face civil rights scrutiny under both c. 151B and federal fair housing law.

### Federal Law Layering

The advisory highlights layering of state and federal law, which PE firms must address in due diligence. Beyond Massachusetts-specific obligations, relevant federal laws include:

- **ECOA and Regulation B (15 U.S.C. § 1691 et seq.):** Require adverse action notices that explain credit denials, including when AI is used. Inability to explain AI-driven credit denials in specific terms is a federal violation independent of the Massachusetts advisory.
- **Fair Housing Act (42 U.S.C. § 3604):** Applies to AI-based tenant screening by residential real estate portfolio companies operating in Massachusetts.
- **FTC Act Section 5:** The FTC has separately signaled enforcement interest in AI-related deception and bias, adding a federal layer on top of the AG's state framework.

### The 2025 Earnest Operations Enforcement Action

The AG's July 2025 [$2.5 million settlement with Earnest Operations LLC](https://www.mass.gov/news/ag-campbell-announces-25-million-settlement-with-student-loan-lender-for-unlawful-practices-through-ai-use-other-consumer-protection-violations) confirmed that the 2024 advisory was an enforcement signal. The AG alleged that Earnest's AI underwriting model used a "Cohort Default Rate" variable that produced racially and ethnically disparate results for Black and Hispanic applicants. Claims were brought under Chapter 93A and ECOA. [Paul Hastings' analysis](https://www.paulhastings.com/insights/client-alerts/disparate-impact-lives-massachusetts-attacks-ai-in-lending) characterized the action as confirming the viability of disparate impact claims against AI systems under both state and federal law without proof of intent.

For PE firms, this enforcement action demonstrates the factual pattern that triggers liability: a consumer-facing portfolio company deploys an AI model in a regulated context (lending), the model produces disparate outcomes for protected groups, and the AG pursues relief under existing state and federal consumer protection and civil rights statutes.

## Impact Assessment [MEDIUM confidence]

### Who Is Affected

The Troutman Pepper analysis identified the following PE firm stakeholder categories as directly affected:

- **General partners and portfolio company boards:** GPs with board representation at portfolio companies face the clearest exposure. Board membership that includes directing or approving AI adoption could satisfy the "control" standard from the H.I.G. precedent.
- **Investment committees conducting AI-adjacent sector investments:** Any PE firm considering investments in consumer-facing sectors (lending, healthcare, insurance, staffing, housing) must treat AI compliance as a material diligence item.
- **Operating partners:** PE operating partners who advise portfolio companies on technology adoption strategies may be implicated if they recommended or facilitated AI deployment that caused consumer harm.

### Compliance Requirements and Timelines

There is no new statutory deadline created by the advisory — compliance with Chapter 93A, c. 151B, and Chapter 93H is immediately required and ongoing. The advisory's effect is to put the market on notice that existing obligations apply. PE firms should:

- Treat AI compliance as a live and immediate obligation across existing portfolio companies, not merely a future investment screen.
- Recognize that the Earnest settlement (July 2025) confirmed enforcement is active; the advisory's grace period — to the extent it existed — has expired.

### Enforcement Outlook

The Massachusetts AG has demonstrated both the willingness and the legal theories to pursue PE firms as control persons. The combination of (1) the H.I.G. precedent establishing PE investor liability for portfolio company conduct, (2) the April 2024 advisory putting all market participants on notice, and (3) the July 2025 Earnest enforcement action confirming that AI underwriting is a live enforcement priority creates a credible and immediate litigation risk for PE firms with portfolio companies in consumer-facing AI-intensive sectors.

[Regulatory Oversight's analysis](https://www.regulatoryoversight.com/2024/05/ags-focus-on-ai-has-implications-for-private-equity-investors/) noted that the AG's focus on AI has broad implications for PE investors and that investors should incorporate AI compliance analysis into their due diligence processes. The [Ropes & Gray 2024 analysis of AI regulation for private capital](https://www.ropesgray.com/en/insights/viewpoints/102ivhz/private-capital-impact-of-ai-and-its-regulation-for-investors-in-2024) similarly flagged increasing state-level AG activity as a key risk vector for PE portfolios in consumer-facing sectors.

## Action Items

- **Update deal due diligence checklists** to include an AI compliance section covering: what AI systems are in use by the target, whether they have been bias-tested, whether adverse action notice procedures exist for AI-driven credit or employment decisions, and whether data security practices under Chapter 93H are documented.
- **Audit existing portfolio company AI deployments** in consumer-facing roles (lending, hiring, housing, healthcare) and confirm that bias testing, model documentation, and adverse action notice procedures are in place.
- **Review board governance at portfolio companies** to assess whether board-level involvement in AI strategy could satisfy the "control" threshold from the H.I.G. precedent; engage outside counsel to evaluate exposure before a regulatory inquiry arrives.
- **Update operating partner engagement protocols** to require that technology adoption recommendations from operating partners include a regulatory compliance assessment for consumer-facing AI tools.
- **Implement vendor AI due diligence** for portfolio companies that rely on third-party AI systems — the advisory applies to users of AI, not just developers, meaning a portfolio company deploying a third-party AI tool is responsible for that tool's compliance.
- **Monitor AG enforcement actions** targeting AI in financial services, healthcare, and employment; the Earnest settlement pattern (AI model + disparate outcomes + Chapter 93A/ECOA) is the factual template for future PE exposure.
