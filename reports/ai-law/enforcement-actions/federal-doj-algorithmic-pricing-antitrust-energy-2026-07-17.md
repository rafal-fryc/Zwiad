# Collusion by Code: DOJ's Algorithmic Pricing Enforcement and the Energy Sector

**Jurisdiction:** Federal | **Category:** ai-law | **Date:** 2026-07-08

## Executive Summary [MEDIUM confidence] [VERIFIED]

The Department of Justice Antitrust Division has escalated its posture toward AI-driven and algorithmic pricing tools, most visibly through a May 14, 2026 speech by Acting Deputy Assistant Attorney General for Criminal Enforcement Daniel Glad, titled "Old Crime, New Code," delivered at the Antitrust West Coast Conference in San Francisco ([DOJ Office of Public Affairs](https://www.justice.gov/opa/speech/acting-deputy-assistant-attorney-general-criminal-enforcement-daniel-gladd-delivers)). Glad announced that the Division will pursue criminal prosecution — not just civil enforcement — against competitors who use shared pricing algorithms, SaaS platforms, or large language models ("LLMs") to coordinate prices, applying a "hub-and-spoke" conspiracy theory under the per se rule of the Sherman Act. Law firm analyses, including a July 2026 client alert from Mayer Brown, have since applied this framework specifically to the energy sector, where AI-driven forecasting, wholesale power pricing, fuel procurement, and capacity-bidding tools are common and where concentrated market structures and shared third-party data platforms create elevated collusion risk ([Mayer Brown](https://www.mayerbrown.com/en/insights/publications/2026/07/collusion-by-code-what-dojs-algorithmic-pricing-enforcement-means-for-the-energy-sector)). This finding originates from a Lexology digest headline and law-firm secondary analysis rather than a formal DOJ enforcement action, indictment, or consent decree; no criminal case involving an algorithmic pricing tool has yet been filed by the Division. Energy companies using shared or third-party pricing/bidding algorithms should treat this as an imminent enforcement priority requiring compliance review. Direct WebFetch access to the DOJ transcript was unavailable during research and review; the quotations and details attributed to the speech in this report are corroborated by independent, consistent secondary reporting from multiple law firms (DLA Piper, AO Shearman, Hogan Lovells) and DOJ/conference program listings, but a direct fetch of the primary DOJ transcript is recommended before this report is relied upon for the exact wording of the speech. <!-- unverifiable: source unavailable (DOJ transcript WebFetch blocked across all review rounds; corroborated via independent WebSearch) -->

## Background [MEDIUM confidence] [VERIFIED]

Algorithmic pricing has been a growing focus of both DOJ and FTC antitrust enforcement since at least 2024, driven by a wave of civil litigation against pricing-software vendors (most prominently RealPage in real estate and various revenue-management platforms in hospitality) alleging that competitors used a common algorithm to coordinate prices in violation of Section 1 of the Sherman Act. Those cases established the "hub-and-spoke" theory in the civil context: competitors (the "spokes") feed competitively sensitive, non-public data into a shared pricing tool (the "hub"), and the resulting coordinated outputs form the "rim" connecting the spokes into a horizontal conspiracy ([Hogan Lovells](https://www.jdsupra.com/legalnews/doj-antitrust-official-lays-out-7984391/)). <!-- verified -->

Until Glad's May 2026 remarks, the Division's algorithmic pricing enforcement had been confined to civil actions. The "Old Crime, New Code" speech marked the most detailed public statement yet by a senior DOJ Antitrust Division official on extending this theory into the criminal sphere, and did so against the backdrop of two enforcement tools cited in the speech: the Procurement Collusion Strike Force (PCSF), an interagency task force the Division established on November 5, 2019 and has expanded in scope and partnerships in the years since ([DOJ, Nov. 5, 2019 announcement](https://www.justice.gov/archives/opa/pr/justice-department-announces-procurement-collusion-strike-force-coordinated-national-response); [DOJ PCSF program page](https://www.justice.gov/atr/procurement-collusion-strike-force)), and the Antitrust Division's Whistleblower Rewards Program, a considerably newer tool launched in partnership with the U.S. Postal Service Office of Inspector General in July 2025 ([DOJ speech, as summarized by DLA Piper](https://www.dlapiper.com/en-us/insights/publications/2026/06/doj-antitrust-division-puts-focus-on-algorithmic-collusion); [Wiley](https://www.wiley.law/alert-DOJ-Antitrust-Whistleblower-Rewards-Program-First-1-Million-Award-Signals-an-Expanding-Enforcement-Tool)). <!-- verified: PCSF establishment date and WRP timeline independently confirmed via DOJ archives, PCSF program page, and Wiley 2019 alert --> On January 29, 2026, the Division paid its first-ever whistleblower reward — $1 million — for information that led to a deferred prosecution agreement and a $3.28 million fine against EBLOCK Corporation, an online used-vehicle auction platform, over a bid-rigging and shill-bidding scheme with a competing platform that ran from November 2020 to February 2022 ([Crowell & Moring](https://www.crowell.com/en/insights/client-alerts/doj-antitrust-division-issues-first-ever-award-under-whistleblower-rewards-program); [Concurrences](https://www.concurrences.com/en/bulletin/news-issues/january-2026/the-us-doj-awards-its-first-whistleblower-payment-for-exposing-a-bid-rigging)). <!-- verified --> While that case did not involve an algorithmic pricing tool, DOJ officials have cited the reward program's early success as evidence the Division's investigative architecture is prepared to surface algorithmic collusion cases going forward.

## Detailed Analysis [MEDIUM confidence] [VERIFIED]

In the "Old Crime, New Code" speech, Glad articulated the Division's core legal position: "you cannot do with the knowing use of an algorithm what you could not do with a simple agreement that does the same thing" — i.e., software cannot "launder" what would otherwise be per se illegal price-fixing under Section 1 of the Sherman Act ([DOJ speech](https://www.justice.gov/opa/speech/acting-deputy-assistant-attorney-general-criminal-enforcement-daniel-gladd-delivers); reported by [AO Shearman](https://www.aoshearman.com/en/insights/ao-shearman-on-investigations/us-doj-signals-criminal-risk-for-ai-powered-pricing-tools)). <!-- verified: quote independently corroborated via DLA Piper, Axinn, and Daily Journal coverage of the speech --> According to law firm summaries of the speech, Glad stated that "the door to the per se rule, and therefore to criminal enforcement, is open" when competitors have used the same pricing tool and "have understood that their sensitive non-public data will be used to set prices for competitors and have participated on that understanding" (as quoted in [Hogan Lovells](https://www.jdsupra.com/legalnews/doj-antitrust-official-lays-out-7984391/)). The speech reportedly covered three areas: (1) how current civil enforcement demarcates the line into criminal liability; (2) the Division's existing investigative tools — the Procurement Collusion Strike Force and Whistleblower Rewards Program; and (3) open questions the Division intends to keep pressing, including what it means for antitrust purposes when the "hub" of a pricing arrangement is an LLM rather than conventional software ([DLA Piper](https://www.dlapiper.com/en-us/insights/publications/2026/06/doj-antitrust-division-puts-focus-on-algorithmic-collusion)).

Mayer Brown's July 2026 client alert, the source article underlying this finding, applies this hub-and-spoke/per se framework specifically to energy markets. It argues that energy presents "a particularly acute set of risks" because of concentrated market structures, repeated interactions among the same competitors in organized wholesale markets (e.g., RTOs/ISOs), shared third-party data and analytics platforms that aggregate generation cost data, fuel price indices, and capacity availability across market participants, and a regulatory history of antitrust scrutiny in energy trading dating back to the Enron-era manipulation cases ([Mayer Brown](https://www.mayerbrown.com/en/insights/publications/2026/07/collusion-by-code-what-dojs-algorithmic-pricing-enforcement-means-for-the-energy-sector); [Mondaq syndication of the same alert](https://www.mondaq.com/unitedstates/antitrust-eu-competition/1813374/collusion-by-code-what-dojs-algorithmic-pricing-enforcement-means-for-the-energy-sector)). The alert states that the same antitrust rules apply "regardless of whether prices are set by a human trader or an AI-driven optimization engine," and that DOJ has signaled it is not opposed to algorithmic tools generally, but will scrutinize tools that pool non-public competitor data and produce coordinated outputs.

It is important to note the evidentiary basis for this report: no criminal indictment, civil complaint, consent decree, or formal enforcement action targeting an energy-sector algorithmic pricing tool has been identified in available reporting. The finding is grounded in a policy speech (a primary DOJ source) and secondary law-firm commentary applying that speech's framework prospectively to the energy industry. The Division has stated its criminal enforcement, as of this speech, remains limited to civil cases historically, with the Division "yet to bring a criminal antitrust case involving an algorithmic pricing tool" as of mid-2026 ([summary corroborated across multiple secondary sources](https://www.swlaw.com/publication/algorithmic-pricing-under-the-antitrust-microscope-doj-and-ftc-sharpen-their-enforcement-posture/)). <!-- verified: no contrary indictment, complaint, or consent decree found as of 2026-07-18 -->

## Impact Assessment [MEDIUM confidence] [VERIFIED]

Energy companies — including regulated utilities, independent power producers, wholesale power marketers, and fuel/capacity traders — that use third-party or shared algorithmic pricing, bidding, or forecasting tools face elevated antitrust risk under this framework, particularly where those tools ingest non-public competitor data (e.g., generation costs, fuel indices, capacity availability) shared across multiple market participants through a common platform. The risk applies whether the algorithm is a conventional rules-based pricing engine, a SaaS revenue-management platform, or an LLM-based tool, according to DOJ's stated position ([Mayer Brown](https://www.mayerbrown.com/en/insights/publications/2026/07/collusion-by-code-what-dojs-algorithmic-pricing-enforcement-means-for-the-energy-sector)).

Because the underlying legal theory (hub-and-spoke conspiracy under Sherman Act Section 1's per se rule) can support criminal charges — including individual criminal liability for executives and traders — companies should treat this as materially different from prior civil-only exposure. The Division's Whistleblower Rewards Program, which pays 15-30% of recoveries above $1 million to individuals reporting original information about antitrust violations, increases the likelihood that internal use of shared pricing tools could be reported by employees or counterparties ([Wiley](https://www.wiley.law/alert-DOJ-Antitrust-Whistleblower-Rewards-Program-First-1-Million-Award-Signals-an-Expanding-Enforcement-Tool)). <!-- verified: range consistent with cited source and secondary summaries; primary DOJ program-rules confirmation recommended for compliance-grade precision --> No specific compliance deadline or rulemaking accompanies this enforcement posture shift — it is a policy signal via speech, not a rule or statute — so the impact is primarily one of elevated investigative and prosecutorial risk rather than a new affirmative compliance obligation.

## Action Items

- Energy companies should audit all third-party or shared pricing, bidding, and forecasting algorithms (including any LLM-based tools) to identify whether they ingest non-public competitor data alongside the company's own data.
- Legal and compliance teams should review vendor contracts for pricing/bidding software to assess whether competitor data pooling occurs and whether output coordination could be characterized as a "hub-and-spoke" arrangement.
- Monitor DOJ Antitrust Division public statements, speeches, and any forthcoming indictments or civil complaints for the first case squarely applying criminal theory to an algorithmic pricing tool, which would substantially clarify the Division's enforcement threshold.
- Given the Division's active Whistleblower Rewards Program, ensure internal reporting channels and antitrust compliance training address algorithmic pricing risks specifically.
- Track any parallel FTC activity, as law firm commentary indicates both DOJ and FTC are "sharpening" enforcement posture on algorithmic pricing in 2026.
